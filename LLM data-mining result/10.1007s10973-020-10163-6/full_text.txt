Cu‑based oxygen carriers for chemical looping with oxygen 
uncoupling

Abstract
Chemical looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) is a promising technology to be used in combination with the oxy-fuel 
combustion/gasification processes for carbon capture and sequestration in that it reduces the energy penalty for ­O2 genera-
tion; hence, much attention has been focused on the production of proper oxygen carriers. In this work, four oxygen carrier 
candidates for CLOU made of 90% CuO and 10% ­ZrO2 or ­Y2O3 are first prepared with the sol–gel and graphite impregna-
tion methods. The reduction reactivity of the prepared oxygen carriers is investigated at 900, 950, and 1000 °C in the ­CO2 
atmosphere, and the redox stability is evaluated through ten sequential cycles using the thermogravimetric analyzer. It is 
found that the Avrami–Erofeev random nucleation and subsequence growth model (A2) describes the reduction process 
very well. CuO/ZrO2 prepared by the graphite impregnation performs better reactivity and stability than the others. The 
agglomeration and sintering of the oxygen carriers at high temperatures are found to be the main cause of the low reactivity 
over the repeated cycles.
Keywords  Cu-based oxygen carriers · Chemical looping with oxygen uncoupling · Kinetics · Thermogravimetric analysis ·

Introduction
Excessive emission of carbon dioxide ­(CO2) has led to 
global climate change and ecological disruption. Reduction 
in the greenhouse gas emission by the carbon capture and 
storage (CCS) technology offers one promising solution to 
this anthropogenic problem. Among the proposed CCS sce-
narios, the chemical looping combustion (CLC) is believed 
to be more feasible and efficient compared with the others 
[1–3]. This is because CLC utilizes the lattice rather than 
the gaseous ­O2 as the oxidation agent so as to greatly lower 
the energy penalty caused by a traditional air separation unit 
[4–7]. To date, the CLC technology has been demonstrated 
in several power generation units of different capacities from 
0.3 kW to 3 MW [8–10].
In CLC, the solid fuel needs to be firstly converted to 
syngas which subsequently reacts with the oxygen carriers 
[11]. The reactions between syngas and the oxygen carri-
ers are usually very fast at high temperatures, and hence, 
the slow gasification process determines the final solid fuel 
conversion [12]. In comparison, the chemical looping with 
oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) technology proposed by Mattis-
son et al. [13] can avoid the slow gasification process. With 
the CLOU technology, the solid fuel burns directly with oxy-
gen released from the oxygen carriers, which performs faster 
solid fuel conversion [14, 15]. The CLOU process includes 
three steps in two reactors, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In the fuel 
reactor, the oxygen carrier, ­MexOy, releases oxygen and is 
reduced to ­MexOy−2, as indicated by reaction (1). Then, the 
released oxygen reacts with the solid fuel as indicated by 
reaction (2). The reduced oxygen carrier, ­MexOy−2 is then

Producing proper oxygen carriers for the CLOU process 
is challenging. The criteria for excellent oxygen carrier 
production include high reactivity, high re-oxidation rate, 
good mechanical durability and stability, low cost, and envi-
ronmental friendliness [16, 17]. Among the oxygen carrier 
candidates, CuO/Cu2O, ­Mn2O3/Mn3O4, and ­Co3O4/CoO 
are found prospective for CLOU through thermodynamic 
assessment [13]. Among the three promising oxygen car-
riers for the CLOU process, the Cu-based oxygen carriers 
have been extensively studied due to the high reactivity, high 
oxygen transport capacity, and relatively low cost [18–20]. 
However, practical utilization of the Cu-based oxygen car-
riers still face some issues such as low melting point, low 
mechanical strength and serious agglomeration [21, 22].
To overcome these drawbacks, efforts have been put on 
seeking proper support materials and the corresponding 
preparation methods for oxygen carriers. It is found from 
the literature that the Cu-based oxygen carriers prepared 
by the impregnation method with ­SiO2, ­TiO2 or γ-Al2O3 as 
the support material [23–25], the spray drying method with

known, the preparation methods, like sol–gel, co-precipita-
tion, impregnation, freeze granulation, mechanical mixing, 
and spray drying, have great impact on the performance of 
the oxygen carriers. The sol–gel method has an advantage 
over the co-precipitation and mechanical mixing ones in that 
it allows for the homogeneous mixing of components almost 
at the molecular level and the easy control of pore structure 
[29–31]. The oxygen carriers prepared by impregnation are 
found to have lower attrition rate and higher mechanical 
strength, maintaining a long lifetime [32, 33]. In addition, 
the impregnation method is highly economical compared 
with the others.
Till now, only the effect of different inert support materi-
als on the reactivity and stability of the copper-based oxygen 
carriers is investigated by researchers. The synthetic effect 
of the carriers and the preparation methods are, however, 
rarely reported. In this work, the synthetic effect of two 
different inert support materials including ­ZrO2 and ­Y2O3 
and two different preparation methods including the sol–gel 
and graphite impregnation methods on the reactivity and 
stability of the Cu-based oxygen carriers are investigated 
and comprehensively analyzed. The reactivity and kinet-
ics of all oxygen carriers were evaluated during reduction 
process through the thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) at 
900–1000 °C. The stability of oxygen carriers was tested by 
10 sequential redox cycles. Moreover, insights are also taken 
into the physical characteristics of fresh and tested oxygen 
carriers, such as morphology features, specific surface area, 
and crushing strength. These physical characteristics were 
analyzed by the scanning electron microscope (SEM) and 
the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) for the particles after 
consecutive redox cycles.

As far as the sol–gel method is concerned, two Cu-based 
oxygen carriers were prepared by this method at the ambi-
ent temperature using ­ZrO2 and ­Y2O3 as supports, respec-
tively. In this producing process, copper nitrate and zirco-
nium nitrate (or yttrium nitrate) were added to water that 
had been purified by reverse osmosis. Then, dilute citric 
acid was added slowly to the blended aqueous solution and 
stirred to form the wet gel at 100 °C by the constant tem-
perature magnetic stirrer. The wet gel was dried for 6 h at 
120 °C and then calcined in an electric muffle furnace for 
5 h at 500 °C. The produced sample was sintered for 6 h at 
800 °C to increase the mechanical strength of the particles. 
Finally, CuO/ZrO2 and CuO/Y2O3 sized 300–500 μm were 
sieved for subsequent experimental studies.
As far as the graphite impregnation is concerned, the 
support material was obtained by using graphite (15 mass% 
of support material) as the pore-forming agent and using 
the metal nitrate as the metal oxide precursor. The metal 
nitrate was first dissolved in the deionized water, and then 
the graphite was added to the metal nitrate solution. The 
resulted wet samples were dried for 6 h at 120 °C and then 
calcined for 3 h at 1000 °C. Thereafter, the stoichiomet-
ric amount of copper nitrate was dissolved in the deionized 
water, and then the obtained support material was added to 
the copper nitrate solution. The wet samples were stirred 
by a thermostatic electromagnetic stirrer at 80 °C to form 
paste. The resultant paste was dried for 6 h at 110 °C and 
calcined in a muffle furnace for 5 h at 500 °C. The samples 
obtained were further sintered for 6 h at 800 °C to improve 
the mechanical strength. Finally, the oxygen carrier samples 
in the size range of 300–500 μm were sieved for utilization.
Thermogravimetric analysis
The reduction reactivity and stability over repeated cycles 
were carried out for all the prepared oxygen carriers in a 
TGA/SDTA851e from Mettler-Toledo, Swiss. During the 
experiment, the crucible was kept as shallow as 2 mm in 
depth to mitigate the effect of the gas diffusion over the 
wall of the container [34]. The reductively experiment was 
conducted at 900–1000 °C with 15 mg oxygen carrier. The 
­CO2 flow rate was controlled to be 100 mL min−1 for all 
experiments. The repeated reduction–oxidation cycles of 
the oxygen carriers were carried out by switching the inlet 
gas between the reducing atmosphere and air. During the 
reduction process, ­CO2 was used as the reducing atmosphere 
instead of ­N2. The reactivity of the oxygen carriers does not 
change under different inert reducing atmospheres, which 
is verified by experiments presented in Fig. S1 of supple-
mentary material. The reduction time was kept for 5 min 
at 1000 °C and the oxidation time was kept for 10 min at 
800 °C. The sample was heated or cooled to the expected 
temperature at the rate of 20 °C min−1.
Characterization methods
The surface morphology of the oxygen carriers before and 
after the experiments was detected via a Japan JSM-6301F 
SEM to observe the clustering, scattering, and agglomera-
tion phenomena. Furthermore, the specific surface area of 
the samples was calculated from the ­N2 adsorption method 
(JW-BK112 from China) using the BET model, and the 
crushing strength for oxygen carries was determined by a 
digital-display dynamometer (FGJ-5).

Results and discussion
The inert carrier does not react with the active material dur-
ing the preparation of the oxygen carrier, and the addition of 
graphite has no effect on the phase composition of the oxy-
gen carrier, as shown in Fig. S2 of supplementary material. 
The sample conversions during the reduction for CuO/ZrO2 
and CuO/Y2O3 at temperatures of 900, 950, and 1000 °C 
are calculated and depicted in Fig. 2. The reduction rate 
increases significantly with the increment of the reaction 
temperature. Interestingly, no matter the SG or GI method 
is chosen to prepare the oxygen carrier, the reduction rates 
of CuO/ZrO2 are always slightly higher than those of CuO/
Y2O3 at 900, 950, and 1000 °C. For the oxygen carriers 
prepared by SG (Fig. 2a), the reduction rates at α = 50% are 
measured at around 0.091, 0.385, and 0.833 min−1 for CuO/
ZrO2, while they are 0.083, 0.357, and 0.714 min−1 for CuO/
Y2O3. For the oxygen carriers prepared by GI (Fig. 2b), the 
reduction rates at α = 50% are 0.093, 0.417, and 0.980 min−1 
for CuO/ZrO2 while they are 0.082, 0.362, and 0.847 min−1 
for CuO/Y2O3. The results indicate that CuO with ­ZrO2 as 
support exhibits better reactivity than that with ­Y2O3. More-
over, it can be found that the reduction rate of the GI-pre-
pared oxygen carrier is higher than that of the SG-prepared.

From Table 3, the A2, R2, and P1 mechanisms are found 
to be the most suitable reaction mechanisms according to 
the values of R2. The three models are evaluated further in 
detail because their R2 values are very close. To determine 
the most probable mechanism, the average (AVE) and the 
standard deviation (SD) values of the R2 are obtained and 
are listed in Table 4 for different oxygen carriers. Because 
the A2 mechanism is found having the maximum AVE and 
the minimum SD among the models, the A2 model is finally 
selected to predict the reduction kinetics. The same method 
for determining the most probable reaction mechanism has 
been reported in some other literatures [39, 40].
The fitting history of the A2 model against the corre-
sponding experimental data for the experiments performed 
at 900, 950, and 1000 °C are shown in Fig. 3 for the CuO/
ZrO2(SG), CuO/Y2O3(SG), CuO/ZrO2(GI), and CuO/
Y2O3(GI) oxygen carriers. The Arrhenius plots for all oxy-
gen carriers are drawn and shown in Fig. 4. No obvious 
change in the line trend is observed in the concerned tem-
perature range. Therefore, the activation energies and pre-
exponential factors are calculated based on the slopes and 
intercepts of the Arrhenius plots, and the calculated kinetic 
parameters are listed in Table 5.
Interestingly, the reported activation energy of pure CuO 
sized around 10 μm is 322 kJ mol−1 at temperatures from 
760 to 910 °C [41], while the activation energies of four 
oxygen carriers are found a bit lower at 900–1000 °C in the 
present work. This is because the reaction is mainly diffu-
sion controlled at lower temperature. When the temperature 
rises from 900 to 1000 °C, the equilibrium oxygen partial 
pressure increases from 1.51 to 12.39%. Compared to high 
temperatures, the equilibrium partial pressure of oxygen 
is easily achieved at low temperatures, and the reduction 
rate is dramatically reduced once the equilibrium state is 
reached. On the other hand, agglomeration and sintering can 
occur at high temperatures, leading to the increase in oxy-
gen diffusion resistance and further influence the activation 
energy. Thus, the comprehensive effects of equilibrium ­O2 
partial pressure and structural change should be responsi-
ble for the reduction in activation energy at temperatures 
over 900 °C. In the present work, the activation energies 
for the decomposition of CuO/ZrO2 prepared by SG and GI 
are 281.29 kJ mol−1 and 286.74 kJ mol−1 at temperatures 
ranging from 900 to 1000 °C, respectively, very close to the 
reported activation energy of 280 kJ mol−1 obtained by Sahir 
et al. [42] for the decomposition of a freeze-granulated CuO/
ZrO2 oxygen carrier in the temperature range 900–985 °C.

Conclusions
In this work, four Cu-based oxygen carriers supported 
by ­ZrO2 or ­Y2O3 are prepared by the sol–gel and graph-
ite impregnation methods, respectively. The most prob-
able reaction mechanisms are identified for the reduction 
in CuO/ZrO2(SG), CuO/Y2O3(SG), CuO/ZrO2(GI), and 
CuO/Y2O3(GI) oxygen carriers, and the Avrami–Erofeev 
random nucleation and subsequence growth A2 is found 
to be the most suitable one for the reduction process. For 
CuO/ZrO2(SG), CuO/Y2O3(SG), CuO/ZrO2(GI), and 
CuO/Y2O3(GI) oxygen carriers, the activation energies 
are determined as 281.29 kJ mol−1, 258.29 kJ mol−1, 
286.74 kJ mol−1, and 279.17 kJ mol−1, respectively, and 
the pre-exponential factors are found to be 9.00 × 109 s−1, 
8.33 × 108 s−1, 1.65 × 1010 s−1, and 6.82 × 109 s−1, respec-
tively. Also, the reactivity and stability of oxygen carrier 
are evaluated through 10 sequential redox cycles using 
TGA. Among all the studied carriers, GI-prepared CuO/
ZrO2 shows excellent reactivity and stability. The insta-
bility of the cycle is caused by the agglomeration and 
sintering of oxygen carrier particles.
