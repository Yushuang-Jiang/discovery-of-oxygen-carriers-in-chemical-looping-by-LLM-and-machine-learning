The adaptability of Cu/Zr oxides as oxygen carrier used
for chemical looping air separation (CLAS)
Kun Wang • Qingbo Yu • Wenjun Duan •
Qin Qin • Huaqing Xie
Received: 3 July 2013 / Accepted: 24 September 2013 / Published online: 22 October 2013
 Akade´miai Kiado´, Budapest, Hungary 2013
Abstract
Chemical looping air separation (CLAS), based
on the chemical looping principle, is a novel and energy-
efﬁcient method to separate oxygen from air. The oxygen
carriers used capture oxygen from air in an oxidation
reactor and release oxygen in a reduction reactor. In this
work, the adaptability of Cu/Zr oxygen carrier used for
CLAS was investigated through thermodynamic analysis
and experimental methods. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and
scanning electron microscope (SEM) were used to measure
the phases and surface morphology of oxygen carriers
before and after experiments. The results show that CuO
has the capability of releasing oxygen when the tempera-
ture is higher than 725 C in the nitrogen atmosphere, and
the minimum oxygen reduction temperatures increase with
the increasing of oxygen concentrations. The Cu/Zr oxygen
carrier has high oxygen reduction and oxidation rates when
temperature is higher than a certain values. For reduction,
the value is about 860 C. For oxidation, the value is about
500 C. The reactivity of oxygen carrier increases signiﬁ-
cantly with the temperature increasing. On overall, reac-
tivity of oxygen carrier has little difference under different
particle sizes. The oxygen carrier exhibits a stable oxygen
reduction and oxidation behavior during reduction–oxida-
tion cycles. XRD patterns show that the main phases in
reduced samples are Cu2O and ZrO2. The main phases in
fresh and oxidized samples are CuO and ZrO2. SEM
images show that the fresh and reacted oxygen carriers are
porous. The surface of reacted samples is smoother than
fresh samples and no agglomeration has been found.

Introduction
Chemical looping air separation (CLAS) is a new air
separation technology to produce oxygen. The features of
CLAS are operation-simple, cost-effective and energy-
extensive. The average speciﬁc power of the CLAS
process is about 0.08 kWh m-3 O2 and it is just 26 % of
the speciﬁc power of an advanced cryogenic air sepa-
ration system [1]. CLAS relies on a chemical looping
principle similar to the one used in the chemical looping
combustion (CLC) which has been introduced as an
effective technology to capture CO2 during combustion
[2]. CLC has now proven to be one of the most efﬁcient
methods for CO2 capture during combustion of gaseous
fuels, but it has some shortcomings for combustion of
solid fuels, especially the low reaction rate [3–8]. To
combust the solid fuels effectively using the chemical
looping concept, chemical looping with oxygen uncou-
pling (CLOU) was proposed by Mattisson et al. [9] in
2009. In contrast to CLC, the CLOU requires three steps:
oxygen releases from the oxygen carrier, solid fuel is
oxidized by the oxygen released in the fuel reactor and
oxygen carrier is regenerated in the air reactor [9–11].
CLAS was developed based on CLOU. Figure 1 illus-
trates the schematic view of CLAS system. CLAS sys-
tem consists of two interconnected reactors, designated
as oxidation and reduction reactors, which are similar to
air and fuel reactors used in CLC or CLOU.

oxygen from the air (step 1):

Steam can be selected as the ﬂuidization medium

separated easily. CO2 can also be used as the ﬂuidization
medium and O2–CO2 mixed gas, which can be used for
oxy-fuel combustion is produced.
Selecting suitable oxygen carrier is the key issue to
CLAS process. The properties of oxygen carriers for CLAS
must include: (1) with the capability of oxygen reduction at
suitable temperatures and oxygen partial pressures, (2)
sufﬁcient oxygen transport capacity, (3) high reactivity and
availability, (4) avoidance of agglomeration, (5) high
mechanical strength and chemical stability, (6) low cost of
the metal, (7) low environmental impact [12]. Many
researches have been carried out to obtain suitable oxygen
carriers for CLC or CLOU. The results indicated that Ni-,
Cu-, Co-, Fe-, Mn-based and CaSO4 could be the optional
oxygen carriers for CLC system [7, 13–17]. Oxides of
CuO, Co3O4 and Mn2O3 have the capability of releasing
oxygen at suitable temperatures and oxygen partial pres-
sures [9, 18, 19].
Comparing to the three oxides, copper has the highest
oxygen transport capacity (0.1 g O2–1 g CuO compared to
0.03 g O2–1 g Mn2O3 and 0.06 g O2–1 g Co3O4). However,
agglomeration is the biggest issue because of its low
melting point [20]. Adding binder to copper oxide and
preparing them to oxygen carrier can improve the capability
of anti-sintering. Oxygen carrier prepared is porous, which
can provide higher surface area and increase the reactivity
of oxygen carrier. Research on copper oxide combined with
different binders as oxygen carrier used in CLC or CLOU
process has been conducted in ﬂuidized or ﬁxed bed batch
reactor and thermogravimetric (TG) analyzer [12, 13, 19].
The oxides of ZrO2, Y2O3, Al2O3, SiO2, TiO2 and MgAl2O4
have been investigated as binders used in CLC or CLOU.
De Diego and colleagues [21] found that the best Cu-based
oxygen carriers used for CLC were those prepared by
mechanical mixing using SiO2 or TiO2 as inert, and sintered
at 950 C. Gayan et al. [22] found that particles with 60
mass% CuO supported on MgAl2O4 and 40 mass% CuO
supported on ZrO2 prepared by mechanical mixing fol-
lowing by pelletizing by pressure were identiﬁed as suitable
materials for CLOU process. Mattisson et al. [23] studied
the CLOU process using oxygen carrier particles of CuO/
ZrO2 in a small quartz laboratory ﬂuidized bed reactor and
the oxygen carrier showed high reactivity. The oxide of
Al2O3 was found not suitable for CLOU because they lost
the oxygen release property when CuAl2O4 is formed [24].
The high price of Y2O3 is the limitation when used as binder
for oxygen carrier. At present, research on CLAS is still
scarce in literature. Only thermodynamic calculations and
preliminary experiments were carried out on oxides of Cu
(CuO/Cu2O) by Moghtaderi [1] to ascertain their feasibility
for the proposed air separation method.
In this work, oxygen carrier was prepared by mechanical
mixing containing 60 mass% CuO and ZrO2 as support and
the adaptabilities of oxygen carrier have been investigated
in a TG. To investigate the capabilities of oxygen reduction
and oxidation of Cu-based oxygen carrier, temperature
programmed TG was used in this research. Isothermal TG
was used to investigate the reactivity of oxygen carrier at
different temperatures and during several reduction–oxi-
dation cycles. Phases and surface morphology of oxygen
carriers before and after experiments were measured by
X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscope
(SEM).
Thermodynamics

the most important property needed for oxygen carrier used
in CLAS. To measure it, the Gibbs free energy change
(DG) of the reduction reaction must be calculated. DG is a
measure of the thermodynamic driving force that motivated
the reaction process [19, 25]. A negative value of DG
indicates that a reaction can proceed spontaneously without
external energy inputs. Moreover, the more negative the
value of DG, the more likely that the reaction towards
positive will proceed. DG is a state function and can be
calculated through the equation below:

725 C, pure CuO can release oxygen in steam/CO2

production, the high temperature in the reduction reactor
means a small steam/CO2 demand.
Experimental section
Oxygen carrier material
The Cu-based oxygen carrier was prepared by mechanical
mixing. Commercial CuO powder as active phase and ZrO2
powder as binder with mass ratio of 60/40 were mixed
thoroughly in an agate mortar. Graphite (10 mass%) as a
pore-forming additive was added during the mixing process
to create a suitable macroporosity after sintering. Then dis-
tilled water was added to the mixed powders to form a paste
of suitable viscosity to be extruded in a syringe, obtaining
cylinders of about 2 mm diameter. These cylinders were
gently dried at 105 C for 12 h in a drier and sintered at
950 C for 6 h in a mufﬂe furnace. The cylinders were
ground and sieved to obtain the desired particle sizes
[11, 26]. The characteristics of the oxygen carrier can be
found in Table 1. The BET investigation was carried out
using Micromeritics, ASAP 2020 to obtain surface area of
the oxygen carrier. The oxygen carriers were analyzed before

Experimental set-up
Experiments were performed in a TG using STA409PC
thermal analyzer. Nitrogen was used to substitute for steam
in the reduction reactor. Air was used as the oxidizing gas
in the oxidation reactor. The ﬂow rates of air and nitrogen
were 30 mL min-1. To investigate the capabilities of
oxygen reduction and oxidation of oxygen carrier, the
temperature programmed TG was used. Isothermal TG was
used to investigate the reactivity of oxygen carrier at dif-
ferent temperatures and particle sizes.
In temperature programmed TG experiments, samples
were heated from room temperature to 700 C in air
atmosphere. Then air was turned into nitrogen and
samples were heated to 1,000 C with heating rates of
10, 15 and 20 C min-1 to study the reduction reaction.
After reduction, the samples were cooled to 200 C in
nitrogen atmosphere and then atmosphere was turned
into air and samples were heated to 800 C at the same
heating rates as reduction process to study the oxidation
reaction.

Effect of particle size
Figure 4 shows the conversion ratio of reduction and
oxidation reactions under different particle sizes. The
reduction and oxidation reaction temperature is 1,000 and
600 C, respectively. From Fig. 4 it can be noted that the
reactivity of oxygen carrier with different particle size has
little difference. The reasons are that heat transfer is more
effective among the small particles and the inner portions
of particles could reach high temperature more easily.
However, the mass transfer among the particles is limited
when sample particle size is very small [29]. As a con-
clusion, the Cu/Zr oxygen carrier as well as the CLAS
process has a great applicability on different particle
sizes.

The biggest difﬁculty of copper oxide to be used in
CLAS is the low melting point. Fortunately, temperature
used in CLAS is lower than that used in CLC which is
beneﬁcial to avoidance of agglomeration. Moreover, there
is no fuel that can be used in the reduction reactor and can
prevent carbon stripping usually generated in CLC and
CLOU. Some images of the surface morphology of fresh
and reacted samples are shown in Fig. 8. Both the fresh and
reacted samples are porous. The surface of reacted samples
is smoother than fresh samples and no agglomeration has
been found. The additions of ZrO2 can effectively inhibit
the agglomeration of Cu-based oxygen carriers.
Conclusions
Selecting the suitable oxygen carrier is the key issue to
CLAS. We investigated the feasibility and adaptability of
using Cu/Zr oxygen carrier for CLAS in different tem-
perature ranges through thermodynamic analysis and
experimental method. Conclusions are obtained below:
(1)
The reaction system of CuO/Cu2O has the capability
of releasing oxygen at suitable atmosphere and
temperature. The minimum oxygen reduction tem-
peratures increase with the increasing of oxygen
concentrations.
(2)
When temperature is higher than 860 C, the reduction
reaction of Cu/Zr oxygen carrier has high reaction rate.
When temperature is higher than 500 C, the oxidation
reaction of Cu/Zr oxygen carrier has high reaction rate.
Ti, Tf and Tmax all shift to high temperature with the
increasing of heating rate.
(3)
Reaction temperature is the most important factor on
the oxygen reduction and oxidation reactivity. The
reduction and oxidation reaction rates increase signif-
icantly with the temperature increasing. The reduction
and oxidation reaction rates under different particle
sizes have little difference. The Cu/Zr oxygen carrier
has a great applicability on different particle sizes.
(4)
The oxygen carrier exhibits a stable oxygen reduction
and oxidation behavior during reduction–oxidation
cycles. XRD patterns show that the phases are only
CuO and ZrO2 in fresh and oxidized samples. Cu2O
and ZrO2 are found in reduced samples. SEM images
show that the fresh and reacted oxygen carriers are
porous and no agglomeration has been found during
the cycles.
