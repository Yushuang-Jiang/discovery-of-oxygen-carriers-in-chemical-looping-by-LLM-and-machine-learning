A B S T R A C T
Bimetallic Fe-Cu oxygen carriers (OCs) with SiO2 as a support are attractive materials for coal chemical-looping
combustion (CLC) based on their availability from naturally occurring, low cost materials. The objectives of this
study are to investigate reduction reactivities and CO2 conversion efficiency of synthesized Fe-Cu-Si OCs for both
in-situ gasification CLC (iG-CLC) and Chemical-Looping with Oxygen Uncoupling (CLOU) at high temperatures
(950 to 1100 °C) with varying ratios of OC to coal char (ϕ) using a fixed bed reactor-quadrupole mass spec-
trometer system. Two Cu-Fe-Si OCs with high iron content (40% Fe2O3 + 20%CuO by wt.) (Fe40Cu20) and high
copper content (Fe20Cu40) were prepared using a pressure pelletizing method. Oxygen uncoupling efficiency
and rate for the Fe40Cu20 OC were lower than the Fe20Cu40 OC due to their CuFe2O4 and CuO composition
difference from XRD analysis. For Fe40Cu20 OC with ϕ = 80 in iG-CLC, the carbon conversion rate increased
37% from 950 °C to 1000 °C but only increased 5.4% from 1000 °C to 1100 °C. As the ratios ϕ dropped (80, 40
and 24), the CO2 conversion efficiencies decreased and the content of metallic Cu in the reduced OC residues
increased. So, the optimum ratio ϕ is important to avoid agglomeration caused by Cu at high temperature. The
Fe20Cu40 OC with ϕ = 67 in CLOU had higher the carbon conversion rate (dXc/dt = 0.0083 s−1) and CO2
conversion efficiency (Sco2 = 0.96) compared to the Fe40Cu20 OC with ϕ = 80 in iG-CLC (dXc/dt = 0.0037 s−1
and Sco2 = 0.88).
1. Introduction
Coal chemical-looping combustion (CLC) is a promising alternative
technology for CO2 capture in coal power plants because CLC generates
a higher purity CO2 product stream that minimizes the energy penalty
of CO2 capture [1,2]. Reducing CO2 emissions in coal power generation
would have a positive effect on climate change and is critical to keep
coal in the future energy mix. Research on coal CLC has been the focus
of oxygen carrier (OC) studies and CLC processes during the last
decade. Two main proposed processes for coal CLC are in-situ Gasifi-
cation Chemical-Looping Combustion (iG-CLC) and Chemical-Looping
with Oxygen Uncoupling (CLOU) [3]. Both processes have been suc-
cessfully demonstrated at pilot scale in the range of 1 kWth to 1 MWth
and their results proved the concept of coal CLC [2]. For iG-CLC, a 1
MWth CLC unit combusted bituminous/hard coals with Fe-based oxygen

and air reactor) [4–6]. The 250 kWth pilot unit of a moving bed fuel
reactor coupled with a combined bubbling bed/entrained-flow air re-
actor was tested using synthetic Fe-based metal oxide particles with
sub-bituminous and bituminous coals. Additionally, a large 10 MWe
unit, including four 2.5 MWe modules, is being designed [7]. Fluidized
bed fuel reactors are commonly used in solid fuel CLC tests [2] but the
moving bed fuel reactor is unique and attractive due to maximizing
solids conversion and maintaining complete fuel conversion [7–10]. For
CLOU, Cu-based oxygen carriers (e.g. 60% CuO on a MgAl2O4 support)
were tested to combust coal (e.g. lignite, bituminous, and anthracite) in
a 1.5 kWth CFB [11–14].
In the iG-CLC process, the coal is first gasified to produce syngas
(mainly CO and H2) under H2O and/or CO2 and then the syngas reacts
with the OC (syngas CLC) [3]. This method is used to improve the slow
solid–solid reaction of coal and the OC in the fuel reactor. Fe-based OCs

ilmenite and iron ore) and industrial waste. Also, inexpensive OCs are
preferable since OC losses may occur with coal ash that is purged out of
the fuel reactor to avoid accumulation in the system [3]. From the pilot
plant tests using fluidized bed fuel reactors, lowering the unburnt coal/
char transferred with the OC to the air reactor and improving the CO2
gas quality exiting the fuel reactor are still some of the major challenges

and the amounts of OC and fuel present in the fuel reactor [2]. This
study focuses on that goal. Additionally, proposed process improve-
ments include the addition of a carbon stripper (separating the unburnt
carbon from the OC before the air reactor), conducting an oxygen
polishing step at the outlet of the fuel reactor (high concentration O2 to
oxidize the unburnt gaseous compounds), and use of a moving bed fuel
reactor instead of the fluidized bed fuel reactor to increase gas–solid OC
contact [2]. The pilot test, using the moving bed fuel reactor, reached
near complete coal conversion (95%) and produced high purity CO2
(∼97%) [7]. However, the addition of carbon stripping and oxygen
polishing steps will increase capital and operation costs.
In the CLOU process, coal is burned with gaseous oxygen released
by the OC in the fuel-reactor, similar to conventional coal combustion
[3]. CLOU has higher fuel conversion rates and fuel combustion effi-
ciency compared to iG-CLC because it avoids the slow char gasification
step in the iG-CLC and the fuel is combusted with gaseous oxygen [15].
Cu-based oxygen carriers have been the focus of CLOU development
because CuO has a fast oxygen release rate, high oxygen transport ca-
pacity and favorable combustion thermodynamics that completely
convert fuel to CO2 and H2O [3,16,17]. CLOU with Cu-based OCs have
shown high CO2 capture efficiency and combustion efficiency without
the carbon stripper used in the pilot scale tests [2]. Further materials
development for CLOU to improve the OC lifetime is a still an important
research area based on pilot plant tests [18].
This study investigated bimetallic copper-iron oxygen carriers (Cu-
Fe OC) for coal iG-CLC and CLOU to improve the performance as
compared to monometallic Fe-based and Cu-based OCs. For iG-CLC, the
addition CuO to the monometallic Fe-based OC may increase the CO2
capture efficiency due to favorable combustion thermodynamics of
CuO. The phase transformations of Fe2O3 reduction are Fe2O3 →
Fe3O4 →FeO →Fe. When Fe2O3 is reduced to Fe3O4, it completely
converts CO and H2 to CO2 and H2O based on thermodynamics but it
has low oxygen transport capacity (Ro = 3.3%) [2]. The Ro is defined
as Ro = (mo-mr)/mo, the mass fraction of the oxygen transport between
the fully oxidized mo, and reduced mr, oxygen carrier [3]. The phase
transformations of CuO reduction are CuO →Cu2O →Cu. CuO is re-
duced to Cu2O by releasing gaseous oxygen (R1) and has higher oxygen
transport capacity (Ro = 10%) [3]. The

The future of coal CLC technology development is further process
scale up and industrial validation [2,23]. Therefore, low cost OCs are in
demand and studies on OC preparation using simple methods and low-
cost materials are necessary. Natural Fe ore and Cu ore are relatively
cheap and may be modified to make Cu-Fe OCs. These ores can have
complex compositions so, to simplify analysis of the experimental re-
sults, Cu-Fe OCs were synthesized for this study using pure compounds
and a simple preparation method. In this study, silicon dioxide was
selected as an inert support (designated Cu-Fe-Si) because Cu-Fe-Si is
commercially available and derived from naturally occurring materials
with low cost (e.g. chalcopyrite [24]). The main factors that impact the
CO2 capture efficiency are the type of OC, the temperature in the fuel
reactor, the OC:fuel ratio, and the type of solid fuel [2]. Study of pure
CuO reactivity in coal iG-CLC and CLOU showed that CuO oxygen un-
coupling took place in both processes and the primary reactions
changed with the OC:coal ratio and gas environments (such as Ar or
Ar + H2O) [19]. The objectives of this study are to investigate the re-
activity and CO2 capture efficiency of coal char with Cu-Fe OCs at high
temperature (up to 1100 °C) with different ratios of OC to coal char and
to investigate the primary reactions taking place in iG-CLC and CLOU as
well as the gas product distributions using a fixed bed reactor (FBR)-
quadrupole mass spectrometer (QMS) system. The results will provide
valuable information for OC development and the design and operation
of chemical-looping processes at large scale.

2.2. Bimetallic Cu-Fe-Si OCs preparations and characterization
Bimetallic Cu-Fe OCs were synthesized using mechanical mixing
followed by pressure pelletizing [25–27]. The starting materials were
active metal oxides of cupric oxide (CuO) (powder, 99.99% trace me-
tals) and ferric oxide (Fe2O3) (powder, 99% assay) with an inert silicon
dioxide support (SiO2) (analytical reagent, quartz from XRD analysis)
from Sigma-Aldrich. The SiO2 support was used to inhibit the OCs fast
deactivation via agglomeration and improve the mechanical stability
(e.g. attrition).
A mixture containing 60 wt% CuO and Fe2O3 with 40 wt% SiO2
support was prepared. A water slurry was formed by adding distilled
water to the mixture and stirring intensively. The slurry was dried at
110 °C in an oven under N2 to obtain a suitable mixture for pelletiza-
tion. Cylindrical pellets (13 mm in diameter and 3 mm high) were
pressed (pressing pressure ∼7538 kg/cm2) in a laboratory hydraulic
press. The pellets were calcined at high temperature (1000°C or
1100 °C) under flowing air in a furnace for 2 h in order to enhance OC
mechanical resistance. After calcination, the pellets were ground with a
mortar and pestle and sieved untill particle sizes were less than 180 µm.
The particle sizes of the OCs (Fe60, Fe40Cu20 and Fe20Cu40) were
small and similar with an average particle size of about 100 µm from
sieve analysis (90, 106, 125, 150, and 180 µm screens).
The Cu-Fe OCs with four different ratios of CuO to Fe2O3 (20/40,
40/20, 50/10 and 60/0 wt%) were prepared. The Cu-Fe OCs that con-
tained high CuO content (50 and 60 wt%) exhibited significant sin-
tering at both calcination temperatures; 1000°C and 1100°C. Sintering
was also observed for the Cu40 (40% CuO, 20% Fe2O3 and 40% SiO2)
OC calcined at 1100°C. Adanez et al. also reported the sintering pro-
blem for OCs containing ≥40 wt% CuO with a SiO2 support prepared
using a similar method and calcined at ≥1100 °C [28]. Because of this,
two Cu-Fe OCs were prepared and calcined at 1000 °C: Fe40Cu20 (40%
Fe2O3, 20% CuO and 40% SiO2) and Fe20Cu40 (Table 2). A reference
OC, Fe60 (60% Fe2O3 and 40% SiO2), was also prepared by the same
method as the Cu-Fe OCs to study the impact of CuO addition on the Cu-
Fe OC reactivity (Table 2).
For the Fe40Cu20 OC, the molar ratio of CuO to Fe2O3 was 1:1.
During the calcination process, CuO reacted with Fe2O3 forming copper
ferrite (CuFe2O4). The reaction could be described by R2 [20,29].
CuO(s) + Fe2O3(s) →CuFe2O4 (CuOFe2O3)(s)
(R2)
The X-ray diffraction (XRD) results of the Fe40Cu20 OC showed that
the active phase was mainly CuFe2O4 (mixture of tetragonal and cubic)
with trace amount of CuO (Table 2). This was in agreement with phase
equilibria as a function of oxygen partial pressure in the system for Cu-
Fe-O at 1000 °C [30,31]. For the Fe40Cu20 OC, which contained a
metal mole fraction nFe/(nFe + nCu) = 0.67, in air (21% O2) the stable
compounds were spinel CuxFe3-xO4 (X = 1) + CuO and/or Fe2O3. This
occurred because the composition was close to the line dividing the two
phases of CuxFe3-xO4 (X = 1) + CuO and CuxFe3-xO4 (X = 1) + Fe2O3.
Ristić et al. studied the solid state reaction between CuO and α- Fe2O3
at the initial molar ratio of 1:1 at 800°C, 1000 °C and 1100 °C for 1hr
[32]. They found that the products at 1000 °C contained mostly cu-
prospinel (CuFe2O4, cubic) with a trace amount of tenorite (CuO). The
Fe40Cu20 OC phase composition was mostly in agreement with their
results.
For the Fe20Cu40 OC, the molar ratio of CuO to Fe2O3 was 4:1. A
portion of the CuO reacted with Fe2O3 and formed CuFe2O4 according
to R2 but the remaining CuO was a separate phase. The active phase of
Fe20Cu40 OC was mainly CuO and CuFe2O4 (tetragonal and cubic)
from the XRD analysis (Table 2). This was in agreement with phase
equilibria as a function of oxygen partial pressure for Cu-Fe-O at
1000 °C [30,31]. For the Fe20Cu40 OC, which contained a metal mole
fraction nFe/(nFe + nCu) = 0.2, in air (21% O2) the stable compounds
were spinel CuxFe3-xO4 (X = 1) + CuO. The reference Fe60 OC con-
sisted of Fe2O3 (hematite) from the XRD analysis (Table 2).

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Oxygen uncoupling of Fe40Cu20 OC and Fe20Cu40 OC
In the oxygen uncoupling tests of Fe40Cu20 OC and Fe20Cu40 OC,
gaseous O2 was generated (above 738 °C for the Fe40Cu20 OC and
832 °C for the Fe20Cu40 OC) and detected by the QMS in the product
gas. Fig. 2 shows the oxygen uncoupling (Xo), oxygen uncoupling rates
(dXo/dt) and measured sample temperatures of the Fe40Cu20 OC and
Fe20Cu40 OC. For the Fe40Cu20 OC, the final oxygen uncoupling was
Xo = 0.78 (Table 3), which meant that part of the CuO added during
the Fe40Cu20 OC preparation was decomposed to Cu2O and released
O2 based on the Xo definition (Eq. (1)). The oxygen uncoupling curve
had one peak with the maximum oxygen uncoupling rate dXo/
dt = 0.0031 s−1 at the temperature Tmax = 960 °C.
The oxygen uncoupling behavior of the Fe20Cu40 OC was different
than the Fe40Cu20 OC. Most of the CuO added in the Fe20Cu40 OC
preparation was converted with the final oxygen uncoupling Xo = 0.98
(Table 3). The maximum oxygen uncoupling rate of the Fe20Cu40 OC
was dXo/dt = 0.0077 s−1 at the temperature Tmax = 975 °C. This was

3.2.3. Impact of reaction temperatures on the reactivity of Fe40Cu20 OC
and coal char
To study the impact of reaction temperatures on the Fe40Cu20 OC
reactivity with coal char, the Fe40Cu20 OC was tested at ϕ = 80 at
950°C, 1000°C and 1100 °C in Ar + H2O. Char gasification with SiO2
(same ratio as used with OC experiments) was also performed at the
same conditions because char gasification is the controlling step in iG-
CLC [1]. Based on the results listed in Table 4, both the reduction and
gasification rates increased as temperatures increased but the rate in-
crease was not linear with the temperature. The reduction rate was up
37% from 950 °C to 1000 °C and this was much higher than the rate
increase (5.4%) from 1000 °C to 1100 °C. This was mainly due to the
increased gasification rate from 950 °C to 1000 °C (44%) and was higher
than the rate increase from 1000 °C to 1100 °C (11.1%). The reduction
rates were slightly higher or similar to the gasification rates at all test
temperatures because the reduction of the OC with syngas (mainly
CO + H2) generated from gasification sped up the gasification reaction.
The increasing gasification rate by the OC also resulted in a lower
maximum
temperature
during
reduction
than
char
gasification
(Table 4). During the test at 1100 °C, the maximum temperature
Tmax = 972 °C was below 1000 °C.
The CO2 conversion efficiencies of reduction were higher than char
gasification at the same temperature for all three test temperatures
(Table 4). During reduction, the char was first gasified to syngas then
the syngas (mainly CO and H2) was oxidized to CO2 and H2O by the OC.
The CO2 conversion efficiencies of reduction at 1100 °C were lower
than at 1000 °C and 950 °C (Table 4). For char gasification, the CO2
conversion efficiencies decreased as temperature increased from 950°C
to 1100 °C, which indicated that more CO was in the syngas at high
temperature. As the char gasification temperature increased, more CO
was generated from the water gas reaction (R6) because this reaction is
endothermic and thermodynamically favored at higher temperature.
On the other hand, less CO was converted to CO2 through the water gas
shift reaction (R8) because the reaction is exothermic and thermo-
dynamically favored at lower temperature. This resulted in more CO in
the syngas from high temperature char gasification. CO is not desired in
the product gas and may require an oxygen polishing step to be con-
verted to CO2. Therefore, the Fe40Cu20 OC in coal iG-CLC at tem-
perature ≥1100 °C did not show any benefit based on the CO2 con-
version efficiency and the rate.

3.3. Fe20Cu40 OC with coal char in CLOU
3.3.1. Reactivity of Fe20Cu40 OC and coal char with ϕ 67 at 1000 °C in Ar
The reactivity of the OC with the coal char was tested at 1000 °C in
Ar. The final carbon conversion efficiency of char was Xc = 1.0 at
1000 °C. This was close to the theoretical carbon conversion efficiency
(1) and indicated that the chars were completely converted. Based on
the oxygen transport capacity, Ro = 0.04 g O2/g OC (Table 4), 670 mg
of Fe20Cu40 OC releases 26.8 mg gaseous O2 that ideally combusts
10.05 mg carbon, which is more carbon than present in the char
(8.46 mg). Therefore, the char should be completely combusted by the
O2 released from the Fe20Cu40 OC and generate mainly CO2 and a
small amount of O2 in the product gas.
The QMS detected mainly CO2 with small amounts of O2 and CO in
the product gases during the test (Fig. 6). The O2 was mainly generated
at the end of the test (temperatures above 949 °C, Xc > 0.8) as ex-
pected less carbon and excess O2 in the system. Little CO was produced
at the beginning of the test because the released O2 was relatively small
at the lower temperature and carbon partial oxidation took place.
Therefore, Fe20Cu40 OC uncoupling and char combustion were the
main reactions and char partial combustion was a minor reaction. The
reactions were further supported by XRD phase analysis of the residues
of reduced Fe20Cu40 OC with char. CuO and CuFe2O4 in the Fe20Cu40
OC were reduced to Cu2O and CuFeO2 (Cu2OFe2O3) respectively, which
was the same as the Fe20Cu40 OC oxygen uncoupling. In addition, after
re-oxidation of the reduced Fe20Cu40 OC in 22.4% O2/Ar, the phases
were CuO and CuFe2O4 (tetragonal, cubic) and were the same as the
fresh Fe20Cu40 OC (Table 2). So, after one cycle, the Fe20Cu40 OC did
not change its phases and may be used for multi-cycle testing in the
future.
The carbon conversion rate had a maximum dXc/dt = 0.0083 s−1 at
Tmax = 942 °C. This was lower than the carbon conversion rate of pure
CuO with char at ϕ = 26 and 950 °C, dXc/dt = 0.012 s−1 [19]. It is due
to lower uncoupling rate of the Fe20Cu40 OC compared to CuO
(Table 3). The maximum reduction rate of the Fe20Cu40 OC with char
was slightly faster than the oxygen uncoupling rate of Fe20Cu40 OC. It
also occurred at a lower temperature Tmax than the Fe20Cu40 OC
oxygen uncoupling Tmax (Table 3). This was because the oxygen gen-
erated from uncoupling was consumed by the char and the char com-
bustion increased the rate of oxygen uncoupling. Compared to the
Fe40Cu20 OC with char in iG-CLC at 1000 °C (Table 4), the carbon
conversion rate of Fe20Cu40 OC with char in CLOU was about two
times faster. In addition, the CO2 conversion efficiency of the Fe20Cu40
OC in CLOU (Sco2 = 0.97) was higher than the Fe40Cu20 OC in iG-CLC
(Sco2 = 0.88) (Table 5).
3.3.2. Impact of water on the Fe20Cu40 OC and coal char reactions
The Fe20Cu40 OC with char at ϕ = 67 was also tested at 1000 °C in
Ar + H2O to study the impact of water on its reactivity. The carbon
conversion efficiency Xc = 0.98 was similar to the test at the same ϕ in
Ar. There was a slight difference in the carbon conversion rate and
compositions of the product gas between in Ar + H2O and Ar. In
Ar + H2O, the maximum rate dXc/dt = 0.0088 s−1 at Tmax = 938 °C
was slightly higher due to char gasification and syngas combustion by
O2 released from the OC along with the char combustion with O2.
Syngas combustion is usually faster than direct char combustion. H2
was detected along with CO but the amount of CO was smaller com-
pared to the test with Ar only. This may have occurred due to the water
gas shift reaction at relatively low temperatures as the sample heated to
the test temperature. The XRD phase analysis results of the reduced
Fe20Cu40 OC residue with char in Ar + H2O were the same as the test
in Ar. Therefore, Fe20Cu40 OC uncoupling and char combustion were
the main reactions for the Fe20Cu40 OC with char at ϕ = 67 in
Ar + H2O. Additional char gasification and syngas combustion were
minor reactions.

Bimetallic Cu-Fe oxygen carriers (OC) with SiO2 as a support (Cu-
Fe-Si) have the potential for low cost since they can be derived from
naturally
occurring
materials.
Two
Cu-Fe
OCs:
40%

(by
weight)
(Fe40Cu20)
and
40%CuO + 20%Fe2O3 + 40%SiO2 (by weight) (Fe20Cu40) were pre-
pared using a pressure pelletizing method. From XRD analysis, the
Fe40Cu20 OC contained mostly CuFe2O4, but the Fe20Cu40 OC had
mostly CuO along with CuFe2O4. During the preparation, CuO and
Fe2O3 reacted and formed CuFe2O4 (CuOFe2O3). Both the Fe40Cu20
and Fe20Cu40 OCs released gaseous O2 but the uncoupling rate of the
Fe20Cu40 OC was 2.5 times higher than that of Fe40Cu20 OC.
Reactivities of the prepared Cu-Fe OCs were investigated at high
temperatures in coal iG-CLC and CLOU. For the Fe40Cu20 OC in coal
char iG-CLC with a ratio of OC to char ϕ = 80 at 950 °C, 1000 °C and
1100 °C in Ar + H2O, the reduction rate increased by 37% from 950 °C
to 1000 °C. This was much higher than the rate increase (5.4%) from
1000 °C to 1100 °C due to the char gasification rate increasing. The CO2
conversion efficiency at 1100 °C was lower than at 1000 °C and 950 °C.
Therefore, the Fe40Cu20 OC in coal iG-CLC at temperatures ≥1100 °C
did not have an obvious benefit. The CuO in Cu-Fe OC improved the
CO2 conversion efficiency (Sco2) compared to the monometallic Fe-
based OC at the maximum Sco2 (excess Fe2O3 to Fe3O4). To achieve this
advantage, the optimum ratio of OC to coal (like ϕ = 80) is important
to avoid agglomeration caused by Cu at high temperature. The contents
of Cu in the reduced Fe40Cu20 OC increased as the ratio of OC to char
descended from ϕ = 80, 40 and 24. This maybe be a result of easily
reduced Cu oxides compared to Fe oxides. In addition, the CO2 con-
version efficiencies reduced as the ratio decreased.
For the Fe20Cu40 OC in coal char CLOU with ϕ = 67 tested with
Ar + H2O, the primary reactions and reactivities were similar to those
with Ar. As the ratio of OC to char decreased ϕ = 18, the reactions and
reactivities were similar to iG-CLC with Fe40Cu20 OC and char at
ϕ = 24 in Ar + H2O. At lower ϕ = 18, Cu was generated and may cause
agglomeration at high temperature. To prevent this, the optimum ratio
of OC to coal in CLOU is very important, especially in a coal gasification
gas environment (e.g. steam).
For the Fe40Cu20 OC in coal iG-CLC with ϕ = 80 at 1000 °C, the
carbon conversion rate was dXc/dt = 0.0037 s−1 and CO2 conversion
efficiency Sco2 = 0.88. The carbon conversion rate of Fe20Cu40 OC in
coal CLOU with ϕ = 67 was dXc/dt = 0.0083 s−1 and about two times
faster than the Fe40Cu20 OC in coal iG-CLC. In addition, the CO2
conversion efficiency of the Fe20Cu40 OC was Sco2 = 0.96 and higher
than the Fe40Cu20 OC. Overall, Cu-Fe-Si has potential as a competitive
oxygen carrier in the high temperature coal chemical looping com-
bustion process.
