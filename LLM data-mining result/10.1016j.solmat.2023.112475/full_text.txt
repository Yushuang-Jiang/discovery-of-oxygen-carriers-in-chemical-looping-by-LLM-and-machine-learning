A B S T R A C T   
The redox system of Co3O4/CoO is very promising for the thermochemical energy storage systems coupled to 
concentrated solar power plants because of its high energy storage density and reversibility. Nevertheless, the 
practical application of Co3O4/CoO system is limited by thermal hysteresis of the redox reaction and material 
sintering. Here, we synthesized Co-based metal oxides doped with Al and Cr, and studied the influence of the 
doping of these two metal elements on the thermochemical properties and sintering resistance of the pure Co3O4/ 
CoO system. Both Al and Cr doping narrows the thermal hysteresis of the Co3O4/CoO system. In particular, when 
the molar doping rate of Al is 10%, the thermal hysteresis of Co–Al oxides is only 4.02 ◦C, which is much 
narrower than that of pure Co3O4, and the increase of its oxidation temperature also means that a higher level of 
thermal energy can be released during oxidation. In the redox cycle, the addition of Al inhibits material sintering 
and the formation of a loose porous structure facilitates the oxidation, ensuring the reversibility of the redox 
reaction. The 10% Al–Co honeycomb shows stable conversion behavior with an average conversion of 77.75% in 
15 redox cycles. Co–Al oxides exhibit excellent oxidative exothermic characteristics and long-term cycle stability, 
making them suitable for efficient thermochemical energy-storage processes.   
1. Introduction 
Currently, owing to environmental concerns and the rapid depletion 
of fossil fuel resources, renewable energy sources have attracted 
tremendous interest for energy production. Solar power is one of the 
most promising renewables, owing to its low cost and wide availability 
[1]. However, the intermittency and low efficiency of solar power makes 
the incorporation of energy storage systems crucial for large-scale ap­
plications. Among the various energy storage technologies, thermal 
energy storage (TES) is characterized by low cost and high efficiency, 
making it suitable for concentrated solar power (CSP) plants. TES can 
store excess energy during idle operation and release it during off-sun 
hours, thus ensuring continuous operation of the CSP [2,3]. Solar 
power tower plants equipped with molten salt (a type of TES) can in­
crease the annual solar energy utilization from 25% to 65% [4]. 
Based on the heat-storage mechanism, TES can be divided into sen­
sible heat storage (SHS), latent heat storage (LHS), and thermochemical 
energy storage (TCES) [5]. SHS stores or releases energy by changing the 
temperature of a liquid or solid storage medium. LHS uses the 
solid-liquid phase change process of phase change materials (PCM) to 
store and release energy under near-isothermal conditions. TCES stores 
energy through reversible chemical reactions and is technically complex 
and less mature than SHS and LHS. Nevertheless, it exhibits the highest 
energy storage density (five and fifteen times higher than those of LHS 
and SHS systems, respectively [6]) and an infinite storage period at 
ambient temperature and heat-pumping capability, which makes it a 
promising candidate for high-temperature energy storage [7]. 
Among the various TCES systems, metal oxide systems use air as the 
reactant and heat transfer fluid, avoiding the need for gas storage and 
management systems and high-temperature heat exchangers (e.g., metal 
carbonate systems or metal hydroxide systems [8,9]), and their oper­
ating temperatures are compatible with those of CSP plants. Therefore, 
metal oxide systems are perfectly suited to the operating conditions of 
CSP plants with volumetric air central receivers [10].

Metal oxide systems store and release energy via the following steps 
[11]:  
Charge (on-sun): MxOy + z + ΔH→MxOy + z/2O2                                       
Discharge (off-sun): MxOy + z/2O2→MxOy + z + ΔH                                 
Pure metal oxide systems have been intensively investigated, the 
feasibility of 74 pure solid oxide systems for thermochemical heat 
storage was investigated by Wong et al. Only 16 pure metal oxides 
exhibit redox transformation behavior, which can be used to store and 
release heat within their corresponding temperature ranges. Consid­
ering the material cost, energy density, reaction temperature, and 
cycling stability, the three metal oxide systems of Co3O4/CoO, Mn2O3/ 
Mn3O4, and BaO2/BaO exhibit potential for TCES [12]. Among them, 
Co3O4/CoO system has the highest energy storage density (844 kJ/kg) 
and complete reaction reversibility [13]. The Co3O4/CoO redox reaction 
can be expressed as follows [11]: 
2Co3O4 + ΔH ↔6CoO + O2 ΔH = 844 kJ/kg
(1) 
However, some inherent characteristics of the Co3O4/CoO system 
hinder its usability without further modification. First, it exhibits poor 
structural stability. At the microstructural level, when the thermal cycle 
temperature rises to 1100 ◦C, severe sintering occurs during the redox 
reaction, which reduces the surface area, porosity and increases the 
grain size [14,15]. Redox reaction kinetics are limited by different 
transport phenomena. Reduction rate is influenced by heat transport, 
whereas oxidation rate is limited by the oxygen diffusion rate and thus 
sintering causes a gradual decay of the oxidation rate [1]. Wong et al. 
[12] suggested that the re-oxidation kinetics of pure Co3O4/CoO system 
needs to be improved to ensure a complete redox reaction, and thus the 
structural stability of the material is a key factor in the long-term stable 
operation of TCES. Secondly, the reduction and oxidation reactions of 
the Co3O4/CoO system theoretically occur at the same temperature. 
However, in practice, the onset temperature of oxidation is slightly 
lower than that of reduction due to kinetic difficulties encountered 
during the oxidation step, which is referred to as thermal hysteresis [15, 
16]. More specifically, the thermal hysteresis of the Co3O4/CoO redox 
systems reported in previous studies ranges from 33 to 50 ◦C [10,17,18]. 
Narrowing the thermal hysteresis of the Co3O4/CoO system means that 
heat can be stored and released in a closer temperature range, thus 
improving energy efficiency of charge/discharge and promoting system 
integration.

A brief summary of various mixed oxide systems is shown in Table 1. 
Block et al. [14,18] showed that both addition of Fe2O3 (13% mol Fe) 
and CuO (10% mol Cu) to the Co3O4/CoO system results in higher 
microstructural stability compared to pure Co3O4 in high-temperature 
cycles (>1100 ◦C). This may be due to the effective grain boundary 
barrier mechanism formed by the existence of two-phase components at 
high temperatures. In addition, the deceleration of the reduction reac­
tion of Co–Fe and Co–Cu mixed oxides in long-term cycles is less pro­
nounced than that of pure Co3O4. Wu et al. [24] reported that the 
addition of CuO with relatively small particle size can improve the 
reversibility of cobalt oxide. Carrillo et al. [19] narrowed the thermal 
hysteresis of the Mn2O3/Mn3O4 redox system from 225 to 80 ◦C by 
doping with 20 mol% Fe and 5 mol% Cu. Portilla-Nieto et al. [20,25] 
used nickel oxide doping to lower the reduction onset temperature of the 
Co3O4/CoO system to below 700 ◦C, broadening the application tem­
perature range of the Co-based oxide systems. And they improved the 
cyclability and reduced the densification of Co–Ni oxide by adding 5 wt 
% SiO2. Moreover, the addition of Mg was also used to reduce the 
reduction onset temperature of the Co3O4/CoO system [26]. Wu et al. 
[27] found that the addition of LiCoO2 could improve the redox rate of 
the Co3O4/CoO system and increase its discharging heat. Wong et al. 
[12] reported that the addition of Al2O3 and Cr2O3 can improve the 
re-oxidation rate of the Co3O4/CoO system. Specifically, the doping of 5 
at% Al2O3 and 5.6 at% Cr2O3 can improve the re-oxidation rate from 
36% of the pure Co3O4/CoO system at a cooling rate of 30 ◦C/min to 
100% and 84%, respectively. In addition, the conversion of Co3O4 -5% 
Al2O3 sample increased after cycling. Karagiannakis et al. [28] reported 
that Co3O4-based honeycomb with 10 wt% Al2O3 demonstrated excel­
lent structural stability in 104 cycles. Al2O3 and Cr2O3 are promising 
doping materials to enhance the reaction properties of cobalt oxide. 
However, the kinetic characteristics of the redox reaction of Co–Al and 
Co–Cr oxides and the conversion performance of metal oxide honey­
combs need to be further investigated. 
In this work, the effects of Al and Cr doping on the redox reaction 
temperature (thermal hysteresis) and anti-sintering properties of the 
Co3O4/CoO system were investigated with special attention to the 
possible improvement of the cycling stability. And the redox kinetics of 
Co–Al and Co–Cr oxides were analyzed to add more insight into the role 
of Al and Cr incorporation. Finally, the material with the best energy 
storage characteristics was selected to prepared Co3O4-based 
honeycomb. 
Co–Al and Co–Cr mixed oxides with different doping ratios were 
successfully synthesized by modifying the Pechini method. The ther­
mochemical properties and cycling stability of these oxides were eval­
uated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning 
calorimetry (DSC), and their structural and morphological evolutions 
before and after redox cycling were characterized by X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The redox perfor­
mances of Co3O4-based honeycomb were evaluated using a TCES 
system. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Material preparation 
Pure Co3O4, Co–Al, and Co–Cr oxides with different molar doping 
rates x (i.e., Al/(Al + Co)) were synthesized by modifying the Pechini 
method [36]. 
Certain amounts of metal (Me) precursors and citric acid (CA, AR, 
Sinopharm) were dissolved in deionized water (10 mL) in a beaker and 
stirred at 70 ◦C for 1.5 h. The following Me precursors were used for 
synthesis: Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O (99.99%, Aladdin), Al(NO3)3⋅9H2O (99.99%, 
Aladdin), and Cr(NO3)3⋅9H2O (99.99%, Aladdin). Subsequently, the 
temperature of the solution was increased to 95 ◦C and ethylene glycol 
(EG, 99.5%, Sinopharm) was added. The resulting solution was stirred at 
95 ◦C for more than 2 h to remove excess solvent, and the obtained resin

2.3. Reaction kinetic analysis 
Similar to the redox cycle analysis, the reaction kinetic analysis was 
performed using a STA 449 F3 Jupiter instrument. In order to avoid the 
reduction of materials before reaching a constant temperature, the 
reduction kinetics was analyzed at three different heating rates (5, 10 
and 20 ◦C/min). And the reduction was conducted under Ar atmosphere 
to eliminate any possible effect of oxidation. In the oxidation kinetic 
analysis, materials were firstly reduced by heating them up to 1100 ◦C to 
generate the reduced phase. The reduced materials were then cooled to a 
certain temperature under Ar atmosphere, and then subjected to 
isothermal oxidation under a synthetic air flow (80 mL/min Ar and 20 
mL/min O2). Some authors [22] have pointed out that high-temperature 
oxidation may be decelerated due to the effect of inverse reaction. 
Therefore, we measured the oxidation rate of cobalt oxide at different 
temperatures to select the appropriate temperature for the oxidation 
kinetic analysis.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Thermochemical properties of Co–Al and Co–Cr mixed oxides 
The thermochemical properties of each sample were studied by 
performing three successive thermal cycles between 750 and 1100 ◦C 
under synthetic air flow in the STA instrument. The redox reaction of Co- 
based mixed oxides involves two steps. Considering the redox process of 
pure Co3O4 (Eq. (1)) as an example, in the heating steps, Co3O4 is 
endothermically reduced to CoO, O2 is released, and the final theoretical 
mass loss is 6.64%. In the cooling steps, CoO reacts with O2 to form 
Co3O4 and releases heat, whereas the previous mass loss is recovered. 
The mass change in the reduction and oxidation reactions of each 
sample can be easily monitored by TGA. Fig. 2 shows the TGA redox 
curves of the Co–Al and Co–Cr mixed oxides. The mass change data for 
the second redox cycle are presented in Table 2. Pure Co3O4 exhibited 
the largest mass loss of 6.48% in the second cycle, resulting in a 97.6% 
conversion (6.48%/6.64%) from Co3O4 to CoO. Among the Co–Al mixed 
oxides, the 5% Al–Co sample exhibited the largest mass loss of 5.8%, and 
the mass loss of the Co–Al mixed oxides decreased with increasing Al 
doping (Fig. 1a). Among the Co–Cr oxides (Fig. 1b), the 10% Cr–Co 
sample exhibited the largest mass loss of 4.63%. The 30% Cr–Co sample 
demonstrated the smallest mass loss (1.44%) of all samples, indicating 
that only a small fraction of the active material was involved in the redox 
reaction. In addition, the further reduction of the Co–Cr mixed oxides 
may be limited by the upper temperature limit. Therefore, we increased 
the upper reaction temperature limit of the reaction to 1500 ◦C for the 
10% Cr–Co sample. This sample reacted completely below 1100 ◦C, 
which also justified the choice of the temperature range of 750–1100 ◦C. 
Notably, the mass gain in the oxidation reaction was smaller than the 
mass loss in the reduction reaction for all samples (Table 2), which 
implies incomplete oxidation reactions. More specifically, the 10% 
Al–Co sample exhibited the most complete oxidation reaction among all 
the samples, with a mass gain of 99.56% (4.53%/4.55%), whereas only 
84.57% (3.51%/4.15%) of the mass loss of the 5% Cr–Co sample in the 
heating step was recovered in the subsequent cooling step. This can be 
attributed to the reactivity loss owing to sintering and the fast-cooling 
rate in the oxidation reaction temperature range [12], which prevents 
the oxidation reaction from proceeding completely in the cooling step. 
As shown in Fig. 1, the Co–Al and Co–Cr mixed oxides have different 
degrees of mass gain before the start of the third reduction reaction, 
which enables mass loss in the previous redox step to be recovered. The 
mass of the 5% Cr–Co sample increased by 0.4% before the start of the 
third reduction reaction. In the first heating step, the increased mass of 
the Co–Al and Co–Cr mixed oxides may have been caused by partial 
reduction of the synthetic samples during the calcination process. 
The thermal storage and release of the Co–Al and Co–Cr mixed metal 
oxides were evaluated by DSC (Fig. 3ab). In the heating stage, an 
endothermic peak appeared in the DSC curve, which corresponded to 
the reduction reaction of metal oxides. In contrast, during the cooling 
stage, an exothermic peak appeared, corresponding to the oxidation 
reaction of the reduced metal oxides. Fig. 4 depicts the endothermic and 
exothermic peaks for the second cycle of pure Co3O4, and the enthalpy of 
the reduction and oxidation reactions was determined by integrating the 
endothermic and exothermic peak areas, respectively, using the 
tangential method. The reduction and oxidation reaction enthalpies and 
reaction temperature ranges of the Co–Al and Co–Cr mixed metal oxides 
are depicted in Fig. 5. Similar to the variation pattern of mass loss in the 
reduction reaction, pure Co3O4 exhibited the highest reduction enthalpy 
of 533.85 kJ/kg. Among the Co–Al mixed metal oxides, the 5% Al–Co 
sample showed the highest reduction enthalpy of 499.08 kJ/kg, while 
the 30% Al–Co sample revealed the lowest reaction enthalpy of 146.07 
kJ/kg. Compared with Co–Al mixed metal oxides with the same doping 
ratio, the reaction enthalpy of Co–Cr mixed metal oxides was slightly 
lower, and the 30% Cr–Co sample showed the lowest reduction enthalpy 
of 94.66 kJ/kg. Notably, the enthalpy of the reduction reaction of pure 
Co3O4 was much lower than the theoretical value (844 kJ/kg) reported 
in the literature. This large discrepancy is primarily due to the change in 
enthalpy of 221.8 ± 16 kJ/kg resulting from the change in the Co3+ spin 
state, which could not be determined by DSC [45]. As shown in Fig. 5, 
the enthalpy of the oxidation reaction is higher than that of the reduc­
tion reaction, except for the 5% and 20% Cr–Co samples. We speculate 
that the low thermal conductivity of the alumina crucible and oxide 
materials may cause nonuniform temperature distribution within the 
materials, resulting in a deviation in the measured reaction enthalpy. 
Several researchers have proposed similar ideas [46]. 
The reduction and oxidation reaction onset temperature and thermal 
hysteresis of the Co–Al and Co–Cr mixed metal oxides are shown in 
Fig. 5 and Table 3. For pure cobalt oxide, the difference between the 
onset temperature of reduction and oxidation was 54.12 ◦C. When 
doped with different rates of Al, the oxidation onset temperature 
increased from 861.05 ◦C to >886.36 ◦C, whereas the reduction onset 
temperature decreased slightly. As shown in Fig. 5a and Table 3, the 
thermal hysteresis of the Co–Al mixed oxides was much narrower than 
that of pure cobalt oxide. The 20% Al–Co sample exhibited the nar­
rowest thermal hysteresis of 1.3 ◦C. As shown in Table 3, the reduction 
onset temperature of Co–Cr mixed metal oxides was more than 15 ◦C

kJ/mol. The 5% Al–Co and 5% Cr–Co samples showed the reduced 
activation energy of 55.60 ± 7.63 and 47.60 ± 8.89 kJ/mol, respec­

difficulties in the oxidation process. As shown in Table 3, the oxidation 
reaction of the 5% Al–Co and 5% Cr–Co samples started at higher 
temperature than that of CoO, which corresponds to an earlier time in 
the cooling process. 
In contrast to the reduction reaction kinetics, the doping of Al and Cr 
changed the oxidation reaction mechanism of CoO (Fig. 8). As shown in 
Fig. 8a, the oxidation of CoO is closest to nucleation and growth 
mechanisms in the α range of 0–0.5, and the oxidation could be 
described by the third-order model (F3) when α > 0.5. In the case of the 
5% Al–Co sample (Fig. 8b), its first half of the oxidation can be described 
by Avrami-Erofeyev (A4) model, while at high conversion (α > 0.65) the 
oxidation showing an intermediate behavior between the 3-D diffusion 
(D3) and the third-order model (F3) models. As can be seen in Fig. 8c, 
the experimental data for the 5% Cr–Co sample exhibits severe fluctu­
ations in the first half of the oxidation, which implies a non-uniform 
oxidation. At a glance, its oxidation can be approximately described 
by the 3-D diffusion (D3) model. 
These findings indicate that doping with Al and Cr can narrow the 
thermal hysteresis, which is beneficial for the application of Co-based 
metal oxide reactors. The narrowing of thermal hysteresis can reduce 
the energy consumption of the heating and cooling steps of the TCES 
system, which will improve the charge/discharging energy efficiency. In 
addition, when the oxidation temperature increases, a higher level of 
heat energy is released during the oxidation process.

structural stability before and after thermal cycling. After thermal 
cycling, there was no change in the overall size of the honeycomb 
structure, with a diameter of 29 mm both before and after thermal 
cycling. After thermal cycling, the diameter of the honeycomb channel 
was measured to be 6.67 mm, which is 1.6% smaller than that of fresh 
honeycomb (6.78 mm). 
In summary, the XRD results reveal that only the 10% Al–Co sample 
exhibited similar diffraction patterns before and after long-term cycling, 
whereas the CoO phase appears in the other three samples after thermal 
cycling, which implies an incomplete oxidation reaction. The doping of 
Al acted as an inhibitor of material sintering. In contrast to the densified 
sintering of CoO, the porous microstructure of the 10% Al–Co sample is 
more favorable for the redox reaction. In addition, the 10% Al–Co 
honeycomb showed good structural stability during 15 thermal cycles.

as follows. 
(1) Pure Co3O4 exhibited the highest conversion and reduction re­
action enthalpy (533.85 kJ/kg) among the Co–Al and Co–Cr 
mixed metal oxides. However, the oxidation reaction could not be 
completed in a single thermal cycle. Its wide thermal hysteresis 
(54.12 ◦C) was detrimental to the high-efficiency redox reaction. 
Additionally, its microstructure underwent severe sintering after 
several thermal cycles.  
(2) The addition of Al can significantly narrow the thermal hysteresis 
of Co3O4/CoO by increasing its oxidation temperature and 
reducing its reduction temperature. Moreover, Co–Al oxides 
exhibit a more stable microstructure during the thermal cycling 
than that of Co3O4/CoO. The 10% Al–Co oxide is the most 
promising material due to its great reactivity (mass change of 
4.86% in the 45th cycle) and narrow thermal hysteresis as low as 
4.02 ◦C. The addition of other dopants is suggested to enhance 
reaction conversion in future studies.  
(3) The results of the metal oxide honeycomb experiment indicate 
that the 10% Al–Co honeycomb exhibits stable conversion 
behavior with an average conversion of 77.75% and good struc­
tural stability in 15 consecutive redox cycles. And the incorpo­
ration of pore-forming agents is proposed as a means to optimize 
the pore structure within the honeycomb, thereby enhancing the 
energy storage performances.  
(4) Co–Cr oxides display an unstable microstructure and incomplete 
oxidation. For instance, the 5% Cr–Co sample exhibited a mere 
84.57% mass recovery rate during the oxidation reaction in the 
2nd cycle. These drawbacks make Co–Cr oxides unsuitable as 
TCES materials. Although these materials exhibit a narrower 
thermal hysteresis and a higher operational temperature than 
those of Co3O4/CoO.
