A B S T R A C T   
Because of spinel’s suitable redox properties, this article focuses on its application as a candidate of thermochemical energy storage materials. Pechini method was 
used to synthesize of the copper and manganese mixed metal oxides with spinel structure. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to determine the crystal structure of the 
sample. The thermochemical energy storage of the samples was measured by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). It was 
found that sample with Cu mole ratio 1/3 showed suitable cycle stability and more flexible temperature range. It is a potential candidate for thermal energy storage 
material. Through the comparison of air and nitrogen-air redox cycle, it is revealed that the reduction is more thorough in inert atmosphere than in air atmosphere.   
1. Introduction 
Concentrated solar energy (CSP) systems will play an important role 
in future renewable energy or heat production. Romero et al. review the 
concentrated solar power plant because of its suitable power generation 
capacity and makes it possible to integrate thermal storage equipment 
and mix with standby fossil fuels [1]. Thermal energy storage (TCS) 
system is proposed, which can still achieve continuous use of energy 
during the intermittent period [2,3]. TCS has a higher energy storage 
density in the field of high-temperature storage. From the practical view, 
thermochemical storage based on reversible chemical reactions is a very 
promising research area. As compared with sensible heat and latent heat 
technology, the reversible thermochemical energy storage is based on 
the existence of materials in the form of stable and transportable solids, 
has a higher energy storage density, can store heat and materials in the 
form of chemical bonds. In addition, the thermochemical energy storage 
can provide longer storage time because it can prevent heat loss by 
eliminating reactants such as O2, CO2 or H2O in the redox, carbonation 
or hydration process [4]. Usually carbonation and hydration process will 
provide very high energy density, but the reaction conditions are more 
severe. On the contrary, redox process can work in atmosphere, so they 
are more realistic and easier to achieve. The degradation for materials 
based on redox process after multiple cycles should be negligible. In 
addition, the cost should be as low as possible, and the volumetric en­
ergy density should be high. 
In the list of potential single metal oxides for TCS, cobalt oxide, 
manganese oxide and copper oxide show interesting properties [5–8]. 
Among them, the Co3O4/CoO system shows excellent reaction kinetics, 
complete reaction reversibility and good cycle stability [9]. The main 
disadvantages of cobalt oxide involve cost and toxicity issues and high 
reduction temperature. The report pointed out that the weight energy 
storage density of Mn2O3/Mn3O4 is about 110–160 kJ/kg [10,11]. 
However, the material is significantly slowed down in the oxidation 
stage and only partially re-oxidized. The main reason is that the strong 
kinetic limitation and the huge difference between the reduction and 
oxidation initiation temperatures, leading to poor reversibility of the 
reaction. Haenchen et al. [12]. measured the thermochemical energy 
storage of CuO/Cu2O oxidation pair. It is confirmed that CuO/Cu2O 
system is a kind of chemical energy storage material with great potential 
[12]. However, the material is prone to sintering during the 
high-temperature reduction process, which has a greater impact on the 
reactivity and stability of the material. 
Spinel is composed of magnesium aluminum oxide (MgAl2O4). The 
general composition is AB2O4, in which A may be magnesium, iron, zinc, 
manganese, or nickel; B may be aluminum, chromium, or iron; and O is 
oxygen. Spinel structure reduce the Gibbs free energy of the reaction 
intermediate to the optimal level, and simultaneously facilitate the 
transport of the electrons and the diffusions of generated gas, which 
results in improved catalytic performance [13]. Since oxidation of these 
compounds is thermodynamically more favorable, this process may

twice that of pure cobalt oxide. 
In order to improve the redox performance of the material, the 
performance of the material can be improved by controlling the syn­
thesis of the morphology or by adding inert materials; alternatively, the 
pure oxide can be modified by adding a second transition metal oxide to 
adjust the reaction kinetics, the temperature range of the redox reaction 
and the stability of the cycle. In this paper, the improved Pechini method 
is used to synthesize Cu-Mn-O mixed metal oxides with spinel structure. 
After high temperature treatment, they can be quickly sintered to reach 
a stable structure directly. The thermochemical energy storage of the 
samples was studied by TGA and DSC. Sample with Cu mole ratio 1/3 
showed excellent cycle stability and more flexible temperature range. It 
is a potential candidate for thermal energy storage material. Through 
the comparison of air and nitrogen-air redox cycle, it is revealed that the 
reduction is more thorough in inert atmosphere than in air atmosphere. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Materials preparation 
In this experiment, an improved Pechini method was used to syn­
thesize Cu-Mn-O mixed metal oxides with spinel structure. The molar 
mass ratio of Cu(NO3)2•3H2O (>99% purity) and Mn(NO3)2 (>99% 
purity) is 1:0, 1:2, 0:1 dissolved in a certain amount of deionized water, 
and the molar mass ratio of metal cation, citric acid and ethylene glycol 
according to the ratio of 3:15:10, mix the dissolved metal nitrate and 
citric acid evenly, concentrate at 70 ◦C for 3 h, then add ethylene glycol 
and concentrate at 90 ◦C for 2 h to form a viscous gel shape. Then the 
configured gel was placed in a muffle furnace, calcined at 200 ◦C in air 
for 3 h, then calcined in air at 450 ◦C for 4 h, and further calcined at 
700 ◦C in air atmosphere for 4 h, and naturally cooled down to obtain Cu 
synthetic material with a ratio of 1,1/3,0 (the ratio of the molar mass of 
copper to the sum of the molar mass of copper and the molar mass of 
manganese). 
2.2. Characterization of the test 
JSM-IT500 Scanning Electron Microscope was used to observe the 
morphology of the sample. X-ray diffraction is measured on a BRUKER 
D2 PHASER diffractometer with CuKα radiation (λ= 1.54178 Å) at a 
scan rate of 0.2 s−1. NETZSCH’s STA 449 F3 thermogravimetric analysis 
was used to determine the redox cycle performance, and the measured 
sample was corrected by the blank control group and then measured. Put 
a 15 mg sample in a 90 mL alumina crucible and perform multiple redox 
cycle tests. The cycle temperature of the first cycle test is between 650 ◦C 
and 1050 ◦C, heated from room temperature at 10 K•min−1 to 1050 ◦C, 
then cooled at 10 K•min−1 to 650 ◦C, and then at 650 ◦C to 1050 ◦C. The 
test is carried out at a circulation rate of 10 K•min−1 and the air flow rate 
is 100 mL•min−1 (PO2= 0.21). The second round of cyclic testing is 
based on the first round of testing, and the air flow of 100 mL•min−1 is 
intermittently introduced with nitrogen and air. At the same time, dif­
ferential scanning calorimetry  was performed on TGA to study the

3.2. High temperature redox cycle 
Fig. 4 shows the variation curve of the weight of metal oxide ther­
mochemical energy storage with Cu = 1 and Cu = 1/3 as a function of 
temperature, as well as the reaction temperature of each stage of 
reduction and oxidation. The average onset temperature of the metal 
oxide Cu= 1, Cu= 1/3 of the 5 redox cycles is shown in Fig. 4(A). It can 
be clearly evidented that at least with in 5 cycles, both the metal oxide 
Cu= 1 and Cu= 1/3 exhibit a stable redox cycle temperature. The main 
change in the mass reduction of the metal oxide Cu= 1 take place when 
the temperature exceeds 997.68 ◦C, which is caused by the decompo­
sition of copper oxide into cuprous oxide and the release of oxygen; the 
main change in the mass increment of the metal oxide Cu= 1 take place 
when the temperature exceeds 978.35 ◦C because the cuprous oxide 
absorbs oxygen in the air and reacts to produce copper oxide, and the 
molar mass increases. These can be obtained from the reaction equation 
of formula (4). 
The main change in the mass reduction of the metal oxide Cu= 1/3 
take place when the temperature exceeds 929.44 ◦C, which is caused by 
the decomposition of CuMn2O4 into CuMnO2 and Mn3O4 and the release 
of oxygen; the main change in the mass increment of the metal oxide 
Cu= 1/3 take place below 861.05 ◦C. This is because CuMnO2 and 
Mn3O4 absorb oxygen in the air and react to produce CuMn2O4, and the 
molar mass increases. These can be obtained from the reaction equation 
of formula (5). Comparing the reduction reaction temperature of the 
metal oxide Cu= 1 and Cu= 1/3, the reduction temperature of the metal 
oxide Cu= 1/3 is 68.24 ◦C lower than the reduction temperature of Cu=
1. It can be determined that Cu= 1/3 greatly improves the oxygen 
mobility compared to Cu= 1. Also comparing the oxidation temperature 
of the metal oxide Cu= 1 and Cu= 1/3, the oxidation temperature of 
Cu= 1/3 is 117.30 ◦C lower than the oxidation temperature of Cu= 1, 
which greatly increases the temperature gap of the reaction and it very 
benefits for the adjustment of operating temperature, energy density and 
process kinetics. The mass changes of the metal oxides Cu= 1 and 1/3

In order to analyze and compare the difference in more detail, the 30 
mass change curve with temperature is shown in Fig. 6 (C,D). As shown 
in Fig. 6(C), it can be clearly observed that as the number of cycles de­
creases, the reduction temperature for metal oxide Cu= 1 decreases with 
the number of cycles. Decreasing from 846.12 ◦C in the 8th time to 
844.30 ◦C in the 18th cycle, after that, it dropped from 884.30 ◦C in the 
18th to 842.36 ◦C in the 23rd one. This shows that the stability of metal 
oxide Cu= 1 becomes worse and the cycle performance decreases as the 
number of cycles increases; while for the metal oxide Cu=1/3, the 
reduction temperature shown in Fig. 6(D) is stable at 825.17 ◦C without 
significant changes. The termination temperature of the reduction re­
action is 1034.01 ◦C. As compared with the metal oxide Cu= 1, the 
temperature is reduced by 12.18±2 ◦C, which makes the reduction 
temperature far away from the melting point of Cu2O by 1235 ◦C and 
avoids suboxide. Inhibition of copper sintering should be one of the 
reasons why the material with the mixed metal oxide Cu= 1/3 shows 
good cycle stability. Comparing the oxidation reaction temperature of 
the metal oxide Cu= 1 and Cu= 1/3, it can be clearly observed that as 
the number of cycles increases, the oxidation temperature metal oxide 
Cu= 1 decreases , from 1048.92 ◦C in the 8th cycle to 1046.19 ◦C in the 
18th one. After that, the temperature dropped from 1046.19 ◦C in the 
18th to 1044.59 ◦C between the 18th and the 23rd cycle. The temper­
ature of the mixed metal oxide Cu= 1/3 is 91.89±0.70 ◦C lower than 
that of the mixed metal oxide Cu= 1. After 30 cycles, it was evident that 
both reduction and oxidation temperature of mixed metal oxide Cu= 1 
showed a significant decline of the gradient., On the contrary, both 
reduction and oxidation temperature of mixed metal oxide Cu= 1/3 
were stable, indicating the suitable cyclic stability of mixed metal oxide 
Cu= 1/3. Most materials are difficult to apply in TCS systems under high 
temperature conditions. They sensitively react to sintering process of 
compacting or generation of a solid mass and will gradually lose their 
reaction stability with the loss of surface area at high temperatures. The 
material based on the mixed metal oxide Cu= 1/3 displays excellent 
cycle stability and it is a potential candidate for practical applications. 
In the second round of TGA thermogravimetric experiment, we 
heated the mixed metal oxide Cu=1/3 in a nitrogen atmosphere to 
1050 ◦C, and then cooled it to 650 ◦C in the air to perform a reoxidation 
cycle experiment. As shown in Fig. 7, by comparing 5 cycles under inert 
atmosphere and air atmosphere, the reduction temperature in an inert 
atmosphere is significantly lower than that in an air atmosphere, with a 
decrease of 112.39 ◦C. This is because the reduction reaction in an inert 
atmosphere happened more easily than in an air atmosphere without 
oxygen partial pressure. While the oxidation temperature in an inert 
atmosphere has a slight increase compared with that in an air atmo­
sphere, with an increase of 36.03 ◦C. The mass reduction under inert 
atmosphere is 4.33 wt%, and the average change in mass under air at­
mosphere is 3.89 wt%. The difference between the two values is 0.44 wt 
%, which proves that more complete reduction reaction can occur under 
inert atmosphere than under air atmosphere. 
The DSC data analysis for the first and second rounds of the mixed 
metal oxide Cu= 1/3 was compared and the change of heat flow with 
time is shown in Fig. 7(C). Through the analysis of Fig. 7(C) it is 
observed that there are two peaks in the energy absorbed in the 
reduction stage under an inert atmosphere, which are far greater than
