A B S T R A C T   
To date, numerous dopants have been investigated to promote the initial heat storage performance or sintering 
resistance of Co3O4 for thermochemical energy storage. Owing to the different synthesis methods and heat 
storage test conditions, the performances of these materials are not comparable. In this work, nine dopants, 
including four unreported dopants, were systematically compared and screened under the same conditions. 
Results demonstrated that for five redox cycles, the Si- and Mg- doped sample had adverse effects on the con­
version rate, and the others maintained the conversion rate above 0.95. Cu and Zr doping exhibited the highest 
re-oxidation rate values, and those of the others were lower than that of pure cobalt oxide. Moreover, except for 
Cu and Zr, all dopants adversely affected energy density. According to aforementioned heat storage properties, 
Cu-, Zr-doped samples, and pure cobalt oxide were subjected to 100 cycles for further comparison. The Cu-doped 
sample exhibited a higher re-oxidation rate and energy density, and the Zr-doped sample exhibited more stable 
cyclability. Finally, by doping Cu and Zr, the micromorphology evolution was more stable than that of pure oxide 
for multiple cycles, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy results revealed that the higher surface oxygen was the 
primary reason for the superior performance facilitated by Cu and Zr. The Cu- and Zr-modified cobalt oxides 
were the potential candidates for thermochemical energy storage.   
1. Introduction 
Owing to its cleanliness, abundance, and inexpensiveness, solar en­
ergy is among the most promising renewable energy sources to over­
come the fossil energy crisis. However, solar power production does not 
generally fulfill the energy requirements. For instance, energy demand 
often peaks at night, while energy generation peaks at noon [1]. For 
continuous energy generation, the development of concentrated solar 
power (CSP), which can enable electricity generation during the day and 
night, with an efficient thermal energy storage system has attracted 
significant attention [2,3]. Thus far, sensible (SES), latent (LES), and 
thermochemical (TCES) energy storage systems are typically used en­
ergy storage systems [4]. SES, which depends on the specific heat ca­
pacity of the heat storage medium, has been used in a few commercial 
CSP plants [5]. LES releases and absorbs heat during the phase transition 
of the heat storage medium [6–8]. However, the operating temperatures 
of SES and LES are below ~400 and ~565 ◦C, respectively, and only 
cater to 1st and 2nd generation CSPs, respectively [9–11]. In recent 
decades, the heat storage capacity of CSP plants has continuously 
increased, and upcoming 3rd generation CSP plants can operate at 
temperatures over 700 ◦C [12,13]. It is challenging to meet the large 
tank volume and high operating temperature requirements of 3rd gen­
eration CSP plants using SES and LES systems owing to their low energy 
densities and operating temperatures [14,15]. TCES systems release and 
absorb heat by breaking and recombining chemical bonds at tempera­
tures over 700 ◦C and thus are considered to be more suitable for 3rd 
generation CSP plants. In addition, the energy density of the TCES heat 
storage medium is approximately 10 and 5 times higher than those of the 
SES and LES materials, respectively [16,17]. 
It is required to investigate materials with better performances for 
TCES applications. Recently, hydrides [18], hydroxides [19], carbonates 
[20], and redox oxides [21] have been attracting increasing attention for 
their application in gas–solid TCES systems [22]. Among them, redox 
oxides provide the possibility of an open-mode system [23]. Air can

Eq. (1) shows that the reversible redox reaction for the Co3O4/CoO 
system comprises two steps. The reduction reaction corresponds to the 
heat absorption process occurring at the heating step, in which oxygen is 
released, and high-valence metal elements are reduced to low-valence 
species. The oxidation reaction consists of the absorption of oxygen 
and corresponds to the heat release process occurring at the cooling step. 
The Co3O4/CoO system has a higher energy density, superior redox 
reversibility, and faster reaction kinetics than other redox couples. For 
example, the theoretical energy density of Co3O4/CoO is approximately 
844 kJ/kg whereas that of Mn2O3/Mn3O4 is merely 202 kJ/kg [26,27]. 
The average re-oxidation rate for Co3O4/CoO is approximately 100–300 
μmol/min/g whereas that of Mn2O3/Mn3O4 is approximately 0–20 
μmol/min/g [26,28,29]. However, the primary drawbacks of the 
Co3O4/CoO system are that cobalt is toxic and the 32nd rarest element 
occurring in the crust of the earth, and thus is more expensive and 
harmful than Mn. However, the Co3O4/CoO system remains worthy of 
investigation owing to its favorable TCES performance. 
The long-term cycle performance is essential to the Co3O4/CoO 
system integrated into CSP. However, the Tamman temperature for 
cobalt oxides is approximately 314 ◦C, which is significantly lower than 
its redox operating temperature [30]. Cobalt oxides are prone to sin­
tering over long-term redox cycles. The two primary processes occurring 
in sintering are particle coarsening and densification [31–33]. For redox 
couples, the oxidation reaction depends on oxygen diffusion, while the 
reduction reaction is determined by heat transfer [34]. Owing to sin­
tering, the oxygen diffusion path to the bulk material increases, conse­
quently resulting in a considerable drop in re-oxidation activity. In this 
regard, the key to improving the performance of cobalt oxides in TCES 
systems is enhancing its initial redox performance and promoting sin­
tering resistance [22,26]. From a thermodynamic point of view, it has 
been demonstrated that doping a secondary metal element in Co3O4 is a 
prospective method for increasing energy density and redox activity. 
Furthermore, to retain the redox performance for long-term cycles, inert 
materials have been used to enhance the sintering resistance of cobalt 
oxides. 
The effects of the doping of cobalt oxides on their TCES performances 
as observed in previous studies are summarized in Table S1. As shown in 
the table, the spinel structure materials (e.g., Co3−xMnxO4, Co3−xNixO4, 
and Co3−xCuxO4) and a few mixed metal oxides (e.g., Co3O4 and CeO2, 
Co3O4, and Fe2O3) improved the initial redox performance. For 
Co3−xMnxO4, although Mn ions can be effectively substituted in the 
Co3O4 lattice, they slightly deteriorated reaction kinetics and shifted the 
redox process toward higher temperatures [35], which significantly 
decreased the operability of the oxide. For Co3−xNixO4, while Ni doping 
decreased the onset temperature, it reduced the reaction kinetics [36]. 
For Co3−xCuxO4, 10% Cu doping adversely affected the energy density 
and reaction reversibility, but a lower Cu doping was favorable as it 
enhanced the heat release and absorption capacity [37]. Moreover, for 
mixed metal oxides, it was observed that doping both CeO2 and Fe2O3 
into Co3O4 can enhance reaction kinetics, particularly the oxidation 
rate. However, it did not contribute to an increase in energy density of 
the TCES system [24,38]. On the other hand, adding inert supports 
prevents the particles from coarsening and reduces material sintering. 
Al2O3, SiC, and SiO2 are commonly used refractory materials owing to 
their high melting point [38,39]. Although the sintering resistance was 
slightly increased, the energy density reduced owing to the formation of 
CoAl2O4 and Co2SiO4. 
Previous studies have demonstrated that energy density, reaction 
reversibility, and kinetics are crucial properties [4,40–42]. However, 
neither the doping of second metal element in Co3O4 nor using inert 
supports concurrently promote these properties. In addition to the 
modifications mentioned above, the addition of Mg, La, Yb, and Zr as 
supports or promoters of catalyst materials has been explored across 
various fields to improve their amphoteric properties and thermal sta­
bility, which may subsequently lead to enhanced thermochemical per­
formance of Co3O4 [43–45]. Moreover, various studies have reported 
different effects of various elements on the thermochemical perfor­
mance of cobalt-based systems. These differences may have arisen from 
various experimental conditions or synthesis methods, consequently 
leading to different physical properties (e.g., chemical and physical 
compositions, specific surface area, and total pore volume). In this 
aspect, although a few dopants have been extensively studied previ­
ously, it remains unclear which dopant is optimal under identical test 
conditions, and an efficient framework is required to evaluate the dif­
ference in energy storage performance. 
Therefore, nine dopants, including four previously unreported novel 
options (i.e., Yb, Mg, La, and Zr), for Co3O4 were selected in this study. 
All the samples used in this study were synthesized via a hydrothermal 
method to form micro cubic morphologies [46]. In our previous work, a 
cubic structure exhibited the optimal activity compared with those of 
other morphologies [47]. In this study, the chemical modification of 
Co3O4 was based on the cubic micro-nano structure. After doping with 
different dopants, the phase composition was determined using X-ray 
diffraction (XRD), and under the same test condition, each sample was 
subjected to five redox cycles. The conversion rate, re-oxidation rate, 
reaction temperature, and energy density were evaluated on the basis of 
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry 
(DSC) curves. Moreover, to further investigate the optimal dopant, 
long-term redox cycles (100 cycles) were performed for pure Co3O4 as 
well as the Zr- and Cu-doped samples. To evaluate the energy storage 
performance of the materials, three standard evaluates (i.e., the average 
capacity loss, theoretical cycle number, and total energy density) were 
proposed. Moreover, the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images 
were investigated before and after subjecting the samples to the 
long-term redox cycles to gain insights into morphology evolution. The 
phase compositions of the samples were also determined after 5 and 100 
cycles. Finally, chemical states of the fresh and used samples were 
analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to study the 
reaction mechanism. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. The synthesis process of materials 
The detailed information of raw materials and characterization 
techniques was given in supporting information. In this work, the hy­
drothermal method was used to synthesize the nine samples doped 
second elements with the molar content of 15% and a pure cobalt oxide. 
0.0085 mol cobalt acetate tetrahydrate, 0.0015 mol dopant precursor, 
2.00 g triethanolamine, and 3.00 g CO(NH2)2 were dissolved in 100 mL 
of deionized water under continuous stirring. After that, the transparent 
solution was obtained. The solution was placed into a Teflon autoclave 
and kept at 180 ◦C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the 
product was separated by centrifugation and washed several times with 
ethanol and deionized water. Then, the obtained precipitate was dried in 
an oven at 60 ◦C for 24 h. Last, the resulting product was heated in a 
muffle furnace at a rate of 10 ◦C/min and calcined at 900 ◦C for 10 h. 
Finally, the ten samples were obtained by grinding. 
For all samples in present study, pure Co3O4 was also used to 
compare redox performance, which was denoted as Co100 and the 
modified samples were denoted as CoMe15 according to the element of 
dopants, such as CoYb15, CoSi15, CoCe15, CoMg15, CoLa15, CoZr15, 
CoCu15, CoAl15 and CoFe15, respectively.

2.2. Operation conditions 
A Netzsch STA 449 F5 instrument was used for thermogravimetric 
analysis (TGA). In TGA tests, each sample (about 20 mg) was subjected 
to redox cycles in an alumina (Al2O3) crucible of 85 μL. Each cycle 
consisted of two steps (heating and cooling): the heating temperature 
was from 700 ◦C to 1000 ◦C, and cooling temperature was from 1000 ◦C 
to 700 ◦C. In addition, both heating and cooling rates were 10 ◦C/min 
with an airflow of 100 mL/min (N2: 79 mL/min; O2: 21 mL/min). Ac­
cording to TGA curve, the conversion rate and average re-oxidation rate 
of materials were calculated. The energy density was gained by DSC 
curve. From long-term redox cycles and energy density, three evaluated 
standards were proposed. 
3. Result and discussion 
3.1. Characterization of Co-based materials 
The phase compositions of all the synthesized materials were 
determined using powder XRD patterns, which are shown in Fig. 1. All 
the PDF cards with the corresponding phases are shown in Table S2. For 
pure Co3O4 (Co100), the main 2θ diffraction peaks observed in the 
pattens are 19.0◦, 31.3◦, 36.9◦, 38.5◦, 44.8◦, 55.6◦, 59.3◦, and 65.2◦, 
which agreed well with standard pattern (PDF#78–1969). After doping 
with nine different dopants, to better distinguish the phase composition, 
all the materials were classified into two based on their phase 
composition: Type I and Type II. As shown in Fig. 1(a) and (b), CoYb15, 
CoSi15, CoCe15, CoMg15, and CoLa15 were classified as Type I, for 
which multiple phases are observed. Additional to the diffraction peaks 
of Co3O4, the secondary diffraction peaks can be observed in Fig. 1(a) 
and (b), and the secondary diffraction peaks belonged to Yb2O3, 
Co2(SiO4), CeO2, MgO, and LaCoO3, respectively. Moreover, as shown in 
Fig. 1(b), the main peaks of CoSi15 belonged to Co2SiO4. By adding Si to 
Co3O4, Co2SiO4 formation is in good agreement with the result of pre­
vious study [38]. Type II samples are shown in Fig. 1(c) and (d), and the 
single phase for the Type II samples is confirmed owing to the secondary 
diffraction peaks not being observed. However, overlapping peaks were 
distinctly observed for Type II samples in Fig. 1(d) at approximately 
36.9◦, indicating the presence of CoCo2O4. The space groups of CoCo2O4 
and Co3O4 were F-43m and Fd-3m, respectively. CoCo2O4 with Co3O4 
was isomeric and was synthesized via controlling the Co2+ concentra­
tion [48]. CoCo2O4 formation indicates that after doping, the ratio of 
Co3+ to Co2+ changed, which may result in a different redox activity. 
Additionally, noticeable peaks shifted for all Type II samples owing to 
the distinct radii of the elements incorporated in the Co crystal structure. 
The atomic radius is also shown in Table S3. Whereas, for CoFe15 and 
CoZr15, the peaks slightly shifted, which may be due to the low content 
of doped oxides, or iron and zirconium oxides were highly dispersed on 
the Co3O4 surface [43]. The dopants with larger atoms radius are not 
going to replace cobalt in the crystal structure, but crystalize as phase 
segregations. The dopants with lower atoms radius can well substitute 
Co atoms in Co3O4 crystal structure to form single-phase oxides.

3.3. Reoxidation reaction rate 
For each redox cycle, the predominant change occurred during the 
oxidation reaction. The re-oxidation rate decayed with cycling, which is 
ascribable to sintering. The reduction step was related to heat transfer, 
while the oxidation step depended on oxygen diffusion [34]. The pore 
structure and morphology is destroyed owing to sintering. Thus, the 
oxygen diffusion was resisted, and the re-oxidation rate was decreased. 
In contrast, sintering negligibly affected heat transfer. In this respect, 
the key to improving the material reaction kinetics was to enhance the 
re-oxidation rate [26]. In the TCES process, the re-oxidation rate expo­
nentially decreased at the initial cycling stage. In subsequent cycles, the 
re-oxidation rate slightly decreased. In this study, according to the TGA 
curves, the average reaction rate was calculated using Eq. (6): 
Vreoxidation = Δm
M⋅Δt (μmol / min / g)
(6)  
,where Δm is the value of the weight gain/loss for every cycle, Δt is the 
reduction or oxidation reaction time, and M is the molecular mass of 
oxygen (32 g/mol). 
As for the re-oxidation rate shown in Fig. 3, the re-oxidation rate of 
Co100 for the first cycle was approximately 275 μmol/min/g, and after 
the fifth cycle, the re-oxidation rate decreased to 190 μmol/min/g. A 
faster and more stable re-oxidation rate is preferred for the TCES pro­
cess. This was obtained via Zr and Cu doping. CoZr15 and CoCu15 
exhibited a higher re-oxidation rate than Co100, among which, CoCu15 
exhibited the highest oxidation rate (351 μmol/min/g), but after five 
cycles, its re-oxidation rate decreased to 316 μmol/min/g. Compared 
with CoCu15, although CoZr15 exhibited a lower re-oxidation rate than 
CoCu15, the re-oxidation rate of CoZr15 was more stable than that of 
CoCu15, which maintained its initial value (approximately 261 μmol/ 
min/g). For CoAl15, the oxidation rate was lower than that of Co100 and 
exhibited a similar trend to Co100 (240–154 μmol/min/g). The results 
for CoAl15 is attributable to the reaction between Al2O3 and CoO at a 
high temperature [38], which was a competing process for the 
re-oxidation of CoO. For the other samples, the re-oxidation rate was 
lower than those of pure cobalt oxides. 
The stable reoxidation rate change is also observed for CoSi15, 
CoFe15, CoYb15, CoCe15, CoLa15, and CoMg15. A more stable but 
lower re-oxidation rates for these materials indicated that Si-, Fe-, Yb-, 
Ce-, La-, and Mg-doping had the opposite effect on cobalt oxides re- 
oxidation, while the sintering resistance ability improved. Theoreti­
cally, a higher surface area was conducive to oxygen diffusion; thus, a 
higher re-oxidation rate can be obtained, but in this study, the re- 
oxidation rate shown in Fig. 3 was not in accordance with the BET 
specific surface areas listed in Table 2. For instance, as shown in Fig. 3 
and Table 1, the special surface area of CoSi15 was the highest (31.35 
m2/g). However, its oxidation rate was considerably lower than those of 
CoCu15 and CoZr15, indicating that the chemical effect played a more 
important role than physical effects in the re-oxidation process. To 
summarize, the reoxidation performance of all samples followed the 
descending order: CoZr15 > CoCu15 > Co100 > CoAl15 > CoLa15 >
CoFe15 > CoYb15 > CoCe15 > CoSi15 > CoMg15. 
3.4. Energy density 
The endothermic reaction enthalpy based on Eq. (1) was defined as 
the energy density for TCES [16], shown in Fig. 4. The enthalpy was 
obtained by integrating DSC curves, and the DSC curves for all samples 
are shown in Fig. S4. Notably, from 1953 to 2012, numerous researchers 
investigated the theoretical enthalpy for Co3O4, and the theoretical 
enthalpy ranged from 552.74 to 858.01 kJ/kg [50]. From TGA–DSC to 
packed bed, the heat ranged from 293 to 600 kJ/kg in the TCES system 
[27,42]. The considerably lower enthalpy (244–551 kJ/kg) was caused 
by the Co3+ spin-state change, which was not measurable using 
DSC–TGA. Thus, in this study, the energy density was used to compare 
the relative enthalpies before and after doping, which does not entirely 
reflect the actual energy density values. 
As shown in Fig. 4, the energy densities for the 2nd–5th cycles 
exhibited similar values, whereas the first cycle exhibited a different 
result owing to the incomplete reaction between the dopants and cobalt 
oxides. That is, during the synthesis process of the samples at 900 ◦C, the 
reaction between the dopants and cobalt oxides was incomplete. 
Meanwhile, for the first redox cycle, the highest temperature was 
approximately 1000 ◦C, which was significantly higher than the syn­
thesis temperature. Moreover, it has been reported that the irreversible 
loss occurring during heating for first cycle because of the impurities. 
Such impurities were not observed in the XRD patterns [51]. Thus, 
additional to the heat flow of the redox reaction, extra heat flow could 
cause different energy densities. In this aspect, the second redox cycle is 
generally considered as the initial stage of the charge/discharge cycles. 
As for the 2nd–5th redox cycles shown in Fig. 4, CoSi15 and CoLa15 
exhibited the lowest energy density (approximately 190 and 245 kJ/kg), 
owing to the formation of the inert materials, Co2(SiO4) and LaCoO3. For 
CoCe15, the energy density was approximately 260 kJ/kg, indicating 
that 15% CeO2 adversely affected the energy density in this study. 
Regarding CoMg15, MgO could cause an incomplete conversion, which 
is observed in Fig. S3. The lower energy density is ascribable to the 
incomplete reaction at the temperature range of 700 ◦C–1000 ◦C. As for 
CoAl15 and CoYb15, the energy densities remained lower than that of 
pure cobalt oxides (approximately 358.8 kJ/kg). Notably, although the

3.5. Onset reaction temperature 
The influence of doping different dopants at the reduction and 
oxidation onset temperatures was analyzed. The TGA results shown in 
Fig. 5 show the redox onset temperature for the second cycle. During the 
heat charging and discharging process, higher reaction temperatures 
represent greater thermal grades. Because heat released at higher tem­
peratures is greater than that under lower temperatures; thus, for the 
temperature range (700 ◦C-1000 ◦C) in this study, a higher onset reac­
tion temperature was preferred. In addition, the onset temperature for 
oxidation was generally lower than that for reduction. However, owing 
to the overheating and undercooling phenomena required for achieving 
the necessary driving force to complete the reduction and oxidation 
reactions, the reduction reaction did not proceed entirely, and the 
oxidation reaction initiated so that the onset temperature for oxidation 
could be higher than that of reduction [22,52]. As shown in Fig. 5, the 
samples predominantly exhibited similar behaviors. The reduction re­
action initiated at approximately 910 ◦C except for CoCu15 and 
CoMg15, whose onset temperature was approximately 880 ◦C. A similar 
onset reduction temperature as those of the synthesized materials was 
ascribavle to the similar heat conduction characteristics, because heat 
transfer determined the reduction step. The result of the reduction onset 
temperature experiment indicated that after doping with 15% Yb, Si, Ce, 
La, Zr, Al, and Fe had little effect on the Co3O4 heat conduction char­
acteristics. However, CoCu15 and CoMg15 exhibited a lower onset 
reduction temperature. The lower onset temperature for reduction in­
dicates that they can be used in other fields, such as the utilization of 
industrial waste heat. Contrastingly, the oxidation onset temperature for 
pure cobalt oxide was approximately 900 ◦C, close to its reduction onset 
temperature, similar to CoCe15. After doping with Yb, Si, La, Zr, and Cu, 
the oxidation onset temperature was lower than that of pure Co3O4, and 
they exhibited a similar onset temperature. Moreover, similar results 
were observed for CoAl15. Notably, CoMg15 exhibited a different onset 
temperature, which was significantly lower than other samples. The 
onset temperature for reduction and oxidation was 797.7 and 784.6 ◦C, 
respectively. The decreasing order of the samples based on their onset 
temperatures is as follows: CoAl15 > CoFe15 > Co100 = CoCe15 >
CoCu15 > CoZr15 > CoLa15 > CoLa15 > CoSi15 > CoYb15.

charge/discharge cycles. Long-term cyclability is also an essential 
property. Therefore, Co100, CoZr15, and CoCu15 were subjected to a 
more extreme cycling experiment (up to 100 cycles) to test its stability.

higher surface area and pore volume [47]. The conversion rate for 100 
redox cycles is shown in Fig. 6. The conversion rate of Co100 exhibited a 
moderately linear decrease, and the slope decreased from 1.00 to 0.92.

3.8. The evolution of physicochemical properties for redox cycles 
The influence of Zr- and Cu-doping on the morphology was analyzed 
utilizing SEM-EDS. Fig. 8 shows SEM-EDS micrographs of the synthe­
sized pure cobalt, Co–Zr, and Co–Cu oxides. As shown in Fig. 8, the 
morphology of the materials was a cubic structure and the length of each 
cubic particle was approximately 5–8 um [46]. The final powder sam­
ples were obtained via calcining their precursors (CoCO3). After 
calcining, the porous structure was formed; thus, they possessed a higher 
surface area and pore volume. According to the SEM-EDS mapping 
result, the elements were observed to be homogeneously distributed on 
particle surfaces as shown in Fig. 8, demonstrating the effectiveness of 
mixing process. 
In addition, the SEM images of the pristine material as well as the 
materials after the 5th and 100th cycles were analyzed to obtain the 
morphology evolution information for the redox cycles. The relatively 
high-resolution SEM images are shown in Fig. 9, and the different res­
olution images are also shown in Fig. S8 to Fig. S16. As shown in Fig. S8 
to Fig. S16, all the samples exhibit a cubic structure. The aggregates of 
the irregular particles formed the materials with a cubic structure. 
Additionally, the surface of each sample was different. The Co100 (Fig. 9 
(a)) surface was denser than those of CoZr15 (Fig. 9(d)) and CoCu15 
(Fig. 9(g)). After the five cycles, the particle sizes of the three samples 
became coarser than those of the fresh samples. After 100 cycles, the 
particles became significantly coarser and denser. 
The cubic structure of the particles was destroyed owing to sintering 
after five cycles, as shown in Fig. S15. After 100 cycles, the particles 
coarsened, and densification significantly increased. Our previous work 
demonstrated that the cubic structure was retained after five cycles, 
because the calcination temperature during the synthesis process was 
only 500 ◦C [47]. However, in this study, the calcination temperature 
was 900 ◦C, which was significantly higher. Nonetheless, for CoZr15 
(Fig. S9) and CoCu15 (Fig. S12), the cubic structure was retained after 
five cycles. But after 100 cycles (Fig. S10 and Fig. S13), the cubic 
structure was destroyed. This phenomenon indicated that Zr- and Cu- 
doping enhanced the capacity of sintering resistance, and a more stable 
redox cycle performance was obtained, because, for Zr doping, presence 
of unreacted Zr particles is observed in the SEM micrographs (Fig. 9(d), 
Fig. S8, Fig. S9, and Fig. S10) but the Zr particles are not observed in the 
XRD patterns. From the SEM images for CoZr15 in Fig. 9(a), (b)), and 
Fig. 9(c), the Zr particle sizes increased with cycling, in which case, the 
superior performance for CoZr15 in long-term cyclability might be 
owing to the presence of Zr particles, which were inertia materials and 
could effectively reduce material sintering. Moreover, for Cu doping, 
though the Taman temperature of CuO was considerabley low, based on

To gain more insights into the effect of dopants on redox cycles 
performance, the change in chemical states for Co and O on the surfaces 
before and after five cycles were investigated using XPS. Fig. 11(a) 
shows the XPS profiles of Co 2p in Co100, CoZr15, and CoCu15. The two 
main peaks at 795.1 and 779.9 eV observed in Co 2p for the pure cobalt 
oxides spectra corresponded to the Co 2p1/2 and Co 2P3/2 spin-orbital 
peaks of Co3O4 spinel [43]. Two weak satellite peaks were also 
observed at 790.0 and 804.0 eV. Furthermore, the peaks at 781.6 and 
797.1 eV is attributable to Co2+, and the peaks at 780.0 and 795.2 eV is 
attributable to Co3+. The Zr- and Cu-doped materials had XPS spectra 
similar to those of pure cobalt oxides. 
According to Eq. (1), the Co3+ in Co3O4 transformed to Co2+ in CoO, 
or the Co2+ in CoO transformed to the Co3+ in Co3O4 was the primary 
process of the redox cycles. More Co3+ indicated a higher redox activity. 
By analyzing the XPS spectra using the Gaussian peak fitting method 
[54], the ratios of Co3+ and Co2+ as well as the O 1s core levels were 
determined, as shown in Fig. 11(a) and (b), respectively. 
As shown in Fig. 11(a), for fresh Co100, CoZr15, and CoCu15, the 
molar ratios of Co3+/Co2+ were 0.63, 0.67, and 0.66, respectively. The 
molar ratios for the three fresh materials exhibited a similar value, 
indicating that after Zr and Cu doping, the chemical states of the Co ions 
remained unchanged. However, after five redox cycles, the molar ratios 
of Co100, CoZr15, and CoCu15 were 0.72, 0.57, and 0.55, respectively. 
For pure cobalt oxides, the molar ratios increased after five cycles. 
Increasing molar ratios indicated a lower redox activity. In contrast, 
after five cycles, the molar ratios of CoZr15 and CoCu15 decreased. The 
change in phase composition resulted in a different behavior, occurring 
at high temperatures. After high-temperature redox cycles, the dopants 
better combined with the cobalt oxides, and a more stable phase 
composition was obtained. From this point of view, the Co3+ content 
increased via Zr and Cu doping to improve the redox activity.

Contrastingly, the O 1s core levels for Co100, CoZr15, and CoCu15 
were also obtained, as shown in Fig. 11(b). Three components for the O 
1s core levels were obtained by fitting the XPS profiles. The first peak, 
located at binding energies of 528.8–529 eV, belonged to metal–oxygen 
bonds, which is attributable to lattice oxygen (OI) [55]. The second peak 
at 531.2 eV (OII) was related to the electron-rich oxygen anions on the 
surface, which may include O2
−, O22−, and O−[56]. The slight difference 
in relative broadness for the third peak at 533.2 eV (OIII) may be as­
cribable to the physical adsorption of adventitious water and/or car­
bonates species on sample. The lattice oxygen (OI) is derived into 
surface-adsorbed oxygen species (OII). Owing to unsaturated bonds, the 
electrons for lattice oxygen and surface-adsorbed oxygen were more 
tightly bonded to their nuclei. This resulted in a shift to higher binding 
energies for this part of the oxygen species, thereby forming a new peak 
at a higher binding energy. Among three components for O 1s core 
levels, the surface oxygen was considered as the reactive oxygen, and it 
has been demonstrated that surface oxygen could decrease the reaction 
temperature and improve the redox capacities of materials [57]. This is 
because the existence of more surface oxygen indicated that the mobility 
of oxygen species was more robust, and its redox capacity was superior. 
In re-oxidation processes, the gaseous oxygen is always adsorbed on the 
solid surface, and then, the adsorbed surface oxygen reacts with the low 
valence metal elements. The mobility of the surface oxygen to lattice 
oxygen is significant for the oxidation process. 
As shown in Fig. 11(b), for pure cobalt oxides (Co100), the per­
centages of lattice, surface, and adsorption oxygen were 45.9%, 41.0%, 
and 13.1%, respectively. The percentage of lattice oxygen was slightly 
higher than surface oxygen, whereas for five cycles, the percentages of 
lattice, surface, and adsorption oxygen were 70.9%, 25.7%, and 3.4%, 
respectively. The percentage of surface oxygen significantly decreased. 
After high-temperature redox cycles, the materials were sintered; thus, 
the ability to adsorb oxygen to the material surface decreased, conse­
quently resulting in a lower conversion and re-oxidation rates. Notably, 
after Zr doping, the surface oxygen in CoZr15 decreased, which was 
significantly lower than that in pure cobalt oxides (Co100), revealing the 
lower redox activity for fresh CoZr15. For instance, as shown in Fig. 3(a), 
the re-oxidation rate of CoZr15 for the 1st cycle exhibited a lower value 
than that of Co100. However, for Cu-doped samples, the increased 
surface oxygen led to a higher re-oxidation rate for the 1st cycle. 
Furthermore, after five cycles, for CoZr15 and CoCu15, the surface ox­
ygen maintained at the initial value and was higher than that of Co100. 
Thus, a higher and more stable re-oxidation rate was obtained. 
In general, the higher surface oxygen was considered as the driving 
factor for the superior performances shown by CoZr15 and CoCu15. 
That is, after Zr and Cu doping, the chemical bonds for Co–O could be 
easily broken, and it would be more likely for lattice oxygen to diffuse 
into the surface-active oxygen sites [58]. 
4. Conclusion 
In this study, nine dopants, including Yb, Ce, Si, Mg, La, Zr, Cu, Fe, 
and Al, with a mole content of 15% were screened for optimizing dop­
ants for cobalt-based TCES materials. There were four unreported dop­
ants (Yb, Mg, La, and Zr), among which the Cu- and Zr-doped samples 
exhibited the superior performances with respect to conversion rate, re- 
oxidation rate, and energy density after five redox charging–discharging 
cycles. The result showed that the re-oxidation rate and energy density 
of the Cu-doped sample was 319.9 and 432.6 kJ/kg, respectively, and 
that of the Zr-doped sample was 253.5 and 407.5 kJ/kg, which were 
lower than those of the Cu-doped sample. Then, for testing their cycla­
bilities, pure cobalt oxide, Cu-, and Zr-doped samples were subjected to 
100 cycles. The result indicated that the Zr exhibited the most favorable 
cyclability. After 100 cycles, for pure cobalt oxide, the conversion rate 
decreased to 0.921. The conversion rates of Cu- and Zr-doped samples 
were only from 1.00 to 0.981 and 0.993–0.983, respectively. Both Cu 
and Zr were considered as the optimal dopants. Finally, the valence state
