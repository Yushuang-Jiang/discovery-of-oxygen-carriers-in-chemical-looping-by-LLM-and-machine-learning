A B S T R A C T   
Chemical looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) and chemical looping air separation (CLAS) are novel and 
potentially promising processes for the combustion of solid fuels (e.g. biomass) for power generation with 
inherent CO2 capture. Redox-experiments at 850–950 ◦C confirmed that copper manganese spinel oxides are 
promising oxygen carriers for these processes, as they combine a relatively high O2 release capacity and fast O2 
release kinetics. Furthermore, this work presents a novel method to calculate the O2 partial pressure equilibrium 
and the heat of O2 release from observed rates of reaction. To demonstrate this method, oxygen carriers were 
prepared via mechanical mixing and co-precipitation with varying molar Cu:Mn ratios and synthesis conditions, 
thereby tuning material properties and the pore structure. The precursors and calcined materials were charac­
terised, and the crystalline phases were determined using X-ray diffraction. The insights from the post cycling 
analysis of the oxygen carriers and the experimentally obtained O2 release capacities were combined to elucidate 
the redox-reactions relevant for the two processes. It was found that the presence of a higher partial pressure of 
O2 during the O2 release results in the formation of different (perovskite-like) phases than those occurring during 
the decomposition in an O2-free environment. The oxygen carriers demonstrated excellent stability at CLOU and 
CLAS process conditions during extended redox cycling (100 cycles in a thermo-gravimetric analyser and 50 
cycles in a fluidised bed reactor), showing no significant loss of reactivity or O2 release capacity and a high 
resistance towards attrition and agglomeration. The degree of degradation after 100 cycles was in the order: 
temperature swing (CLAS) < O2 partial pressure swing (CLOU) < reduction with CH4 (chemical looping 
combustion).   
1. Introduction 
The development and wide-scale deployment of carbon capture and 
storage (CCS) technologies such as chemical looping combustion (CLC) 
is an essential and lowest-cost component of the portfolio of options for 
decarbonising the global economy and avoiding disastrous anthropo­
genic climate change [1]. The most commonly proposed chemical- 
looping reactor designs involve the continuous circulation of an oxy­
gen carrier material between two interconnected fluidised bed (or 
entrained flow) reactors – the air reactor and the fuel reactor [2]. 
Oxygen carrier containing transition metal oxides facilitate oxygen 
transport from the air reactor to the fuel reactor. In the fuel reactor, the 
oxygen from the oxygen carriers reacts with the fuel. For chemical 
looping combustion (and its variants – CLOU and CLAS), the transition 
metal (oxides) of the oxygen carriers’ active phases are typically selected 
to produce primarily CO2 and H2O during the combustion of the fuel at 
the conditions prevailing in the reactor (though for other chemical 
looping variants, such as chemical looping reforming, redox-pairs might 
be selected to facilitate only partial combustion of the fuel, thus pro­
ducing an off-gas stream that may contain large fractions of CO, CH4 and 
other hydrocarbons) [3]. Condensation of the water in the off-gas stream 
produces an almost pure CO2 stream for sequestration or utilization [4]. 
1.1. Chemical looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) 
When combusting solid fuels in a CLC process, the use of oxygen 
carrier materials with the ability to release gaseous O2 (e.g. those con­
taining Cu, Mn or Co oxides) is typically preferred, as this allows for a 
faster gas–solid reaction. This variant is known as chemical looping 
combustion with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) and is illustrated in Fig. 1a 
[5].

1.2. Chemical looping air separation (CLAS) with oxy-fuel combustion 
In chemical looping air separation (CLAS) the ability of the afore­
mentioned materials to release gaseous O2 is used to enrich CO2 with O2. 
In this case, only the reaction shown in R2 takes place in the reducer. 
The mixture of O2 and CO2 is then used in a downstream oxy-fuel 
combustion process, to combust a solid fuel to produce mainly CO2 
and water vapour (R3). The water is removed during cooling and 
compression of the flue gases, providing a near-pure, dense-phase 
stream of CO2 for transport and geological sequestration. In addition to 
electricity production, oxy-fuel CO2 capture has been piloted for 
capturing process emissions from carbon-intensive industries such as 
lime and cement [9], and iron and steel production [10]. Convention­
ally, cryogenic air separation (instead of CLAS) is suggested for the 
supply of O2. However, the high energy requirements of this air sepa­
ration technology (resulting in an energy penalty of about 9%-points on 
the thermal efficiency of the electricity production) [11] is a major 
barrier to commercialisation. CLAS has been proposed as a more effi­
cient alternative to conventional cryogenic air separation [12]. The 
combination of the CLAS and the oxy-fuel process is illustrated in Fig. 1b 
[12]. A synergetic advantage of combining the processes is that the CO2- 
rich stream from the oxy-fuel combustor can be used as the fluidisation 
medium for the oxygen carriers in the CLAS process. Furthermore, the 
CLAS process is especially promising when considering the processing of 
solid fuels that are associated with detrimental interactions involving 
solid fuel-derived contaminants such as ash, tar and sulphur-containing 
gases, as the direct contact between the oxygen carriers and the fuel is 
avoided. This would contribute to improved oxygen carrier lifetimes 
compared with an analogous CLOU process. As the oxygen carrier 
reduction (O2 release) and the combustion of the fuel takes place in two 
separate reactors, each can be operated at their individual optimal 
conditions: The reducer would be operated at temperatures well below 
the melting point of the metal oxides, but yet sufficiently high to achieve 
acceptable rates of O2 release (e.g. > 990 ◦C for the redox pair CuO/ 
Cu2O) [12]. The oxy-fuel combustor can be operated at temperatures 
optimal for the combustion (e.g. with an adiabatic flame temperature of 
ca. 1550 ◦C at 28 vol% O2/CO2, which would match the flame charac­
teristics of the combustion of fuel in air) [13]. However, the additional 
capital expenses for a third reactor and the higher operating tempera­
tures required for the O2 uncoupling are disadvantages of the process in 
comparison to CLOU [14]. 
For efficient oxy-fuel combustion, a stream of 25–35 vol% O2 in CO2 
is required [13,15–17]. Although the Cu-Mn oxides would allow 
uncoupling of O2 at this volume fraction (above the corresponding O2 
equilibrium temperatures of 960–974 ◦C), it is not advantageous from a 
heat integration standpoint to operate the reducer and oxidiser with 
such a large temperature difference [18]. 
Using steam as an inert dilutant inside the reducer (injected together 
with the CO2) would allow operation at lower temperatures while 
achieving the desired O2 concentration after downstream condensation 
of the steam. 
A dilution of the CO2 with steam in a molar steam-to-CO2 ratio of 2:1 
and 10 vol% O2 at the reducer outlet (the volume fraction of O2 inside 
the reactor would be identical if it was well-mixed) would increase to ca. 
30 vol% O2 after condensation. We therefore used a 10 vol% O2/CO2 
mixture for the CLAS relevant O2 release experiments at 850–950 ◦C in 
this work. It shall be mentioned that experiments with a decomposition 
under pure CO2 (and pO2 ~ zero) were not carried out. During a 
decomposition of oxides in pure CO2 (instead of O2/CO2) the equilib­
rium is shifted towards the formation of carbonates, which could hinder 
the release of O2. However, for Cu and Mn oxides, carbonates are not 
thermodynamically stable in the studied temperature, carbonate for­
mation is thus unlikely for the materials in this work, and it is also 
(arguably) impossible to operate the reducer of the CLAS process with

1.3. Process efficiencies and economics 
The CLOU and the CLAS processes are also promising from an eco­
nomic standpoint, as their relatively low energy penalties for gas sepa­
ration allow them to have higher thermal efficiencies than most other 
CCS processes: The U.S. Department of Energy’s NETL estimated that a 
coal-fired in situ gasification CLC power plant (i.e. the CLOU process) has 
a lower heating value (LHV) efficiency and a levelised cost of electricity 
(LCOE) of 36.4% and 115 $2011/MWhe, respectively [19]. The same 
study estimate for a pulverised coal plant with a post combustion CO2 
capture using amine scrubbing a LHV efficiency and LCOE of 29.5% and 
137 $2011/MWhe, respectively [19]. 
For the CLAS process, a study assessed the economics for retrofitting 
eight existing coal-fired power plants in Australia with oxy-fuel com­
bustion systems [20]. The LCOE were calculated for each plant with 
three different configurations for the O2 production: (i) cryogenic air 
separation (113–131 AU$2014/MWh ~ 101–117 US$2014/MWh), chem­
ical looping air separation with reduction of the Cu-based oxygen car­
riers (ii) in steam (101–114 AU$2014/MWh ~ 91–103 US$2014/MWh) or 
(iii) in recycled-flue gas (92–103 AU$2014/MWh ~ 83–93 US$2014/ 
MWh) [19]. It has been shown that the CLC processes could reduce the 
cost of electricity systems thereby adding significant value to them [21]. 
The utilisation of biomass in CL-processes allows for net negative CO2 
emissions (a net removal of CO2 from the atmosphere) and could thus be 
vital for achieving the countries’ CO2 emission reduction targets [21]. 
Furthermore, niche applications tailored to local circumstances may be 
lucrative as well. For example, the novel ideas for CLAS processes 
include a system in which the enthalpy for the reducer is provided by 
concentrated solar power, while the oxy-fuel combustor utilises olive 
tree pruning. This process is particularly interesting, as it uses cheap and 
abundant energy carriers (solar energy and agricultural residues) for a 
net removal of CO2 from the atmosphere [22]. However, the investors’ 
perception of excessive risks, costs and inefficiencies of CLC and its 
variants continues to be a main barrier for commercialisation [21]. 
1.4. Oxygen carrier material selection 
For the CLOU and the CLAS processes, careful selection of the ma­
terials and the design of the synthesis procedures, for the oxygen carriers 
is essential for optimal process performance [23]. Conventional methods 
for preparing oxygen carrier materials include the co-precipitation of 
metal salts [24,25], impregnation of an active metal on an inert support 
material [26,27] and the mechanical mixing of metal oxide powders 
[25]. 
The CuO/Cu2O pair is the most widely investigated metal oxide 
system for the CLOU and CLAS processes owing to the materials fast 
rates of the redox reaction at relevant temperatures (ca. 800–900 ◦C for 
the CLOU [28–30] process, and 850–1050 ◦C for the CLAS process [12]) 
and a high theoretical O2 release capacity (10.1 wt%).[12,31–33] In 
comparison, the redox-pair Fe2O3/Fe3O4, which has widely been

Mn2O3/Mn3O4 has also been considered for CLOU and CLAS appli­

The use of Cu-Mn oxides combines the advantages of both materials. 
These materials have been tested for their chemical looping perfor­
mance with gaseous fuels [35,39], such as CH4 [35] and syngas [39], 
and with solid fuels [6–8,35–41], such as pine sawdust [7], almond 
shells [7], olive stones [7], lignite coal [6], sub-bituminous coal [6–8], 
bituminous coal [36,37] (and its pyrolytically obtained char [37]), 
wood char [40] and charcoal [41]. The authors used for these experi­
ments a fixed bed reactor [35], a 1.5 kWth continuous CLC rig [6–8] 
(consisting of two interconnected fluidised bed reactors), or a batch 
fluidised bed reactor [36,37,40,41]. A high crushing strength, a low 
attrition rate and stable oxidation and O2 release rates have been re­
ported [8,36]. 
However, to our best knowledge, Cu-Mn oxides have not yet been 
investigated as oxygen carriers for the CLAS process. In this study, we 
compare the performance of Cu-Mn oxides during experiments at CLOU 
conditions with those at CLAS conditions. We elucidate the relevant 
reactions and present as well as apply a novel approach for estimating 
the equilibrium O2 partial pressure and heat of reaction (which can also 
be used for other combined metal oxide systems). The oxygen carriers 
were prepared by an optimised co-precipitation procedure to achieve a 
high degree of Cu and Mn dispersion. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Preparation of the oxygen carriers via co-precipitation 
For the co-precipitation of the oxygen carrier precursors, Cu 
(NO3)2⋅2.5H2O (99.99%, Sigma) and Mn(NO2)3⋅4H2O (97.5%, Sigma) 
were weighed out in the desired molar Cu:Mn ratios and dissolved in de- 
ionised water (DI water) to obtain 200 cm3 solutions with a total ion 
concentration of 1 M. Alkaline solutions with [Na+] = 2 M were pre­
pared by dissolving Na2CO3 (99.6%, Acros) or mixtures of Na2CO3 and 
NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich) in DI water for controlling the pH value during 
the co-precipitation. 
A co-precipitation rig (illustrated in Fig. S3 in the ESM†) was used for 
the synthesis: After priming the reaction vessel with a small amount of 
DI water (30 ± 1 cm3), Masterflex L/S (Cole-Parmer) pumps were used 
to pump the acidic and the alkaline solutions into the reaction vessel. 
The liquid phase in the reaction vessel was continuously stirred 
(300 rpm) by a magnetic stirrer, and the temperature was kept constant 
at 25 ± 1 ◦C. The flow rate of the acidic solution (containing the metal 
nitrates) was set to 10 cm3 min−1, while the flow rate of the alkaline 
solution was controlled by a programme written on VEE (Agilent) to 
maintain the target pH value (±0.2). After complete mixing of the so­
lutions, the mixture was allowed to age for 30 min without further 
stirring. The precipitate was then washed in DI water, which was then 
separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 2 min. This step was 
repeated until the measured conductivity of the liquid phase was σ < 50 
µS. The precipitate was dried overnight at 60 ◦C in a well-ventilated 
oven and then calcined for 2 h at 900 ◦C in an airflow of 16.67 cm3 
s−1 at ca. 20 ◦C and 101.325 kPa, i.e. at normal temperature and pressure 
(NTP). For the calcination, a heating rate of 15 ◦C min−1 was used. 
The first series of samples was prepared with varying molar Cu:Mn 
ratios (i.e. 1:2, 1:1, and 2:1) using only Na2CO3 as the co-precipitation 
agent. These samples were designated 1Cu-2Mn-CP, 1Cu-1Mn-CP and 
2Cu-1Mn-CP, respectively. A second series of samples was prepared 
using a molar Cu-Mn ratio of 1:1 and [Na+] in the alkaline solution of 
2 M, but partially substituting the Na2CO3 with NaOH. The samples in 
which the Na+ was contributed from Na2CO3 and NaOH in molar ratios 
of 1:2 and 2:1 were named 1Cu-1Mn-CP-H and 1Cu-1Mn-CP-HH, 
respectively. Materials with only NaOH in the alkaline solution were 
not co-precipitated, as no pH value at NTP allowed co-precipitation of a 
solid manganese species without simultaneous formation of CuO (which 
is undesirable as it inhibits the formation of Cu-Mn oxides during 
calcination as later shown). The theoretical molar fractions of the metal 
species during co-precipitation were calculated based on their reaction 
enthalpies (the equilibrium plots are presented in the ESM† as Fig. S1 
and Fig. S2). This calculation was used to select a pH value, which was as 
high as possible, but still within the pH range to precipitate the desired 
product. We have also prepared numerous materials at a lower pH value 
and report their material characterisation results in the ESM†. Further­
more, a batch of mechanically mixed oxygen carrier (1Cu-1Mn-MM) was 
prepared as a benchmark: This was done by mixing 0.2 mol of CuO and 
0.2 mol of MnO with 30 cm3 of DI water. The slurry was loaded into a 
planetary ball mill (PM 400, RETSCH) together with steel balls and 
milled for 6 h at 250 rpm. X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF) 
showed no traces of iron, confirming that no contamination of attrited 
fines from the steel balls had occurred. The samples were dried for 12 h 
at 60 ◦C in a well-ventilated oven and then calcined for 6 h at 950 ◦C 
under air (16.67 cm3 s−1 at NTP) using a heating rate of 15 ◦C min−1. 
Compared to the co-precipitated oxygen carriers, a longer calcination 
time (6 h instead of 2 h) and a higher temperature (950 ◦C instead of 
900 ◦C) was used for the calcination of the mechanically mixed oxygen 
carriers to obtain a comparable conversion to the bimetallic copper 
manganese oxide. The freshly calcined samples prepared via co- 
precipitation or mechanical mixing were carefully crushed with a 
mortar and pestle and then sieved into the desired size fractions. 
2.2. Characterisation of the oxygen carriers 
N2 adsorption analysis at 77 K was carried out in a Micromeritics 
ASAP 2000 gas adsorption analyser. The data were used to calculate the 
specific surface area and the cumulative pore volume according to the 
BET method [42] and BJH model [43], respectively. 
An AccuPyc II 1340 (Micromeritics) was used for He displacement 
pycnometry (He-pyc.) to measure the skeletal density, ρsk. Mercury 
intrusion porosimetry (MIP) was carried out in an AutoPore IV (Micro­
meritics) to obtain the cumulative intrusion, ΣVI, in the range between 
5 nm and 10 µm. The intrusion in larger pores than 10 µm was not 
considered, to avoid overstated results due to concave features in the 
external surface of the particles and inter-particle voids. The porosity, ε, 
and the particle density, ρp, were calculated iteratively from ρsk (from 
He-pyc.) and ΣVI (from MIP) by ε = ΣVI⋅ρp and ρp = (1- ε)⋅ρsk. 
X-ray fluorescence (XRF) was carried out in an Epsilon 3XLE spec­
trometer (PANalytical) to determine the molar Cu:Mn ratio of the 
calcined oxygen carriers. Cu-Mn oxides with five different molar Cu:Mn 
ratios prepared via mechanical mixing were used for the calibration of 
the XRF measurements. 
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using a LEO 
Gemini 1525 at 10 kV to visualise the surface morphology of the fresh 
and the redox-cycled particles. The particles were loaded onto an SEM 
stub using double-sided carbon tape and sputter-coated with gold for 
30 s at 20 mA to improve their conductivity (resulting in a ca. 5 nm gold 
film). SEM was also used to conduct electron-dispersive spectroscopy 
(EDS) to examine the ion dispersion by elemental mapping and point 
analysis. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out in an 
X’Pert PRO (PANalytical) spectrometer using Cu-Kα radiation with 
λ = 0.1541 nm at 40 kV and 20 mA. During all XRD experiments, a 2θ-

2.3. Measurement of the crushing strength 
The crushing strength of the oxygen carrier was measured using a 
50 N force gauge (Mark-10). The average crushing strength (σCS) was 
determined by averaging twelve subsequent measurements of the 
crushing of single oxygen carrier particles sieved into a diameter size 
range of 300–425 µm. A typical force-versus-time plot during the 
crushing of an oxygen carrier particle is shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM†. 
The value for the uncertainty of the average crushing strength within a 
95% confidence interval was taken as two times the standard deviation 
of the results from three of these experiments for each sample. In the 
ESM† we report also the standard deviation across all 36 measurements 
for each sample, which (in contrast to the uncertainty of the average 
crushing strength) also contains information about distribution of the 
strength of the individual particles. 
2.4. Thermo-gravimetric analysis 
A thermo-gravimetric analyser (TGA) model Q5000 (TA In­
struments) was used for thermo-gravimetric analysis of the oxygen 
carriers in the temperature range 850–950 ◦C. The set-up of the system is 
illustrated in the ESM† (Fig. S4). The experimental O2 release capacity 
(ORCexp), as well as the O2 release and oxidation rate, were determined 
by isothermal redox-cycling at 850 ◦C, and during temperature swing 
experiments between 850 ◦C and 950 ◦C. During all TGA experiments, 
oxygen carriers in a size range of 90 – 150 µm were used (low enough to 
ensure negligible intra-particle diffusion limitations). The total gas flow 
rate was kept constant at 200 cm3 min−1 at NTP. A platinum crucible (h: 
1.5 mm, d: 9.8 mm) was used to hold a single layer of oxygen carrier 
particles. The oxygen carriers were redox-cycled by temperature swing 
(T swing), O2 partial pressure swing (pO2 swing), and using CH4 as the 
reducing gas. The T swing experiments were carried out using 10 vol% 
O2, which was balanced with N2 during the oxidation at 850 ◦C. For the 
decomposition, the gas was switched to 10 vol% O2/CO2 and the tem­
perature was increased to 950 ◦C with a maximum heating rate of 
300 ◦C min−1. Additionally, T swing experiments with decomposition in 
O2/N2 (instead of O2/CO2) and oxidation in O2/CO2 (instead of O2/N2) 
were conducted to study the effect of substituting N2 with CO2 on the 
redox-behaviour of the oxygen carriers. 
The pO2 swing experiments were conducted isothermally at 850 ◦C 
using 10 vol% O2/N2 during the oxidation, and pure N2 during the 
decomposition. Since the focus of this article is the nature of the O2 
release, the temperatures were selected so that the O2 release during the 
two different O2-uncoupling modes (T swing and pO2 swing) occurred at 
practically the same temperature (850 ◦C). 
The experiments with CH4 were carried out at 700 ◦C using 5 vol% 
CH4/N2 during the reduction and 10 vol% O2/N2 during the oxidation. 
The lower temperature was selected, as the CH4-CLC process is typically 
operated at temperatures lower than those common for the CLOU pro­
cess, possibly providing here an advantage with regard to the oxygen 
carrier lifetime. 
The redox experiments were carried out twice: at a higher sample 
mass (70 ± 2 mg) which allowed determination of the crystalline phases 
of the cycled oxygen carriers by means of XRD (a significantly lower 
sample mass would have compromised the quality of the analysis) and 
for the reporting of O2 release rates at a lower sample mass (2 ± 0.6 mg), 
a compromise between maintaining a high signal-to-noise ratio while 
minimising the depletion or enrichment of O2 in the gas phase inside the 
TGA furnace during oxidation and decomposition, respectively. During 
the experiments for the determination of the O2 partial equilibrium 
pressure, the samples were oxidised in 10 vol% O2 and decomposed in a 
mixture of O2 and N2 with varying [O2]. The maximum rate of external 
mass transfer during the O2 release was determined by calculating the

corundum of MnCO3, Cu2CO3(OH)2 and CeO2 were used to calculate the

detailed in section 2.7 in the ESM†, revealed that only 12 ± 6 wt%,

and 2Cu-1Mn-CP, respectively, is crystalline. Though most of the peaks 
of malachite and MnCO3 overlap, it is likely that most of the crystalline 
material is MnCO3, owing to the absence of a reflection at 2θ = 17.4◦for 
malachite (c.f. Fig. S15 in the ESM†), and the decrease in the crystallinity 
of the samples with a decrease in its manganese content (c.f. Fig. S16 in 
the ESM†). These calculations indicate that the larger mass fraction of 
the samples consisted of amorphous Cu and Mn, which allows for a 
better dispersion of the two types of metal ions, and hence aids the 
formation of copper manganese oxide (as indicated by the XRD results).

resulted in an increase in precursors’ crystallinity as Cu precipitated 
predominantly as Cu2(OH)3⋅NO3 (JCPDS 00–003-0061). This was also 
correctly predicted by the equilibrium diagrams for the precipitated 
solution (ESM†, Fig. S1 and Fig. S2). Precursors with a high mass fraction 
of a crystalline phase containing only one of the metal species (like Cu 
hydroxide nitrate in the sample Cu-Mn-CP-HH) are not ideal, as the lack 
of mixing of Cu and Mn inhibits the formation of copper manganese 
oxide (as confirmed by a high mass fraction of single metal oxides 
detected by XRD in the calcined samples).

3.3. Determination of the O2 release rate and the oxygen partial pressure 
equilibrium function in the TGA 
A series of experiments was conducted following the procedure 
described in section 2.4 to determine the O2 equilibrium as a function of 
temperature for sample 1Cu-1Mn-CP. The sample was selected owing to 
the high Cu-Mn oxide mass fraction (almost unity) and the relatively 
high porosity, which minimises the impact of the intra-particle mass 
transfer resistance on the overall rate (further details and results of a 
kinetic screening are provided in ESM†, c.f. Fig. S21). During the ex­
periments at atmospheric pressure, the O2 volume fraction was set 0.05 – 
0.005 above the expected equilibrium O2 volume fraction for any given 
temperature. The maximum rates of the O2 release were plotted as a 
function of O2 partial pressure for a range of temperatures. Lines of best 
fit were constructed for each temperature series and extrapolated back 
to the x-axis (i.e. where the rate of O2 release is equal to zero); thus 
revealing the equilibrium O2 partial pressure for the oxygen carrier 
system. The measured rates of cycles 3 to 5 were averaged (the data of 
cycles 1 and 2 was discarded, as activation during initial cycles may 
vary). The maximum rate was determined and plotted against the partial 
O2 pressure. The proposed method was validated by comparing the 
measured equilibrium O2 partial pressure data for the well-known CuO 
system with the predicted values generated using the thermodynamic 
modelling software FactSage (Termfact and GGT-Technologies). This 
validation is illustrated in Fig. S5 in the ESM†. 
This method was then applied to sample 1Cu-1Mn-CP, as shown in 
Fig. 5. The maximum rates plotted in Fig. 5a are based on reaction rate 
profiles (rate against time), which are presented in Fig. S20 in the ESM†. 
The linear fit demonstrates that the O2 release reaction from the 
copper manganese oxides is of first order with respect to the difference 
of CO2 and CO2,eq. The x-axis intercept of the linear fit is the equilibrium 
pressure at a given temperature. Plotting those intercepts against tem­

3.4. Quantification of the external mass transfer during the O2 release 
experiments 
In the section above, the apparent rate of O2 release was plotted 
against the O2 partial pressure to obtain the equilibrium pressure 
through extrapolation to the x-axis (Fig. 5). It is only correct to use the 
apparent rate if there is either no external mass transfer resistance or the 
mass transfer affects each observed rate to the same extent (the latter is 
also true if the external mass transfer remains dominant across the entire 
condition range). Note that it was demonstrated earlier that the intra- 
particle diffusion resistance is negligible, otherwise this would have to 
be considered additionally. 
The external mass transfer in the TGA during the O2 release was 
calculated from equation (1) – (3) and 13. At 800 ◦C and 101.325 kPa, 
the value of pO2,eq is 1.82 kPa (yr,s = 0.018; c.f. equation (13) or Fig. 5b). 
As this condition the maximum molar flux of mass transfer, Nr, was 
calculated to be 1.19 × 10-6 mol m−2 s−1. Multiplying this value with the 
area of the crucible (Ac = 7.543 × 10-5 m−2) yields the maximum rate of 
mass transfer, which is equal to 8.9 × 10-8 mol s−1. The apparent molar 
flow rate of O2 release from the bed of the crucible measured in the TGA 
at this temperature is 8.7 × 10-8 mol s−1. The difference between the 
maximum rate of mass transfer and the apparent rate of O2 release is 2%, 
which is within the experimental error. Therefore, in this case, the 
apparent rate is almost equal to the mass transfer and the effectiveness 
factor is zero. This is also the case for all other conditions used in this 
study, as shown in Fig. 5c. 
This demonstrates that at low values for (pO2,eq–pO2) and typical CLAS 
conditions the rate of O2 release is approximately equal to the rate of 
external mass transfer – at least for copper manganese oxides and when 
using a fixed bed reactor / TGA. This in turn shows, that there is a need 
for novel reactor designs in which the rate of external mass transfer is 
enhanced by the removal of the produced O2, as this increases the 
concentration gradient between the particle surface and the bulk and 
thus the driving force of the gas phase diffusion (c.f. equation (1)). For 
example, a reactor, in which the oxygen carriers fall counter-current to 
the recycled flue gas might be very suitable, as it would result in the 
particles moving along a path of a declining O2 concentration, thereby 
replacing the gas around the particles with low O2 gas, thus enhancing 
the mass transfer of O2. Furthermore, it shows that TGA experiments for 
the determination of the intrinsic kinetics of (these) CLAS materials are 
not suitable. Finally, we have demonstrated that the procedure to

3.6. Long-term redox-cycling performance in the TGA and FBR 
The performance over a high number of redox-cycles was assessed in 
the TGA and the FBR. The tests in the TGA showed that the samples had 
a high thermo-chemical resistance over 100 cycles with no significant 
loss of reactivity or O2 release capacity, as shown in Fig. 6Aa. 
Additionally, the long-term cycling performance was tested in the 
fluidised bed reactor over 50 redox-cycles. The results are illustrated in 
Fig. 6Ab. In the FBR, the oxygen carrier particles are exposed to me­
chanical stresses owing to the collision with other particles and with the 
reactor wall in addition to the chemical stresses. The tests in the FBR 
demonstrated stable redox-reaction rates and oxygen release capacities. 
No fines were recovered from the elutriate filter and no agglomeration 
occurred. Further to pO2 swing redox-cycling, the oxygen carriers were 
also redox-cycled 100 times by T swing and with a reduction with CH4, 
for comparison. SE micrographs of the recovered materials are showing 
in Fig. 6B. 
The oxygen carriers recovered from T swing experiments appear 
spherical and dense. Contrarily, the oxygen carriers cycled by pO2 swing 
appear highly porous, and those redox-cycled with CH4 had dis­
integrated. This indicates that the chemical looping mode has a large 
effect on the evolution of the particle morphology, and the degree of 
degradation after the 100 cycles follows the order (from lowest to 
highest): T swing < pO2swing < redox-cycling with CH4. The change in 
mass of lattice-O2 between the oxidised and reduced state (i.e. the values 
of the O2 capacities) follows the same order (13.4 wt% for the reduction 
with CH4, c.f. Fig. S23 in the ESM†), and is possibly the main reason for 
the differences in the degree of degradation. Furthermore, the harsher 
conditions during the T swing cycling (i.e. a higher temperature during 
the O2 release) may have enhanced the sintering and thus resulted in a 
densification of the particle that is shown in Fig. 6Ba. This finding re­
sembles findings from calcium/carbonate looping (Ca-looping) 
research, where higher temperature and partial pressure of the gaseous 
reagent (i.e. CO2 in the Ca-looping process) was reported to influence the 
degree of densification of limestone particles [51,52]. Manovic et al. 
[51] demonstrated with data from N2 adsorption and MIP experiments 
of fresh and cycled limestone that a higher reactor temperature and a 
higher partial pressures of the gaseous reagent increases the rate of 
sintering, and thus the rate of densification of the particles over an 
increasing cycle number. Wu et al. [52] reported similar findings for 
limestone particles in the Ca-process. They observed a reduction in the 
normalised equivalent diameter and porosity with an increase in cycle 
number at high temperatures. These findings and the finding of this 
work confirm that when studying the degradation of particles in high 
temperature looping processes (such as the CLAS process) it is similarly 
of great importance to consider that the change of particle morphology 
may depend significantly on the cycling conditions, such as temperature 
and partial pressure of the reagents. 
Furthermore, the long-term cycling tests in the TGA and FBR 
demonstrate that the unsupported copper manganese oxide oxygen 
carriers can survive the thermo-chemical (TGA) and mechanical stresses 
(FBR) during repeated O2 release and re-oxidation, and thus are 
potentially suitable oxygen carrier materials for the CLAS process. 
Though, further attrition tests with gas velocities of an industrial-scale 
process, preferably at high temperatures (those relevant for the CLAS 
processes) and in a reactive environment would be required to deter­
mine if the rate of degradation is sufficiently low for these materials to 
be economically viable and environmentally benign in an industrial- 
scale CLAS process.

4. Conclusions 
We report the synthesis of Cu-Mn oxides via co-precipitation as ox­
ygen carriers for chemical looping processes and demonstrate how their 
properties (crushing strength, surface area and porosity) can be tailored 
by tuning the precursor composition and the precipitation conditions. 
We have then compared the oxygen carriers’ performance during con­
ditions relevant for the CLAS process with those relevant for the CLOU 
process: The O2 release capacities during redox-cycling at CLAS condi­
tions (decomposition by T swing in the presence of O2) were signifi­
cantly lower than those during CLOU conditions (decomposition by pO2 
swing in the absence of O2). The reason was elucidated by detailing the 
differences in the reaction pathways. This newly identified drawback for 
the CLAS process has significant implications for the economics and the 
design of this process. 
A method to determine the O2 equilibria and heat of reaction for the 
oxygen carriers (both important process design parameters) was devel­
oped, validated, applied and the results reported. Furthermore, long- 
term cycling experiments in the TGA and a fluidised bed were con­
ducted, during which the oxygen carriers showed stable O2 capacities, 
rapid reaction rates and a high resistance to chemical and mechanical 
stresses, making them promising candidates for oxygen carriers in the 
CLOU and CLAS process. 
The protocols applied in this study can be used in the future to assess 
the characteristic and equilibria of more complex metal oxide systems as 
oxygen carriers for the CLC processes. It allows the calculation of 
apparent equilibria and heat of reactions of complex multi-phase sys­
tems such as synthesis mixed metal oxides and ores.
