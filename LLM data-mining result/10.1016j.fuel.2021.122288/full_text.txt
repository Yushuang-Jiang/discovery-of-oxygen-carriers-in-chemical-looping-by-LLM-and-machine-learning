A B S T R A C T   
The use of mixed metal oxides as oxygen carriers for chemical looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) appli­
cations has shown great potential compared to single metal oxide-based oxygen carriers. In this work, the effects 
of adding WO3 to CuO/ZrO2 oxygen carriers on the oxygen capacity and rate indices for CLOU applications are 
investigated using nitrogen gas environment. Two synthesis methods were used to prepare the CuO-WO3/ZrO2 
oxygen carriers, namely the coimpregnation and sequential impregnation, to evaluate the role of the oxygen 
carriers’ preparation method. The resulting oxygen carriers were thoroughly characterized using XRD, ICP-OES, 
SEM-EDS and H2-TPR characterization techniques to evaluate the physicochemical properties of the mixed oxide 
oxygen carriers. The oxygen transport capacity was found to significantly increase for the WO3-modified oxygen 
carriers compared with the single metal oxide CuO/ZrO2 which was due to the formation of a Cu-W intermediate 
phases that was found to enhance the redox characteristics of the oxygen carriers. Moreover, the sample syn­
thesized via coimpregnation method displayed higher oxygen release capacity than the sequentially impregnated 
sample. This was ascribed to the formation a Cu-rich intermediate phase (Cu3WO6) possessing higher Cu/W ratio 
on the coimpregnated sample compared with a CuWO4-x phase with lower Cu/W ratio and larger crystallite 
structure formed on the sequentially impregnated oxygen carrier. The mixed metal oxide oxygen carriers also 
showed a stable redox performance which highlights the potential of WO3 as a modifier to Cu-based oxygen 
carriers to enhance the oxygen capacity and subsequently decrease the solids inventory. The proposed WO3- 
based promotors offer significant trait to synthesize Cu-based oxygen carriers with excellent properties for CLOU 
applications.   
1. Introduction 
Chemical-looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) is a CO2 capture 
technology in which the fuel is oxidized using oxygen provided by solid 
materials called oxygen carriers (OCs) without the need of direct contact 
with air [1]. The OCs are circulated between an air reactor and a fuel 
reactor to transport oxygen from air to fuel. Moreover, the OCs used in 
CLOU process should have the ability to release gaseous oxygen in the 
fuel reactor, so that the fuel can directly react with oxygen in the gas 
phase and form CO2 and H2O when completely combusted [2]. The 
reactions that can typically occur in CLOU fuel reactor are oxygen 
release and fuel complete combustion and can be represented by Re­
actions (R1) and (R2), respectively. While the oxidation of the reduced 
OC transferred to the air reactor can be represented by Reaction (R3) 
[1,2]. The reaction mechanism of CLOU is different from that of con­
ventional chemical looping combustion (CLC) in a sense that no direct 
reaction between the fuel and the OCs occurs, which is beneficial when 
using solid fuels since the slow gasification reaction can be avoided [3].

CLOU should be thermodynamically able to release the required oxygen 
for complete fuel combustion in the fuel reactor and to be oxidizable by 
air in the air reactor. CuO-based OCs are amongst the most promising 
OCs suggested for chemical looping applications due to their attractive 
properties [1]. Nonetheless, one of the main issues associated with the 
use of Cu-based OCs is the low melting point of Cu (1085 ◦C) which 
limits their use in CLC applications. However, under an inert atmosphere 
(N2) CuO is reduced to Cu2O which has relatively higher melting points 
compared with Cu. 
Previous studies [4–6] have demonstrated that Cu-based OCs can 
display high CLOU activities with complete combustion and high CO2 
capture efficiencies for different fuels. Also, literature review on OCs has 
demonstrated that addition of supports and modifiers possesing high 
Tammann temperatures has positive impacts on the sintering resistance 
of CuO-based oxygen carriers. For instance, the use of MgO, which has 
high Tammann temperature compared to SiO2, helped to constrain 
particle growth and inhibits agglomeration of CuO particles, which 
resulted in higher oxygen capacity of CuO/MgO OC [7]. In another 
investigation, TiO2, CuAl2O4, MgAl2O4 and ZrO2 were used to support 
CuO for nirogen (N2) CLOU, and it was found that ZrO2, which also has 
high Tamman temperature (1494 K), displayed the highest sintering 
resistance among the tested supports [8]. Gay´an et al. [9] studied several 
OCs for CLOU by varying metal oxide loadings, supports and preparation 
methods. Amongst the tested OCs, it was found that the sample with 40 
wt% CuO loading supported over ZrO2 has good CLOU properties, such 
as high reactivity, stability, sintering resistance, and fluidization 
characteristics. 
In order to further improve the properties of Cu-based OCs for CLOU 
applications, the use of combined metal oxides has also been proposed to 
benefit from the synergism between the individual metal oxides [10]. 
Cu-Mn mixed oxides were evaluated for CLOU using solid fuels which 
shown significantly improved redox properties with complete fuel 
combustion and efficient CO2 capture [11–13]. Moreover, Cu-Mn-Fe 
mixed oxides were also tested for CLOU, and the OCs prepared exhibi­
ted high reactivity and stability without agglomeration issues [14]. 
Additionally, Mn-Fe combined oxides were investigated by Azimi and 
co-workers [15], and it was concluded that a high oxygen release could 
be achieved during the reduction of bixbyite (Fe,Mn)2O3 phase into (Fe, 
Mn)3O4 spinel. This demonstrates the advantages of forming mixed 
metal oxide phases which can release oxygen upon their reduction. 
WO3 is another promising metal oxide with high Tammann tem­
perature and distinctive structural and physicochemical properties that 
can form intermediate phases with CuO and should be considered as a 
promising candidate for the preparation of combined metal oxides in 
CLOU [16–18]. However, its low reactivity and poor reducibility 
obstructed its use in CLC and CLOU applications. Previous H2-reduction 
investigations were performed on Cu-W oxide mixtures [19,20], and it 
was found that the reduction of WO3 can occur at relatively lower 
temperatures in the presence of CuO due to the formation of Cu-W in­
termediate phases. Moreover, the samples prepared with higher CuO 
content resulted in more homogeneous particels with smaller sizes [20]. 
The disticntive structural properties and thermal stability of WO3, makes 
it a promising modifier for CuO-based oxygen carriers in CLOU appli­
cations. However, to the best of our knowledge there is no studies re­
ported to the date this investigation is performed for the preparation and 
evaluation of CuO-WO3 mixed metal oxide in the context of chemical 
looping applications. This study reveals the promotional effects of WO3 
to CuO/ZrO2 Oxygen carriers in CLOU applications to enhance the redox 
cyclic performance and oxygen carrying capacity. 
In this work, we studied the preparation of WO3 modifed CuO/ZrO2 
OCs using coimpregnation and sequential impregnation and assess their 
performance in CLOU applicaions using N2. Moroever, the propsoed 
study eluicdate how the addition of WO3 could influence the structuaral 
properties of the OCs and their redox properties, oxygen transport ca­
pacity, and kinetics. Detailed charcterizations of the prepared samples 
are perfromed to get an understanding of the effects of the structural 
properties on the CLOU perfromance. This study offers great insights 
into the promotional effects of WO3 on improving the oxygen uncou­
pling characteristics of CuO-based materials. The proposed promotional 
scheme using WO3 will help addressing a major obstacle limiting the 
large-scale use of Cu-based oxygen carriers In CLOU process, which is 
the low oxygen carrying capacity. This is significantly important from 
process performance point of view in large-scale apllications as it can 
considerably decrease the solid make-up and circulation rate between 
the two reactors. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
Zirconium oxide (ZrO2), copper(II) nitrate trihydrate (Cu 
(NO3)2⋅3H2O) and ammonium metatungstate ((NH4)6(H2W12O40)) were 
obtained from Sigma Aldrich, Canada. 
2.2. Preparation of the oxygen carriers 
Oxygen carriers used in this study were prepared using impregnation 
methods. The Cu-based single metal oxide OC was prepared by dis­
solving zirconium oxide in distilled water, followed by the dissolution of 
the active metal precursor (Cu(NO3)2⋅3H2O). The solution was mixed for 
6 h, after which heating to 80 ◦C was performed to evaporate the water. 
The obtained powder was further dried at 120 ◦C for 12 h, and calcined 
at 800 ◦C for 8 h with 2 ◦C/ minute heating rate. The final sample named 
CuO(40%)/ZrO2. For the coimpregnated dual metal oxide oxygen car­
rier (CuO(40%)-WO3(20%)/ZrO2), the required metal precursors 
amounts of (Cu(NO3)2⋅3H2O) and ((NH4)6(H2W12O40)) were added to 
the dissolved zirconia. Similar steps were followed to obtain final

Sequential impregnation was prepared by firstly impregnating WO3 over 
ZrO2 to form WO3(33.33%)/ZrO2, by following the same steps stated 
above. WO3(33.33%)/ZrO2 was used as a support to be impregnated 
with CuO. Similar impregantion steps were followed again, to end with 
an oxygen carrier with a nominal compisition of 40% CuO, 20% WO3 
and 40% ZrO2. 
2.3. Characterizations 
Analysis of metallic elements incorporated within the OCs was per­
formed using Inductively Coupled Plasma- Optical Emission Spectros­
copy (ICP-OES) using Agilent’s 5110 ICP-OES. Before analysis, the 
samples were digested using Anton Paar’s Multiwave 5000 microwave 
digestion system by dissolving 50 mg of the OCs in 5 ml of total acid (1 
ml HNO3, 3 ml HCL and 1 ml HF) for 30 min using 900 W power. After 
digestion, samples and standards for metals were diluted to 1–100 ppm 
range before introduction to analysis system. 
X-ray Powder Diffraction (XRD) was performed using Rigaku X-ray 
Diffractometer (Multiflux 2 kW Copper target), using a Cu K-alpha ra­
diation with a wavelength of 1.5406 Å source. XRD was performed for 
both oxidized and reduced OCs. The analysis was performed using two- 
theta from 5 to 80◦, 0.02-degree step, and scanning rate of 2◦/minute. 
The equipment was operated at 40 kV voltage and 40 mA current. XRD 
patterns were obtained and analyzed using Jade XRD MDI library. 
Scanning Electron Microscopy-Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectros­
copy (SEM-EDS) was conducted on FEI Quanta 250 FEG microscope 
fitted with backscattered electron detector (BSE) for BSE imaging and 
Bruker Quantax 5030 Silicon Drift detector for EDS mapping. 
Fourier Transform Infrared Spectra (FTIR) were collected using 
PerkinElmer Spectrum Two FTIR Spectrometer. The samples were pre­
pared for analysis using KBr pellet technique. The analysis was per­
formed at room temperature with 450–4000 cm−1 spectral range using

2.4. Cyclic Reduction-Oxidation (Redox) Experiments and Stability Tests 
Thermogravimetric Analyzer (PerkinElmer 8000 TGA) was used to 
perform CLOU experiments using 10 mg of the samples. The TGA tem­
perature program started by heating the chamber to 1000 ◦C with 20 ◦C 
per minute heating rate under 27 ml/minute air purge flow rate and 40 
ml/minute system N2 flow to ensure the samples are still oxidized at the 
test temperature. The temperature was kept at 1000 ◦C under air flow for 
3 min before changing the purge gas into N2 for 30 min which resulted in 
reduction of the tested samples. Purge gas was changed back to air and 
temperature was lowered to 800 ◦C with 20 ◦C/minute rate, and the 
samples were kept at 800 ◦C for 10 min before heating again to 1000 ◦C 
with the same heating rate to start the samples reduction afterwards 
using N2 for the following cycle. Ten CLOU cycles were performed for 
each sample to test the OCs’ CLOU performance and redox ability. 
Moreover, to investigate the phases present in the reduced OCs, 
0.3–0.4 g of each sample was loaded in a 0.5-inch quarts fixed bed 
reactor and reduced using 40 ml/minute N2. The reactor temperature 
was raised from room temperature to 1000 ◦C under air. Then, the inlet 
gas was changed to N2 to perform reduction of the samples for 30 min at 
1000 ◦C, after which the reactor was cooled down under N2 flow to room 
temperature and the samples were collected to perform XRD and FTIR 
analyses.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Inductively Coupled Plasma-Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP- 
OES) Elemental Analysis 
The actual composition of metals in the OCs was quantified using 
ICP-OES and the actual composition of the metal oxides are shown in 
Table 1. The equipment provided information based on metal 
composition that is assumed to be completely converted into their 
respective metal oxides without incorporation of other intermediate 
phases in their structure. This assumption was made here in order to 
compare nominal compositions of the OCs with actual loadings. It can 
obviously be seen that the actual CuO compositions in the OCs are close 
to their nominal values (within 3.5% maximum of their nominal 
composition). However, CuO composition was slightly lower than the 
nominal values in the three Cu-containing samples, and it was to some 
extent lower in the coimpregnated mixed oxide (36.79 wt%) compared 
with the single metal oxide sample (38.74%) and the sequentially 
impregnated sample (38.92%). Moreover, WO3 and ZrO2 actual com­
positions were proximate to their nominal values, within 0–2% differ­
ence. Measuring the actual composition of OCs is vital in order to find 
the theoretical oxygen capacity of the materials to be compared with the 
experimental oxygen release.

for the sequentially impregnated sample the first calcination step leads 
to the formation of a stable WO3 phase and when the Cu precursor was 
added in the second step, the extent of reaction between Cu and W was 
limited leading to less incorporation extent of Cu into the intermediate 
phase. Consequently, the Cu/W ratio is different in the two intermediate 
phases, and the CuWO4-x is understood to be oxygen deficient compared 
to the Cu3WO6 phase formed on the coimpregnated OC. This is expected 
to have significant consequences on the CLOU performance of these two 
samples.

following sections. 
3.3. Scanning electron microscopy/Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(SEM/EDS) 
SEM/EDS was used to investigate the oxidized OCs topography and 
elemental composition of their surfaces. The surface structural features 
of single metal oxides were investigated and their backscattered electron 
(BSE) images with EDS mapping are displayed in the supplemental file in 
Figs. S1 and S2. SEM BSE images of the impregnated CuO(40%)/ZrO2 
have shown that CuO was present in the sample as small nanosized 
agglomerated particles that spilled over the large particles of ZrO2 
support. On the other hand, the images of the WO3(33.33%) impreg­
nated over ZrO2 revealed that WO3 was present in a multifaceted crys­
talline structure surrounded by small ZrO2 nanoparticles. Unlike for the 
case of CuO/ZrO2 sample, it is understood that the existence of large 
WO3 crystals impeded the formation of large ZrO2 crystalline structure. 
The SEM BSE images together with the EDS elemental mapping of 
the combined OCs are shown in Figs. 2 and 3 for the coimpregnated and 
sequentially impregnated samples, respectively. EDS elemental mapping 
for the WO3-modified OCs confirmed the existence of Cu-W mixed oxide 
phases on both samples which was in accordance with XRD results 
shown in Fig. 1 and FTIR spectra in Fig. 4. The EDS mapping shown in 
Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 shows clearly that in addition to the distinctive Cu phase 
(shown in blue) and the ZrO2 support (green), that no stand-alone W 
species could be seen, and the W is always present together with Cu. In 
general, the morphology of the crystal structures of the Cu-W interme­
diate phase for both synthesis methods were similar to that of WO3 
supported over ZrO2. However, Cu-W intermediate phase formed on the 
coimpregnated sample is made of relatively smaller crystals (7–10 µm) 
compared to that of the sequentially impregnated OC. Interestingly, the 
CuO phase on the coimpregnated sample was made of bundles of smaller 
crystals whereas the CuO crystal sizes for the sequentially impregnated 
sample were significantly larger in size. One reason for having smaller 
CuO crystals for the coimpregnated OCs could be since large amount of 
CuO was consumed to form the Cu3WO6 phase and the remaining CuO is 
then grown onto the support. Our hypothesis is that the intermediate 
phase is firstly formed then the excess Cu is oxidized to form CuO at later 
stages of the calcination step, hence their crystal sizes were smaller. In 
addition, the Cu3WO6 might also hindered the growth of the CuO 
crystals during the calcination process. In contrast, for the sequentially 
impregnated sample less Cu has interacted with the W phase and CuO 
crystals grew with the progress of the calcination step. 
These observations were also confirmed from the crystallite size es­

3.4. Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) 
FTIR spectra were collected to identify the bonds between the metal 
oxides in the samples and to understand the characteristics differences in 
the samples before and after reduction. FTIR spectra of the oxidized OCs 
are shown in Fig. 4 (A) and the spectra for the reduced samples are 
shown in Fig. 4 (B). 
All the spectra exhibited weak absorption peaks near 1630 cm−1 and 
3450 cm−1 due to presence of moisture in air [30]. Moreover, the

the existence of ZrO2 support, which is in line with previously reported 
investigations [31–33]. Characteristic CuO absorption band ranges were

these characteristic bands were overlapping with other peaks present in 
the spectra, especially with ZrO2 peaks [34,35]. WO3/ZrO2 oxidized

CuWO4 intermediate phase. On the other hand, the coimpregnated 
sample reflected distinctive absorption peaks around 630 cm−1

3.5. Hydrogen Temperature Programmed Reduction (H2-TPR) 
H2-TPR analysis was performed to study the redox properties of the 
OCs in H2 reducing environment and the results are shown in Figs. 5 and 
6. From previous studies it was found that ZrO2 does not exhibit any 
reduction activity with H2 in the testing temperature range (150 ◦C to 
950 ◦C) [23,40], and the peaks appearing in TPR profiles have been 
assigned to the reduction of the active metal oxides, i.e. CuO and WO3, 
or their intermediate phases. From Fig. 5, WO3/ZrO2 sample showed 
high temperature reduction with H2 with the first peak summiting 
around 816.4 ◦C, which can be ascribed to the partial reduction of W+6 
to W+5. Whereas the second peak appearing after 900 ◦C can be ascribed 
to the reduction of W+6 and W+5 to W+4 [41]. The other single metal 
oxide, i.e., CuO/ZrO2, showed lower reduction temperatures compared 
with WO3/ZrO2. The profile of Cu-based OC shows a shoulder peak at 
lower temperature (309.8 ◦C) that can be attributed to reduction of 
highly dispersed CuO, while the second peak occurring at higher tem­
perature (364.3 ◦C) is assigned to reduction of bulk CuO which is more 
difficult to reduce compared with the dispersed species [42,43]. H2-TPR 
profiles of the mixed metal oxide OCs have shown similar trends in terms 
of number of peaks. Hence, it is suggested that both samples follow 
similar H2-reduction mechanisms. Previous reports have shown that the 
H2 reduction of Cu-W intermediate phase may occur in three steps; 
firstly the decomposition of the intermediate phase into CuO and WO3 
and reduction of CuO into metallic Cu, and the other two steps are 
related to WO3 reduction into lower oxidation states [19,20]. Here, 
similar claims can be made about the decomposition of the intermediate 
phases into separated CuO and WO3, and the first reduction peak 
(405.4 ◦C for the coimpregnated sample and 371.12 ◦C for the sequen­
tially impregnated sample) can be assigned to CuO (Cu+2) reduction into 
metallic Cu0. These peaks appear at higher temperatures than that ob­
tained from CuO/ZrO OC, which suggests that CuO present with WO3 is 
more stable and requires higher temperatures for reduction [20]. Whilst 
the second reduction peak can be attributed to the to the partial 
reduction of W+6 to W+5, followed by the partial reduction of W+6 and 
W+5 to W+5 and W+4. The last existing peak can be attributed to the 
reduction of W+4 to metallic W [19,20]. 
Quantitative H2 consumption during reduction of the OCs used in 
this study is shown in Fig. 6. WO3/ZrO2 consumed about 4.26 mmol/g of 
H2 during the tests. Primarily, this amount was recorded at temperatures 
above 750 ◦C as the sample was left in the apparatus for 1 h after the 
final temperature was reached (950 ◦C). Unmodified CuO/ZrO2 con­
sumption of H2 reached 4.82 mmol/g, most of which was consumed at 
low temperatures (below 430 ◦C). Cu-W/ZrO2 mixed OCs exhausted 
larger H2 amounts compared with the single metal oxide OCs, i.e., CuO/ 
ZrO2 and WO3/ZrO2. However, the CuO(40%)-WO3(20%)/ZrO2-Coim­
pregnation recorded higher H2-consumption than the sequentially 
impregnated sample. Higher H2-uptake statistics generally indicate the 
amount of oxygen available in the OC to be used for reduction, and the 
higher uptake of the coimpregnated OC can be attributed to the synergy 
between the metal oxides together with the type of phase present in the 
OC, Cu3WO6, compared with that present in the sequentially impreg­
nated OC, CuWO4-x, which is known to be oxygen deficient phase. The

3.6. CLOU Redox and Stability Testing 
CLOU cycling was performed in a TGA using N2 as inert environment 
for the OCs to release oxygen, and air as oxidizing agent for 10 cycles. 
The oxygen transport capacity (R0) of the different samples was 
measured. The CLOU oxygen carrying capacities (R0) of the OCs and 
their respective rate indices are shown in Table 2, together with the 
capacity of a reference CuO(40)%ZrO2 that was studied in a previous 
report [3]. While the cyclic performances are shown in Figs. S3–S5 in 
supplemental file. The reactions revealing the redox mechanism of the 
samples (Reactions R6-R9) were used to evaluate the theoretical oxygen 
carrying capacity. 
Mattission et al. previously tested CuO(40%)/ZrO2 for CLOU with 
petroleum coke [3]. In their report, Cu-based sample showed better 
performance than Co– and Mn-based OCs. Moreover, CuO(40%)/ZrO2 in 
that investigation exhibited an oxygen carrying capacity of 4.00%. The 
OC with similar nominal composition was prepared for this study using 
impregnation technique, and it showed a slightly higher oxygen carrying 
capacity of 4.17%. WO3(33.33%)/ZrO2 sample is not displayed with the 
other OCs as it did not exhibit any significant CLOU activity within the 
testing conditions. This is also supported by XRD and FTIR analyses 
(Figs. 1 and 4) as the reduced W-based sample did not show any change 
in the phases and spectra reflected before and after reduction. The other 
single metal oxide OC CuO(40%)/ZrO2 showed an average CLOU oxy­
gen transport capacity of 4.17% for the ten cycles. The phase change for 
this sample was from oxidized CuO into reduced Cu2O (See Figs. 1 and 
4), which are the expected redox pair for Cu-based CLOU process. The 
reduction and oxidation indices of this OC were the highest amongst the 
tested samples as shown in Table 2 and conversion profiles displayed in 
Figs. S6 and S7 in supplemental file. The coimpregnated OC showed an 
average CLOU capacity of 6.66%, which is the highest oxygen transport 
capacity obtained from the OCs studied in this work. This OC provided 
average rates indices for both reduction and oxidation steps. With the 
knowledge of the existing phases on both oxidized and reduced samples 
(from Figs. 1 and 4), it is suggested that the redox of the coimpregnated 
sample during CLOU with N2/air to occur through the following 
reactions:  
4 CuO ↔ 2 Cu2O + O2                                                                  
(R6)  
4 Cu3WO6 ↔ 4 Cu2WO4 + 2 Cu2O + 3 O2                                       
(R7) 
Hence, it is suggested that CuO phase will release oxygen through its 
transformation into Cu2O, and Cu3WO6 will generate oxygen via 
dissociation into Cu2WO4 and Cu2O. The presence of Cu3WO6 and its 
reduction/oxidation through the above mechanism together with the 
smaller grain sizes are suggested to be part of the causes of the 
enhancement CLOU capacity and kinetics of the coimpregnated sample 
as this intermediate phase improved the redox properties of WO3 which 
is not reducible in CLOU process when used as single metal oxide OC. 
While the sequentially impregnated sample showed an average 
4.45% CLOU capacity, which is higher than the capacity of the single 
metal oxide OC (CuO/ZrO2) but lower than that obtained by the coim­
pregnated OC. However, the sequentially impregnated sample exhibited 
the slowest reduction and oxidation rate indices compared with the 
other samples. The redox reactions of the sequentially impregnated 
sample based on its XRD and FTIR analyses before and after reduction 
are suggested to occur as:  
4 CuO ↔ 2 Cu2O + O2                                                                  
(R8)  
2 CuWO4-x + 2 CuO ↔ Cu2WO4 + O2                                            
(R9)

amount in the sequentially impregnated sample (as can be seen in 
Table S2 in supplemental file) was suggested to either be directly 
reduced to Cu2O cuprite phase or it will react with the oxygen deficient 
CuWO4 and generate oxygen and Cu2WO4 phase. The reaction between 
CuWO4-x and CuO is expected to be slow, which resulted in small rate 
indices. Furthermore, the existence of oxygen deficient CuWO4-x and the 
large grain sizes of the sequentially impregnated sample (as revealed by 
SEM BSE in Fig. 3 and crystal size estimation in Table S1 in supplemental 
file) hindered the oxygen release from this OC resulting in smaller CLOU 
capacity compared with the coimpregnated sample.

4. Conclusions 
Coimpregnation and sequential impregnation synthesis techniques 
were used to prepare mixed metal oxides oxygen carriers (CuO-WO3/ 
ZrO2). CLOU testing under an inert N2 environment showed that the 
coimpregnated oxygen carrier exhibited better CLOU performance in 
terms of higher oxygen release capacity together with better reduction 
and oxidation rate indices when compared with the sequentially 
impregnated sample. Both mixed metal oxide oxygen carriers (CuO- 
WO3/ZrO) displayed higher oxygen release capacities in contrast with 
the un-modified CuO/ZrO2 due to the formation of intermediate Cu-W 
phases on both mixed oxygen carriers. Consequently, theses interme­
diate phases resulted in enhanced redox properties as was shown by H2- 
TPR. However, further increase in the oxygen carrying capacity and 
higher rate indices of the coimpregnated sample over the sequentially 
impregnated sample was attributed to the formation of Cu rich inter­
mediate phase formed this sample (Cu3WO6) with more compact 
structure and smaller crystallite size as was confirmed by XRD and SEM 
characterization techniques. On the other hand, oxygen deficient 
CuWO4 phase was formed on the sequentially impregnated sample that 
has more elongated structure with larger crystallite size. These two in­
termediate phases are suggested to have different reduction mechanisms 
that can result in different CLOU performances. It is believed that the 
insertion of W into the lattice structure of CuO forming these two solid 
solutions is responsible for the improved performance due to the 
increased distribution of CuO in the mixed oxide which also inhibits the 
diffusion of CuO to the surface during redox cycles. Consequently, this 
has reduced the sintering and agglomeration on the WO3-modified ox­
ygen carriers leading to enhanced oxygen release properties. This work 
reveals the potential for W-based modifications of Cu-based oxygen 
carriers that can result in higher CLOU oxygen carrying capacity without 
drastically compromising the kinetics, which leads to the development 
of efficient oxygen carriers for CLOU process with lower solids in­
ventory. Insitu characterization of the Cu(40%)-WO3(20%)/ZrO2- 
Coimpregnation sample is recommended for future study to confirm the 
suggested redox mechanism for this sample. Although, the present study 
is valuable toward understanding sintering and agglomeration resis­
tance of WO3-modified Cu-based oxygen carriers, other aspects still 
needs to be evaluated such as the mechanical stability and attrition 
resiatance which should be further tested using fluidized bed setup. 
Furthermore, this work opens new avenues for addressing agglomera­
tion and sintering resistance in catalysis and chemical looping applica­
tions using WO3 promotion. Future work on addressing coke formation 
in Ni-based oxygen carriers using WO3 as a promotor are currently being 
developed. The ability of WO3 to form solid solutions with Cu and 
potentially Ni is interesting which can help in enhancing Ni dispersion 
and hence reduce carbon formation.
