A B S T R A C T   
An in-depth investigation of the BaO2/BaO redox couple for reversible solar thermochemical energy storage has 
been performed. Beyond improving the cyclability and energy storage density of the material, an additional 
objective of this work is to better understand inconsistencies in the literature surrounding this chemistry. Sin­
tering of BaO2/BaO is a hindrance for practical high temperature energy storage. MgO-stabilized BaO-based 
materials were synthesized by co-precipitation methods and investigated for improving the longevity and 
reactivity of the redox materials. The effect of the calcination environment during synthesis on sample purity was 
evaluated. Energy storage capacity and stability of commercial BaO2 and BaO2 made by co-precipitation methods 
were compared over 25 consecutive redox cycles. The effect of different amounts of MgO was studied, and it was 
discovered that with increasing amounts of MgO, the stability and reactivity of BaO2 increases while the energy 
storage density decreases. 15 wt% MgO/BaO2 exhibits the greatest stable weight gain/loss with a value of 6.62% 
(estimated 338 kJ/kg). A BaO2/MgO sample with 33 wt% MgO shows good storage capacity with weight gain/ 
loss at 5.65% (corresponds to approximately 290 kJ/kg) over 200 redox cycles.   
1. Introduction 
Harvesting and utilizing solar energy offers a sustainable way to 
reduce fossil fuel use and meet increasing energy demands [1–3]. Solar 
energy is an abundant, clean, and renewable resource, but it is only 
available intermittently. Effective methods need to be developed for 
short- and long-term energy storage to account for the intermittency of 
solar energy and meet changing electricity demand throughout the day. 
Currently, there are three main routes for solar thermal energy storage: 
sensible heat storage, latent heat storage, and thermochemical energy 
storage (TCES). TCES involves storing thermal energy via reversible 
chemical reactions, which is more attractive than the other two methods 
due to its inherently greater energy density. It also offers virtually in­
definite energy storage, provided the charged species are kept separate. 
Recently, solid-gas reactions have become the prevalent form of high 
temperature thermochemical energy storage. Such reversible reactions 
used for TCES have the general form: 
A + B⇄AB + ΔHr
(1) 
Energy is stored via the reverse endothermic reaction and released by 
the forward exothermic reaction. Chemical components investigated for 
TCES systems [3] include metal hydrides, carbonates, hydroxides, ox­
ides, ammonia, and organic species. Most of the reversible chemical 
reactions investigated operate at a higher temperature range of 
600–1200 ◦C, which is attainable using solar tower and dish systems. 
Nevertheless, the promise of reversible reactions has not yet been real­
ized due to issues including but not limited to: reduced material lifetimes 
due to sintering during cycling, impractical and ineffective heat transfer 
with the solar field and/or power block, a dearth of suitable high tem­
perature construction materials, the increased complexity of using 
chemical reactions, and the need to store and release mobile-phase 
reactive species such as H2, CO2, and H2O. A system using O2 as the 
mobile reactant is of interest since the system can operate in air, which 
streamlines the process and can potentially reduce the capital cost. 
R.G. Bowrey and J. Jutsen [4] first reported on the redox couple 
BaO2/BaO for energy storage in 1978. Few additional reports were 
published until recently [5–7] due to renewed interest in redox systems 
for energy storage applications. Other redox couples like Mn2O3/Mn3O4, 
[8–11] CoO/Co3O4, [12–17] and CuO/Cu2O [18,19] have also been 
considered. More recently, perovskite oxides like Ca(1-x)SrxMnO3 [20] 
and mixed oxides such as LiMnO2 [21] for TCES have been proposed, 
which opens new possibilities for finding suitable materials for TCES

Relevant research on Ba-based redox materials is summarized in 
Table 2. The BaO2/BaO redox couple has been recently revisited by 
Carrillo et al. [5] for solar thermochemical energy storage. It shows the 
redox couple has good stability and almost 100% conversion is obtained. 
The effect of the cooling rate on the oxidation conversion was investi­
gated. It was found that faster cooling rates led to faster oxidation ki­
netics. However, the effect of cooling rates on oxidation extent was not 
obvious. A nearly complete oxidation extent is observed for all three 
different cooling rates: 10 ◦C/min, 5 ◦C/min, and 2 ◦C/min. Based on 
our literature review, Carrillo et al. [5] is the only paper that observed 
no loss of reversibility for the BaO2/BaO redox material. 
On the contrary, Fahim and Ford [23] reported that the BaO2/BaO 
reaction system showed a substantial conversion decrease of 60% over 
20 cycles with reduction step carried out at 850 ◦C and re-oxidation step 
dwelt at 550 ◦C. A similar phenomenon was found by Wong et al. [6] 
where subsequent re-oxidation conversions were significantly less than 
that of the initial cycle. Ramping rates of 30 ◦C/min was used for both 
heating and cooling. Agrafiotis et al. [7] also investigates BaO2/BaO for 
potential cascaded thermochemical energy storage operation. In the 
paper, they observed nearly full recovery of the weight loss during 
reduction by lowering the cooling rates during cycling for 
Mn2O3/Mn3O4 redox couple. However, this is not the case for BaO2/­
BaO. The weight gain during oxidation improves from 2% to 3.5% by 
lowering the cooling rates from 5 ◦C/min to 2 ◦C/min, which is still far 
from the weight loss in the initial reduction step. Further lowering the 
cooling rates to 1 ◦C/min does not improve the weight gain in the 
oxidation step. They also observed that lower minimum temperatures 
(Tlow) do not result in higher extent of oxidation reaction at 2 ◦C/min. 
Based on conflicting results on the cyclic stability of BaO2/BaO re­
ported in the literature, it appears there is a need for a deeper look at the 
longevity of the material. From the work of Carrillo et al. [5] and 
Agrafiotis et al. [7], different cooling rates do not have much improve­
ment on oxidation extent and cyclic stability. In previous literature, it 
was reported that an ash layer of BaO2 (formed on the surface of BaO 
particles during re-oxidation) will create an oxygen diffusion barrier 
thus slowing the re-oxidation process and leading to incomplete con­
version [23]. Wong et al. [6] think that morphological changes might be 
the possible reason for the low oxidation conversion. Jin et al. report 
that pure BaO2/BaO system suffers from sintering and integration issue 
from their observation that BaO2 melts at around 450 ◦C, which is lower 
than the typical operating temperature [26]. However, melting at 450 ◦C 
has not been reported elsewhere [5,23], or in our work presented here. 
Here, an extensive characterization is performed to gain insight on 
the correlation of physiochemical properties with the stability and 
reactivity of the BaO2/BaO redox pair.

Different strategies can be employed to combat sintering of the 
material. One way is using steam treatment, widely studied and used in 
calcium looping for CO2 capture [30–34], and initially proposed by In­
dustrial Research Limited of New Zealand [35]. It has been demon­
strated that steam treatment can activate sorbents and regenerate 
surface area owing to structural changes. Different additives can be 
added to facilitate oxygen transport. Addition of MgO has been shown to 
improve the lifetime [26] and oxygen adsorption capacity [27] of BaO2. 
Other oxides such as Al2O3 [36], ZrO2 [37,38], TiO2 [39], La2O3 [40]40 
and Y2O3 [41], have also been studied to improve the stability of other 
redox systems. A third direction involves using a polymorphic spacer 
material (Ca2SiO4) which undergoes a crystal structure change during 
carbonation and calcination of CaCO3/CaO [42]. The effect of poly­
morphic spacers such as CaSO4 and Sr3(PO4)2 and initial moisture 
content of the material on the performance of the SrCO3/SrO system has 
been investigated [43]. 
Addition of MgO has been shown to improve the lifetime [26] and 
oxygen adsorption capacity [27] of BaO2. Pressure pressed pellets of 
mixed BaO2/MgO (weight ratio = 1:1)26 were tested, and the paper [26] 
claimed 90 cycles were tested without loss of reactivity. Park et al. [27] 
used two methods to synthesize mixed BaO2/MgO: hydrothermal and 
sol-gel. The sol-gel method showed better cyclic stability and higher 
adsorption capacity. By substituting Sr2+ for Ba2+ in the crystal lattice, 
Jung et al. [28,29] synthesized mixed metal peroxides (BaSrMgO). Both 
groups used the resultant mixed compounds for air separation and 
demonstrated increasing transition O2 pressure with increasing 
Sr2+/Ba2+ ratios. 
Previously, MgO has been added to mixtures reactive oxides for 
sintering prevention due to its high Tamman temperature and inertness 
to the reactive components [44]. In this work, MgO is also considered as 
an inert support to prevent sintering and improve the reactivity of 
BaO2/BaO redox cycles, especially the peroxide formation step. The 
effectiveness of MgO as a sintering inhibitor made by a different syn­
thesis method (co-precipitation) was investigated. A closer look was 
taken at the effects of composition and morphological properties for 
short-term (25 cycles) and long-term (200 cycles) stability and 
reactivity. 
2. Materials synthesis 
Materials purchased are shown in Table 3. Co-precipitation was 
adopted to synthesize barium peroxide, either pure or mixed with other 
additives. Metal nitrates were added to DI water and stirred until 
completely dissolved. A mixture of ammonium hydroxide and hydrogen 
peroxide was added to the solution. Materials precipitate immediately. 
The slurry was then filtered, washed twice with DI water, and then dried 
overnight under an inert N2 environment at 120 ◦C in the tube furnace. 
The product was then crushed into fine powder in an agate mortar. 
In a typical synthesis, 5 g Ba(NO3)2 precursor was added to 125 mL 
DI water in a 250 mL beaker. The Ba(NO3)2 was completely dissolved by 
continuous stirring at room temperature. Next, a mixture of 5 mL 30% 
H2O2 and 7 mL 30% NH4OH was added to the solution. After 5 min of 
stirring, the precipitated slurry was filtered, rinsed, and dried. 
3. Materials test and characterization 
Before each test, samples were sieved to reduce the effect of particle 
size distribution. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were used to obtain infor­
mation about phases of the synthesized materials. XRD analysis was 
conducted on a Bruker-AXS D8 DISCOVER X-ray diffraction instrument. 
Measured materials were supported on a glass sample holder. 
Energy dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) in conjunction with 
Scanning electron microscopy (FEI QUANTA 600F environmental SEM) 
was used to obtain information about morphology, composition, and 
elemental distribution. The samples were prepared by applying a thin 
layer of powder on a double-sided carbon tape mounted on the sample 
holder and gently tapping the stub to remove any excess powder. 
Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area and pore width distri­
bution were measured using nitrogen adsorption and desorption iso­
therms at −196 ◦C (77 K) using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 High 
Vacuum Micropore Physisorption - Chemisorption instrument. Before 
actual measurement, samples were initially degassed at around 150 ◦C 
for a few hours to remove absorbed moisture. Surface area (A) was 
calculated based on BET theory. Reported pore volume (V) was calcu­
lated by single point desorption with pores less than 1241.402 Å 
diameter at P/Po = 0.984. Reported average diameter (D) was calcu­
lated using D = 4V/A, according to BET. 
Thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA, Netzsch STA 449) measurements 
were performed with sample masses of 20–30 mg placed in an alumina 
crucible. Ultra-high purity gases (99.999% O2 and Ar) were used 
throughout the tests. Weight variations during oxygen release and up­
take cycles were recorded with an accuracy up to ±0.1 μg. All temper­
atures and gas compositions were programmed for each test. A 
correction was conducted to avoid the buoyancy effect by running blank 
runs with an empty alumina crucible under an identical temperature 
program and gas environment. 
In a typical TGA test, shown in Fig. 1, samples were pre-treated (in 
the TGA) to 1200 ◦C at 20 ◦C/min under an 80 mL/min argon envi­
ronment to remove the effect of impurities such as hydroxides and 
carbonates. The TGA program was setup to commence redox cycles 
immediately after this calcination period. Cycles consisting of a heating 
step from 400 ◦C to 950 ◦C and a cooling step from 950 ◦C to 400 ◦C were 
executed for assessing the performance of different samples. Both steps 
were at 5 ◦C/min under an 80% O2 environment with a total flow rate of 
100 mL/min. Mass variations for each cycle were collected and used to 
calculate performance indicators and compare the reactivity and sta­
bility of different materials. 
It should be noted that in several instances, the weight percentages 
exceeded 100% (see Fig. 7 and Fig. 15). This is because the thermal pre- 
treatment was conducted under Ar environment and thus the materials 
were in a decomposed state before the cycling test. The weight of the 
sample was recorded after the thermal pre-treatment. During the sub­
sequent cycling test, the sample was then exposed to oxygen environ­
ment and gained weight. An exception to this pattern is illustrated in 
Fig. 19 which shows cycles 100–125 of a 200-cycle test (Section 5.6). To 
minimize the need for a lengthy correction file, the 200-cycle experi­
mental campaign employed a 25-cycle correction file which was carried 
out 4 times in succession. Although the first segment of this campaign 
showed weight percentages greater than 100% due to the initial thermal 
pretreatment step (and decomposed state), segments thereafter (e.g. 
cycles 100–125 shown in Fig. 19) have weight percentages within the 
normal 100% bounds since they start with the sample in an oxidized 
form. 
For XRD measurements, the as-synthesized materials were charac­
terized without thermal pre-treatment.

5.2. Effect of drying environment (air vs inert) 
BaO2 was synthesized in the lab using the precipitation method, and 
the effect of the drying environment on the purity of products was 
investigated. The precipitated materials were divided in half and each 
was dried overnight in the oven at 120 ◦C under nitrogen and air en­
vironments, respectively. Samples were then tested in the TGA by 
heating up to 1200 ◦C at 20 ◦C/min under an Argon environment. It was 
discovered that an inert drying environment is critical for the purity of 
the synthesized materials, as the sample dried under an air environment 
contained more BaCO3. 
BaCO3 is more stable than BaO2 at high temperatures and it de­
composes later than BaO2, which is clearly shown from Fig. 4. Under an 
Ar environment in the TGA, BaO2 starts to decompose at 600 ◦C and 
finishes at around 800 ◦C, followed by decomposition of BaCO3 at 
around 1000 ◦C. From Table 4 and Fig. 4, it can be seen that weight loss 
due to decomposition of carbonates for the sample dried under air 
(7.33%) is much higher than for the one dried under an inert environ­
ment (1.52%). Due to the low quantities of BaCO3 in the sample dried 
under an inert environment, a BaCO3 phase is not clearly detectable in

As noted by others [4,5,7], BaO2 can readily react with CO2 and H2O 
in the environment. Additionally, since BaO is very sensitive to its sur­
roundings [5], it easily reacts with residual moisture and CO2 in the air, 
which can also be a technical problem and an obstacle for commercial 
applications. Thus, for future scaling up of such a process, operating 
under a CO2 and moisture free environment is vital. Contamination of 
the solid materials by water or CO2 can be mitigated by passing the air 
over a sorbent substance which absorbs moisture and CO2. In addition, 
stored products must be dealt with carefully to prevent deterioration at 
room temperature for further use.

CO2) can be calculated by its corresponding weight decrease at different

5.4. Comparison between commercial BaO2 and BaO2 made by the 
precipitation method

lab using the precipitation method has been compared with commercial 
BaO2 (95% purity, ACROS Organics™) in terms of their thermochemical 
energy storage performance. Through comparing BaO2 made by 
different synthesis methods and tested under similar conditions, it 
would be helpful and meaningful to gain insight on inconsistencies in 
the literature surrounding this chemistry. Extensive characterizations 
have been performed to better understand the correlation of physi­
ochemical properties with stability and reactivity of the BaO2/BaO 
redox pair.

5.5. The effect of MgO content 
Table 9 lists samples with different MgO content in mixed BaO2/MgO 
as well as their corresponding energy storage performance and 
morphological properties. From Table 9, it can be seen that with 
increasing Mg loading, total weight change (wt%loss tot) across 25 
consecutive peroxide formation-decomposition cycles decreases, while 
the initial total weight change also decreases. Meanwhile, surface areas 
and pore volume go up significantly as MgO content increases, which 
might be the fundamental reason for the different cyclic performance. 
Fig. 14 shows how weight change of mixed BaO2/MgO samples with 
different MgO loadings changes with each cycle. Sample BM55 showed 
low initial weight change but extremely stable cyclic weight loss/gain 
(wt%avg last 5) at 3.78%. Without MgO loading, Bp had the most 
aggressive degradation rate with a total percentage decay (%decay tot) of 
26.53% (shown in Table 7) over 25 consecutive cycles. BM15 exhibits 
the greatest stable weight change (wt%avg last 5) with a value of 6.62% 
(estimated 338 kJ/kg), while BM33 demonstrated a smaller total per­
centage decay but a lower stable weight change (wt%avg last 5) of 5.65% 
(corresponds to approximately 290 kJ/kg). Fig. 15 shows representative 
(12th to 17th cycle) TGA curves of weight versus temperature over 25 
cycles for samples Bp, BM15, BM33 and BM55. With decreasing MgO 
content, the peroxide formation reaction separated further into two 
consecutives steps. It can be seen from Fig. 15 that the two different 
shoulders in the weight gain step become steeper with decreasing MgO 
content. There is a tradeoff between higher reactivity/higher energy 
density vs. longevity when selecting the amount of MgO support added 
to the reactive materials. With more inert support materials, the stability 
is greater but at a cost of lower energy density. Jin et al. [45] tested 
pellets of BaO2 and MgO made through mechanical mixing and pressing 
and reported the released oxygen during reduction for BM33 and BM50 
is around 1.5 mmol/g and 1 mmol/g, respectively. In comparison, the 
stable oxygen sorption capacity for BM15, BM33, and BM55 synthesized 
by the co-precipitation method in this paper is around 2.0 mmol/g, 1.8 
mmol/g, and 1.2 mmol/g, respectively. It is clearly better than the 
sample made through the mechanical mixing and pressing method. The 
co-precipitation method showed a comparable result with the sol-gel 
method in the work of Park et al. [27] The best oxygen sorption ca­
pacity synthesized by the sol-gel method is reported to be 2.13 mmol/g. 
The effect of MgO content on sintering and stability is further illus­
trated by SEM images of the samples, shown in Fig. 16. Micrographs are 
shown for samples Bp, BM15, BM33 and BM55 before and after 25 
consecutive redox cycles performed using cooling and heating steps 
between 400 ◦C and 950 ◦C, ramped at 5 ◦C/min under 0.8 bar O2 
pressure. The sample without MgO (Bp) showed a higher degree of 
cluster formation after 25 cycles compared to samples with MgO added. 
Reactivity of Bp decreases gradually over cycles due to less reactive 
materials exposed to oxygen. 
Samples collected before and after TGA testing were characterized by 
SEM to obtain information about morphology change and the distribu­
tion of inert support and reactive materials was measured by EDS 
elemental mapping. In Fig. 17, results of elemental mapping for samples 
BM17, BM33, and BM55 are shown. Mg and Ba are uniformly distributed 
both before and after cycles, indicating the well mixed products pro­
duced by the co-precipitation method. 
Fig. 18 shows the XRD pattern of sample BM55 before and after 
redox cycling. In the freshly synthesized sample (before thermal pre- 
treatment up to 1200 ◦C), it was primarily composed of BaO2, Mg 
(OH)2, and BaCO3. After thermal pre-treatment and redox cycles, peaks 
for Mg(OH)2 disappeared and an MgO phase evolved due to the 
decomposition of Mg(OH)2. It can be seen from Fig. 18 that peaks of 
BaCO3 still exist, indicating an incomplete decomposition during ther­
mal pre-treatment at 1200 ◦C. For complete decomposition of BaCO3, a 
higher decomposition temperature would be required. The absence of 
any apparent BaO2 peak shifting indicates no mixed phase of Ba and Mg 
compounds formed. The only shifting peak of BaO2 after cycling is from 
42.2◦to 43.5◦, the peak becomes broader and asymmetrical compared 
with the freshly synthesized sample due to the overlap of the BaO2 and 
MgO peaks at that angle period. 
5.6. Long-term cycling performance 
Fig. 19 shows a representative (100th to 125th cycle) TGA curve of 
weight versus temperature over 200 cycles for sample BM33 (mixed 
BaO2/MgO with 33 wt% MgO). Fig. 20 shows the weight change over 
200 cycles for sample BM33. After the initial weight drop, the materials 
become extremely stable without much deactivation and have excellent 
stability and reactivity over the cycles at 5.65% (estimated 290 kJ/kg 
based on formula (2)).

separation. Barium peroxides/oxides by themselves are much cheaper 
than other materials for air-based thermochemical energy storage, such 
as manganese, cobalt, and copper-based oxides. 
With increasing oxygen pressure, the charging temperature is closer 
to the discharging temperature. Separation of products from the reaction 
vicinity will be critical to prevent the reverse reaction and can be real­
ized through reactor design and process implementation. As was ex­
pected, it was also found that the synthesis and reaction processes are 
sensitive to moisture and CO2 in the air. Contamination of the solid 
materials by water or CO2 can be mitigated by passing the air over a 
sorbent substance to absorb moisture and CO2. 
To elucidate the disparity in results on the stability of BaO2/BaO in 
the literature, performance differences between BaO2 made by the 
precipitation method and commercial BaO2 were evaluated. It was 
found that BaO2 made by the precipitation method had greater cycla­
bility and energy density than commercial BaO2 due to larger surface 
area, average pore width, and pore volume. 
Samples of BaO2 with inert MgO support were synthesized by a new 
co-precipitation method. MgO was found to be an effective additive to 
improve the stability and reactivity of the BaO2/BaO redox cycle. 
Comparing samples with different MgO loading, it was shown that with 
increasing MgO content, the reactive stability of redox cycles improved. 
The increasing surface area, pore volume and average pore width ach­
ieved with increased MgO support might be the reason for the improved 
reactive stability. 
Mixed BaO2/MgO with 55 wt% MgO has a stable weight gain/loss at 
3.78% (estimated 193 kJ/kg) that does not change over 25 cycles. For 
the 15 wt% MgO sample, the stable weight gain/loss of 6.62% (esti­
mated 338 kJ/kg) is even higher compared to the sample without MgO 
added. A 200-cycle test of 33 wt% MgO in BaO2/MgO was performed to 
further test the stability after 25 cycles. Weight gain/loss at 5.65% 
(corresponds to approximately 290 kJ/kg) remains stable after an initial 
energy density drop. 
Although these energy densities may not be the highest values 
among reported thermochemical energy storage chemistries, they are 
still significantly higher than latent or sensible energy storage schemes.
