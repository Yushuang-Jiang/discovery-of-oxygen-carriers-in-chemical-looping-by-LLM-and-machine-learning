Ca- and Ga-Doped LaMnO3 for Solar Thermochemical CO2 Splitting
with High Fuel Yield and Cycle Stability

Hangbin Zheng, Yong Hao, and Yimin Xuan*

ABSTRACT: Solar thermochemical CO2 splitting to produce fuels
is an eﬀective route to reduce carbon emission. However, it is still a
daunting challenge to achieve a high CO yield and good cycle
stability simultaneously at a low reaction temperature. Here, Ca-
and Ga-doped LaMnO3 is introduced for solar thermochemical
CO2 splitting with an ultrahigh CO yield and excellent cycle
stability at a moderate operation temperature. The average CO
production reaches 513 μmol/g for La0.6Ca0.4Mn0.8Ga0.2O3 when
operating between 1350 and 1050 °C. Such a high value sets a
record among directly measured thermochemical CO production
in the literature with the temperature swing limited within 300 °C.
No obvious performance deterioration over eight cycles is observed
due to stable structures. The high CO production of
La0.6Ca0.4Mn0.8Ga0.2O3 can be attributed to the transition of the surface reaction to internal bulk diﬀusion induced by Ca doping
and enhanced non-stoichiometry due to Ga doping. This work provides a promising alternative for solar thermochemical CO2
splitting with high fuel yield and cycle stability.
KEYWORDS: solar thermochemical, solar fuel, CO2 splitting, perovskites, cycle stability
1. INTRODUCTION
Conventional fossil fuels can hardly meet the increasing energy
demand in a long period of time.1 On the other hand, massive
burning of fossil fuels has led to a prominent increase of
atmospheric CO2 concentration and subsequent climate
change problems. In this regard, solar energy has been
regarded as one of the most promising energy sources to
power the world in a low carbon way.2,3 Solar energy-driven
CO2 reduction can not only overcome the intermittent nature
of solar energy but also helps to close the anthropogenic
carbon cycles by converting CO2 into green fuels.4−6 In
particular, solar thermochemical CO2 splitting to produce CO
via a two-step process has recently attracted much attention as
the entire solar spectrum can be utilized, in stark contrast to
photocatalysis7 or electrocatalysis,8,9 for which only the above-
band-gap photons can be harvested. In general, solar
thermochemical CO2 splitting can be represented as follows

In the endothermic reduction (TR) step, metal oxides
(MOx) lose some oxygen atoms to form non-stoichiometric
oxides (MOx−y) under sunlight illumination. In the following
exothermic step, the reduced metal oxides react with CO2 to
produce CO (CDS) at a lower temperature, and MOx−y as
intermediate products are recovered to the original state after
the completion of one cycle. The net reaction is that CO2 splits
into CO and O2.
Up until now, tens of metal oxides have been investigated for
solar thermochemical CO2 splitting, such as Fe3O4/FeO,
ZnO/Zn,10 SnO2/SnO,11 ABO3/ABO3−δ (perovskite),12−14
CeO2/Ce2O3,15−18 and ferrite19 catalytic systems. Stoichio-
metric redox pairs such as ZnO/Zn and SnO2/SnO have high
oxygen exchange capacities, but they are volatile. The non-
volatile Fe3O4/FeO redox pair sinters easily and has poor
thermal stability. Non-stoichiometric oxides retain their
crystallographic structure unchanged after redox cycles, such

Perovskites have recently emerged as promising alternatives
for thermochemical CO2 splitting due to a lower operation
temperature and a higher CO yield.14,29−35 The general
formula for perovskite is ABO3, wherein cation A is in 12-fold
coordination with oxygen and cation B is in 6-fold
coordination with oxygen, as shown in Figure 1a. The A/B
sites of perovskite materials can be partially replaced by one or
more elements, resulting in a highly ﬂexible elemental
composition and a tunable two-step thermochemical CO2-
splitting performance.12,33,36−45 Among them, Mn-based
perovskite materials are widely studied. McDaniel et al.46
ﬁrst found that Sr- and Al-doped LaMnO3 exhibited a
remarkable improvement of CO production compared with
that of CeO2 in the temperature range of 1000−1350 °C, and
the material was not deactivated after 80 cycles. Nevertheless,
the production of CO (247 μmol/g) is still not suﬃciently
high. Dey et al.37 reported that Y-doped SrMnO3 exhibited
excellent performance (757 μmol/g) in the CO2 fuel
conversion in the redox temperature range of 900−1400 °C.
However, the swing between the reduction and oxidation steps
is as high as 500 °C, so that the heat loss will be too large. Nair
and Abanades32 pointed out that the presence of Co, Fe, and
Ni in site B helps lower the reduction temperatures to release
more O2 (below 1300 °C) but would deteriorate the cycle
stability. Overall, it is still a daunting challenge to develop ideal
solar thermochemical materials, which possess high fuel
production, good cycle stability, and moderate temperature
swing with low heat loss.
Considering that Ca-substituted La at site A has
demonstrated great potential in both thermochemical CO2
splitting and H2O splitting36,47,48 and Ga doped in site B can
increase the non-stoichiometry δ,29,30,45 Ca- and Ga-doped
LaMnO3, that is, La1−xCaxMn1−yGayO3 is proposed for high-
performance thermochemical CO2 splitting. Physicochemical
characterization is carried out to analyze the properties of
materials. The eﬀects of diﬀerent Ca and Ga doping ratios and
reduction and oxidation temperatures on the CO2 fuel
conversion performance are also studied. In addition, the
inﬂuence of diﬀerent preparation methods (sol−gel method

2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Preparation of Materials. La1−xCaxMn1−yGayO3 perovskite
oxides are prepared by the modiﬁed sol−gel method. All the chemical
reagents used in the experiment are of analytically pure grade, and
they are used directly without further puriﬁcation. In a typical
preparation procedure, stoichiometric amounts of materials of
La(NO3)3·6H2O (purity ≥99.0%), Ca(NO3)2·4H2O (purity ≥
99.5%), Ga(NO3)3·xH2O (purity ≥99.0%), and Mn(NO3)2 solution
(50%) are dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water, and citric acid
(C6H8O7) is added to the mixture with a 1:1.5 mol ratio of total metal
cations to citric acid after stirring well. Then, the mixture solution is
heated to 90 °C to remove excess water with continuous stirring until
complete geliﬁcation is achieved. After that, the gel is dried at 120 °C
for 6 h. The xerogel is ground to a powder and calcined in a muﬄe
oven at 400 °C for 2 h to remove organic matter, then calcined in a
high-temperature muﬄe furnace in an air atmosphere at 1300 °C for 6
h, whose purpose is to form a stable phase structure. At last, the
resulting powder is ground again to obtain a ﬁnely powdered sample
for subsequent thermochemical cycling experiments.
2.2. Characterization of Samples. Synthesized perovskite
oxides and used samples are characterized by powder X-ray diﬀraction
(XRD) on a Smart Lab X-ray diﬀractometer (XRD, Bruker D8A A25
X) with Cu Kα radiation. XRD patterns are operated in the range of
20−80° with a step size of 0.02° and a scan rate of 6° min−1.
Microscopic morphology of samples is obtained by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Gemini 300). To obtain the valence states
of elements, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis is
carried out using an XPS photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo
Scientiﬁc K-Alpha) with an aluminum anode (Al Kα, 100 W, hv =
1486.65 eV). EDS mapping analysis is carried out using a Hitachi
TM-3000 for investigating the sample composition and elemental
distribution. The absorption spectrum and reﬂection spectrum are
collected using a ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectrophotometer
(PerkinElmer Lambda 1050+) and a Fourier transform infrared
(FTIR) spectrometer (Thermo Scientiﬁc Nicolet iS 50 FTIR
spectrometer), respectively.
2.3. CO2Splitting Performance Test. The schematic of the
experimental device is shown in Figure 1b. The test for
thermochemical CO2 splitting is carried out in a programed horizontal
high-temperature tubular furnace. The outer diameter of the
corundum tube is 25 mm and the length is 600 mm. The alumina
crucible with the sample (250 mg) is placed in the corundum tube
and heated in the heating section. The gas ﬂow rates of CO2 and Ar
are separately controlled by diﬀerent mass ﬂow meters; diﬀerent gases
are mixed well in the commingler, and then ﬂown into the ﬁxed-bed
reactor for reaction. The outlet gases O2 and CO are detected by the
O2 detector (YF-A01-O2) and the CO detector (CH−H−CO) in real
time, respectively. Detectors are calibrated with the standard known
concentration gases to ensure accuracy of the experiment.
Before the experiment, the device is swept by high-purity Ar
(99.999%) to reduce the oxygen concentration, and the O2
concentration in this system is about 10 ppm. The introduced Ar

3.2. Thermochemical CO2 Fuel Conversion Perform-
ance. 3.2.1. Inﬂuence of the Ga Doping Ratio at the B Site.
The inﬂuence of Ga proportion on the thermochemical CO2-
splitting performance is given in Figure 6. The same
experimental conditions (the reduction temperature is 1350
°C for 45 min in Ar and the oxidation temperature is 1050 °C
for 90 min in CO2) are applied for all samples. As shown in
Figure 6a, the O2 release start-up temperature has a slight
decline with doping concentration and decreases from about
1200 to 1100 °C, as the doping concentration increases to 0.3.
The O2 production rate is also enhanced by doping Ga with
the peak value enhanced up to 0.175 μmol/g/s for LCMG30
compared with 0.116 μmol/g/s of LCMG0. Figure 6c shows
the CO production rate during the CO2-splitting process, and
as can be seen clearly, the CO production rate of all samples
peaks within a few minutes, followed by a slower CO-
producing process. The peak CO production rate increases as
the Ga dopant increases from 0 to 0.2 but decreases with
further increase of the Ga dopant. The maximum rate reached
is 0.464 μmol/g/s while the Ga content is 0.2. Figure 6a,d
shows the total production yield of O2 and CO, respectively.
The total O2 production is 200, 242, 287, and 315 μmol/g for
Ga contents of 0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3, respectively. Clearly, the
presence of Ga at the B site can increase the amount of oxygen
released. The total CO production is also enhanced by doping
Ga, and the highest CO production appears for LCMG20,
which is 30% higher than that for LCMG0. It is noted that
although LCMG30 has the highest O2 production, its CO yield
is not as high as that of LCMG20. This may be because the
more the immutable valence of Ga in materials is, the less the
amount of variable valence transition metal Mn ions will be,
which will inevitably reduce the reactivity with CO2, so the
best Ga stoichiometric ratio is 0.2.

expected to be due to a smaller ionic radius of Ca2+ (1.0 Å)
compared with that of La3+ (1.03 Å). Correspondingly, grain

seen clearly in Figure 7i−l. SEM micrographs of

so serious that a crystalline ﬁlm is formed on the surface of the
crucible and cannot be removed. Overall, the optimal Ca ratio
is 0.4 as we assumed at the beginning. Smaller and higher Ca
ratios will deteriorate the CO production due to low non-
stoichiometry or low O2 production and severe sintering,
respectively.
3.2.3. Inﬂuence of Reduction and Oxidation Temper-
atures. According to previous analysis, the best doping ratio of
Ga and Ca is 0.2 and 0.4, respectively, for which the highest
CO yield can be obtained. To investigate the inﬂuence of
reduction temperature on CO yields, LCMG20 is tested at
diﬀerent temperatures (1050, 1150, 1250, 1350, and 1400 °C)
with other parameters kept unchanged. Figure 8a,b shows the
performance of LCMG20 with the reduction temperature
varying from 1050 to 1400 °C. As shown in Figure 8a, CO
generation is not detected at 1050 °C. That is because metal
oxygen bonds are hard to break at low temperatures, so that
the sample does not even release oxygen in the ﬁrst reduction
step. Increasing the reduction temperature is beneﬁcial to the
thermal reduction reaction, which can enhance O2 production,
hence the CO production also improved until 1350 °C.
However, it can be found that the amount of O2 released
decreases slightly from 287 to 281 μmol/g, while CO
production decreases sharply from 515 to 358 μmol/g when
the reduction temperature is further increased to 1400 °C, as
can be seen clearly from Figure 8b. The reason is that too high
temperatures will cause the sample to sinter severely and lead
to a signiﬁcant decrease of the speciﬁc surface area of the
samples. This will largely inhibit the direct contact of CO2 with
the reduced oxide, in turn. Therefore, 1350 °C is a more
favorable reduction temperature for LCMG20 because of the
highest CO production.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, Ga-doped La0.6Ca0.4MnO3 perovskite materials
are proposed for the two-step thermochemical CO2 splitting.
Compared with undoped La0.6Ca0.4MnO3, introducing Ga is
beneﬁcial to reduce the oxygen release temperature and
increase the CO production yield. The optimal Ga doping
ratio is 0.2 with the total CO production yield increasing to
513 μmol/g in the optimum redox temperature range of
1050−1350 °C. Such a high value sets a new record for the
thermochemical CO production yield measured directly. No
obvious deterioration is observed for eight cycles, and the peak
production rate is also enhanced by 80% compared with those
of undoped counterparts. Diﬀerent fabrication methods are
employed, and the sol−gel method demonstrates a higher CO
production by 40% compared with that obtained by the solid-
state approach. Reaction mechanisms of diﬀerent samples are
evaluated and found that the high CO production of
La0.6Ca0.4Mn0.8Ga0.2O3 can be attributed to the transition of
the surface reaction to internal bulk diﬀusion induced by Ca
doping and enhanced non-stoichiometry due to Ga doping.
This work opens a new route for high-performance two-step
solar thermochemical CO2 splitting at moderate operation
temperatures based on Ga-doped La0.6Ca0.4MnO3 perovskite
materials.
