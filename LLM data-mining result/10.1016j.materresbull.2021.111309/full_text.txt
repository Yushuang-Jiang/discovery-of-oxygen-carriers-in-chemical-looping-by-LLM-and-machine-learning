A B S T R A C T   
The design of new oxygen storage materials remains the key factor for intensive progress in technologies of air- 
free hydrocarbons combustion. The influence of the iron concentration on the oxygen storage characteristics of 
solid solutions PrBaMn2–xFexO6–δ is discussed. The complex oxides are synthesized via combined glycine- 
glycerol-nitrate precursor route. Dependence of unit cell parameters on iron content is estimated by X-ray 
powder diffraction at heating and an extended solid solution is shown to be formed up to x = 1.00. The tem­
perature increase, as well as iron doping, is shown to be favorable factors for the crystallization of highly 
symmetric perovskites. Thermogravimetry with differential scanning calorimetry is used to demonstrate a phase 
stability of iron doped materials at elevated temperatures in air. Solid solutions exhibit large oxygen storage 
capacity and excellent reversibility of exchange under switching gas phase between air ↔ 5% H2 in Ar. With the 
increase of iron concentration, the kinetics of redox processes are shown to decline which caused by suppression 
in anisotropy of thermal expansion under doping.   
1. Introduction 
The development of new ecologically clean methods of hydrocarbons 
processing has become a matter of high interest with the present state of 
environmental degradation in conjunction with the increasing level of 
energy demand. One of the technologies that meet the requirements of 
environment-oriented energetics is the chemical looping with oxygen 
uncoupling (CLOU) based on the cyclic redox processes of compounds 
acting as oxygen storage materials (OSMs) [1–3]. The efficiency of CLOU 
processes directly depends on such OSMs characteristics, as oxygen 
storage capacity, redox kinetics, structural and mechanical robustness. 
Simple oxides of transition metals seem to have been most intensely 
studied for applications in CLOU technologies [4–9]. For instance, 
copper and nickel oxides can release about 15 wt% of oxygen when 
reduced to metals. Unfortunately, the reduction process is accompanied 
with agglomeration of the metal particles. In order to overcome this 
problem, an inert support can be used like alumina or zirconia. How­
ever, this method results in a sharp decrease of the storage capacity of 
the obtained materials [10,11]. Though better withstanding reductive 
caking, iron and manganese oxides show poor kinetics of oxygen ex­
change [9,12] while cobalt oxide is too much expensive for the appli­
cation [4]. The drawbacks of simple oxides call for the search of 
alternatives such as the using of more complex oxides in Ca-Mn-O, 
Mn-Fe-O, Mn-Si-O and Mn-Ni-O systems [13–18]. Among them, perov­
skite related oxides are of particular interest due to favorable arrange­
ments of oxygen ions being capable for fast ionic transport. The 
literature data show that perovskite-like manganites are most numerous 
complex oxides maintaining a single-phase state in oxygen exchange 
reactions for OSM applications, which reflects their abundance, avail­
ability and capability of using in variety of conditions. For example, low 
cost and rather large variations of oxygen content at elevated temper­
atures are attractive features of CaMnO3–δ [4,19]. Still, the oxygen ex­
change rate for simple perovskite-like manganites is not high enough for 
CLOU applications [4] and the research interest is aimed at modifying its 
chemical composition and crystal structure to improve functional 
properties. One of the widely used methods is doping with atoms of 
substantially different size compared with the ones in the pristine oxide 
resulting in formation of ordered perovskite-like structures. The derived 
structures are usually anisotropic and accommodate oxygen vacancies in 
strictly defined energetically non-equivalent crystallographic positions 
[20–22]. Such oxides are characterized by a wide range of oxygen ho­
mogeneity enabling an increased oxygen capacity to be expected. 
Recently, PrBaMn2O6–δ (PBMO) was suggested as a promising CLOU 
material [13–16,23] which belongs to the class of A-site ordered double 
perovskites containing a layered arrangement of praseodymium and 
barium ions. The release of oxygen from the oxide in reducing

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Synthesis 
High purity powdered oxides Pr6O11, Mn2O3, barium carbonate 
BaCO3 and iron carbonyl (R grade) were used as starting materials for 
the synthesis of PrBaMn2–xFexO6–δ (PBMFO). The reagents were weighed 
in appropriate amounts, placed in a quartz glass and fully dissolved with 
the nitric acid. Then glycerol was added to the solution in equimolar 
ratio to the total content of metal cations and glycine in half а of this 
quantity. This mix of organic components are both prevents formation of 
insoluble iron glycerolate complex [26] and high-intensity combustion 
due to activity of metal-glycinate complex. The obtained liquor was 
heated on a hot plate with continuous stirring till complete evaporation 
of water and ignition of the residue. The resulting fine powder was 
ground and calcined in air at 950 ◦C for 10 h in order to remove the 
carbon traces. The obtained powder was pelletized under uniaxial load 
of 2 kBar and fired consecutively in air at 1350 ◦C for 10 h, H2 at 850 ◦C 
for 10 h and finally in air at 400 ◦C for 10 h. A part of the as-synthesized 
sample was separated for characterization experiments. 
2.2. Characterization 
Experimental X–ray powder diffraction (XRD) spectra were collected 
by a Shimadzu XRD-7000 diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation. The ox­
ygen contents (6–δ) of the oxides were determined by iodometric titra­
tion [27]. The morphology of the studied materials was examined by 
JEOL JSM 6390 scanning electron microscope (SEM). The specific sur­
face area of the powders was measured by the BET method with a 
Gemini VII (Micromeritics, USA) analyzer. The particle size distribution 
of the pulverized samples was determined using a LA-950V2 analyzer 
(Horiba, Japan) with deionized water as the suspending agent. In order 
to avoid particles agglomeration measurements were carried out during 
ultrasonic processing. 
2.3. Phase stability tests 
The phase stability was studied with the use of a high-temperature 
Anton Paar HTK 1200 N camera and a PANalytical XPert Pro auto­
mated diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation in the 2θ range 20–80◦with 
0.026◦step size and 50 s acquisition time. In order to obtain reliable data 
the samples were heated up and equilibrated at the desired temperature 
for 20 min before each measurement. The obtained data were utilized 
for the crystal structure refinement with the use of the FullProf suite 
package [28]. The absolute errors of the unit cell parameters determi­
nation were calculated according to the positions of reflexes with 
Nelson-Riley correction [29]. Additionally, the phase stability of single 
phase compounds was tested on powdered samples at heating up to 
1200 ◦C with a rate of 5◦/min in the airflow followed by keeping at these 
conditions for 12 h and slow cooling down. The susceptibility of 
Fe-doped manganites towards reducing conditions was examined by 
calcination of pelletized oxides in 5% H2 in Ar mixture at 850 ◦C during 
12 h. The phase composition of the samples after the abovementioned 
treatments was controlled by XRD. 
2.4. Oxygen exchange with the gas phase 
Simultaneous thermogravimetric and differential scanning calori­
metric measurements (TGDSC) in air were performed with the use of a 
STA 449 F3 Jupiter (Netzsch, Germany). The typical weight of the 
powder specimens was selected as ~40 mg, TG-DSC data were collected 
by heating of the samples in a corundum pot from room temperature to 
1200 ◦C with the rate of 5◦/min in airflow. The oxygen uptake/release 
characteristics were studied in additional experiments. At the first stage, 
the samples were heated to 950 ◦C in the air with the rate of 5◦/min and 
then carefully cooled down to 200 ◦C with 0.5◦/min to provide an 
equilibrium oxygen content. The oxygen loss at reduction was obtained 
from the weight decrease of the samples in a corundum pan at heating 
up to 950 ◦C with a rate of 2◦/min in a 5% H2 in Ar gas flow. After 
heating, the samples were kept at a constant temperature and gas media 
until the weight loss acquired an asymptotic-like view caused by a low 
rate of oxygen exchange between gas and reduced oxides. At the final 
stage, the atmosphere was switched on air to provide a full oxygen 
saturation of the samples. Chamber vacuuming was performed before 
each change of atmosphere. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characterization

be considered as interesting phenomenon that can be of great practical 
prospects of iron doped material at high-temperature applications. It is 
well-known the anisotropic nature of the thermal expansion in disor­
dered perovskite-like manganites is closely related to the deviation of 
cubic symmetry due to MnO6 tilting [32,33]. In the ABO3 perovskite 
structure the angle of the B–O–B bonds close to 180◦and the B–O 
bond length is smaller compared with A–O one. But for manganites, in 
the case of LaxCa1−xMnO3 solid solution, the tilting of MnO6 polyhedra 
results in a little shift of oxygen ion from the Mn–Mn edge to the A ion 
while the Mn–O–Mn angle declines to 160◦. However ordering of 
A-cations is expected to be favorable for impeded tilting of polyhedra 
because of the formation of strong structural anisotropy. Moreover as 
earlier demonstrated the Mn3+O6 octahedra are tended to local 
Jahn-Teller distortions with a various Mn–O bonds that provide a linear 
elongation along the c-axis in perovskite-like manganites [34–36]. In 
this regard it is reasonable to assume the studied oxide PrBaMn2O6–δ, 
containing an equal amount of octahedrally coordinated Mn3+ and 
Mn4+ to adopt these features and manifest the anisotropic expansion 
also. The observed suppression in anisotropic expansion can be easily 
explained by substitution of Mn3+ ions by stable Fe3+ ones. Taking into 
account the constant oxygen content in the oxides upon doping (Table 1) 
it is reasonable to assume that the content of Mn4+ ions remains 
unchanged. Suggested mechanism is expected to stabilize the structure 
of double perovskite by reducing the content of labile Mn3+O6 octahedra 
and to maintain a high oxygen content in the samples. To verify this 
statement the required experiments were carried out and obtained re­
sults are discussed below.

reducing atmospheres are shown by Fig. 4. As can be seen a continuous

3.3. Oxygen intake/release properties 
The weight changes for all PBMFO samples under gas phase 
switching air ↔ 5% H2 in Ar are close to each other (Fig. 6). In reducing 
atmosphere in PrBaMn1.89Fe0.11O6-δ the relatively fast weight loss is 
followed with a slow decrease in isothermal mode. Heating in a reducing 
atmosphere is accompanied by the oxygen deintercalation from the 
oxide with changes in manganese and iron ions valency according to 
reaction (1) and corresponding to intensive oxygen loss:

The further low-rate weight decrease can be related to the lingering 
reduction of trivalent manganese ion resulting in partial decomposition 
of manganite with MnO formation as an impurity, which is confirmed by 
the XRD data. This effect was recently discussed for undoped oxide 
PrBaMn2O6–δ in [23] and its reversibility in an oxidizing atmosphere 
was shown. 
For PrBaMn1.89Fe0.33O6–δ and PrBaMn1.89Fe0.67O6–δ the three-step 
weight change is observed: a fast reduction is followed by a stage with 
a lower rate and finished by a slow drift. The second stage may be 
associated with different reduction kinetics of manganese and iron ions 
due to the difference in the energy for vacancy formation when the 
Fe–O and Mn–O bonds are broken [24]. Therefore, these effects appear 
in doped manganites as the concentration of iron increases. 
It is worth to mention, that in PrBaMnFeO6-δ there is one-step weight 
loss without the following drift showing the high phase stability of the 
oxide in a reducing atmosphere that additionally confirms obtained 
experimental XRD data. The low rate weight decrease in undoped 
manganite PBMO is associated both with the low kinetics of manganese 
3+ ions reduction and the continuous accumulation of impurity. Notice 
such decomposition is observed for the oxygen-deficient phase namely 
‘O5’ where the oxygen vacancies are formed entirely in O–Pr–O layers 
[16]. It is reasonable to assume that the MnO has to be crystallized as 
secondary phase in the ordered matrix PrBaMn2O6–δ near these struc­
tural elements. On the other hand one can observe in Fig. 4(b) some 
decrease of impurity peak with the iron content growth that evidences in 
favor of incline the fraction of manganese ions prone to deep reduction 
down to the simple oxide. TG data show that iron doping leads to the 
decrease of this effect, which completely disappears in the ordered 
composition of PrBaMnFeO6–δ. The XRD spectra from Fig. 4(b), con­
taining reflexes from MnO impurity and pure phase PBMFO after 
reduction in H2 confirm this hypothesis. The weight changes dW in high 
rate oxygen release stages closely correspond to the theoretical value at 
the transition ‘O6’ ↔ ‘O5’ for all PBMFO solid solutions [14,16,23]. 
The switch of the hydrogen-containing atmosphere for air results in a 
very fast oxygen uptake almost to the maximum, which is followed with 
slow saturation. It should be noted that the increase in iron concentra­
tion reduces the oxygen capacity of PBMFO. These effects are almost 
imperceptible in PrBaMn1.89Fe0.11O6-δ and PrBaMn1.67Fe0.33O6-δ oxides 
but become quite significant for the PrBaMnFeO6-δ, in which the sample 
weight is decreased by ≈0.35 % during one red-ox cycle.

On the other hand, some decrease of red-ox activity of the PBMF 
phase could be attributed to the suppression in the anisotropy of thermal 
expansion with iron doping. Indeed, a sharp increase of unit cell pa­
rameters along c-axis in parent PBMO at heating seems to be favorable 
for relatively fast oxygen diffusion thorough zigzag-like channels which 
usually formed in ab plane in double-perovskite structure [40]. These 
routes involve non-equivalent vacancy positions O3 and O2 located in 
O–Pr–O and O–Mn–O layers (Fig. S1), respectively [41]. A slight 
change in the unit cell parameters along ab plane observed in Fig. 3b for 
iron-free manganite is expected to maintain a short distance between 
O2–O2 positions and to facilitate the jumping for oxygen ions. In 
contrast, a significant enlargement of polyhedra along ab plane caused 
by an increase in iron concentration seems to be a key factor for 
hampered ionic diffusion and impaired kinetics of oxygen exchange with 
gaseous media. 
4. Conclusion 
In summary, the layered perovskites PrBaMn2–xFexO6–δ are synthe­
sized via combined glycine-glycerin-nitrate precursor route. The single 
phase iron-doped specimens are shown to maintain invariably tetrag­
onal structure at heating in the air from room temperature to 1000 ◦C as 
observed for undoped manganite earlier. The iron solubility limit is 
found to be x ≥1.0. Increasing of the iron concentration is shown to 
decrease in the specific surface area and to suppress of anisotropic 
thermal expansion in parent manganite. The iron-doped oxide 
PrBaMnFeO6-δ with highest iron concentration exhibits enhanced phase 
stability both in oxidizing and reducing atmospheres compared to other 
perovskites under study. A combination of moderate TEC values of 
PBMFO with enhanced stability under both reductive and oxidative at­
mospheres makes PBMFO solid solutions a good candidate for solid 
oxide fuel cell oxygen electrodes. The solid solutions are characterized 
by large oxygen storage capacity and show reversible oxygen exchange 
when switching gas phase between air and 5% H2 in Ar accompanied by 
a transition from ‘O6’ to ‘O5’ form, respectively. The growth of iron 
content leads to a decrease in the reversibility of oxygen intake/release 
processes which is related with narrowing in the channels for oxygen 
diffusion caused by suppression of anisotropy in thermal expansion 
under doping. Due to differences in oxidation/reduction kinetics of the 
manganese and iron ions the oxygen exchange occurs in several steps. 
The most acceptable oxide for application in CLOU processes is 
considered to be PrBaMn1.33Fe0.67O6-δ, conjoining both increased phase 
stability and sufficient rates of redox processes.
