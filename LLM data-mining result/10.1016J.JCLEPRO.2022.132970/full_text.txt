A B S T R A C T   
The influence of alkali earth metals (AEMs, including Ca, Sr and Ba) doping on the performance of CuO/Al2O3 
oxygen carrier (OC) in chemical looping combustion (CLC) was investigated. The OCs using solid-state synthesis 
method were evaluated in a fluidized bed reactor using municipal solid waste (MSW) syngas as fuel for 50 redox 
cycles. It was found that the doping of AEMs enhanced the CLC performance on CuO/Al2O3. XRD character­
ization and TGA analysis showed that aluminates of AEMs were formed in the doped OCs leading to the 
segregation of CuO from CuAl2O4 spinel, thereby promoting the redox kinetics. The OCs doped with AEMs 
exhibited enhanced stability and agglomeration resistance throughout multiple redox cycles in the fluidized bed 
experiments. CuO/Al2O3 containing 40 wt% CuO suffered from severe agglomeration and deactivation during 
initial 5 cycles, while the OCs with Ba, Sr and Ca doping respectively deferred the agglomeration to 15-20th, 20- 
25th and >50th cycle. 5–10 wt% Ca-doping content exhibited great stability without deactivation or agglom­
eration issues during 50 cycles, but excess Ca doping (≥20 wt%) showed severe agglomeration. The agglom­
eration was attributed to the outward migration of Cu/CuO, leading to the segregation from CuAl2O4 and surface 
enrichment. The enhanced agglomeration resistance with AEM doping was mainly attributed to outward 
migration of AEM species and inward migration of Cu species during the synthesis process.   
1. Introduction 
With the urgent need to address global warming and climate change, 
carbon capture, utilization and storage (CCUS) technologies are 
attracting intense attention to achieve carbon-neutral economies (Bae 
et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2020a; Sunny et al., 2020). Chemical looping 
combustion (CLC) is a promising technology to pre-concentrate CO2 
from carbonaceous fuel combustion in order to ease its capture and 
storage (Buelens et al., 2019; Ishida and Jin, 1997; Zhu et al., 2020). CLC 
separates the combustion process into two reactions in fuel reactor (FR) 
and air reactor (AR), between which an oxygen carrier (OC) used as 
oxygen medium is circulated. Without the need of using oxygen from air 
for combustion of fuel, the carbonaceous fuels can be oxidized into 
N2-free flue gas in FR, thereby producing pure CO2 stream after water 
condensation. Moreover, as compared with the conventional oxy- and 
post-combustion carbon capture processes, CLC has a lower cost and 
footprint for achieving CO2 capture (Lyngfelt, 2020). 
The selection of solid OC is one of the cornerstones of CLC, in which 
excellent redox activity, stability and mechanical strength are required, 
especially for fluidized-bed operation. Oxides of transition metals, 
including Fe, Ni, Co, Cu and Mn (Adanez et al., 2012; Lin et al., 2020; 
Man et al., 2020), have been widely applied to CLC process. Cu-based 
OCs are attractive candidates owing to their excellent redox activity, 
high oxygen transport capacity and exothermic reaction with fuels 
making it easier for auto-thermal operation (Imtiaz et al., 2013). 
Furthermore, Cu-based OCs can release gaseous oxygen at >700 ◦C 
making them possible to achieve high combustion performance for solid 
fuels (Gay´an et al., 2012; Imtiaz et al., 2014). Nevertheless, the

challenge of Cu-based OCs is the low Tammann temperature of CuO and 
Cu, leading to severe agglomeration, deactivation, and even fluidization 
failure of OC particles. Therefore, homogenous mixing with an inert 
support (including Al2O3, MgAl2O4, SiO2 and ZrO2, etc. (Imtiaz et al., 
2021; Miao et al., 2022)) is required to minimize the growth and binding 
of Cu/CuO grains thereby avoiding the sintering issues. Al2O3 is the most 
widely-used support for Cu-based OCs due to its abundance and rela­
tively low cost. Al2O3-supported CuO OCs have also been successfully 
prepared in industrial scale, which were used in the pilot-scale inter­
connected fluidized bed CLC units (Cabello et al., 2016; Luis et al., 
2007). 
Regarding the type of fuels, municipal solid waste (MSW) and 
biomass derived gaseous fuels generally contain acidic gases (i.e., HCl 
and H2S), which could cause corrosion of the heat recovery system 
(Chen et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2020b) and additional costs for the 
downstream gas cleaning equipment. Efforts have been made on the 
removal of acidic gases through loading alkali and alkaline earth metals 
(AAEMs) on OCs. Fe2O3/Al2O3 loading with K2O, Na2O and CaO can be 
used as OC and HCl sorbent for simultaneous CLC and dechlorination, 
while the loaded AAEMs also promoted the CLC process (Wang and 
Zhao, 2015). More recent studies have shown that the loading of alka­
line earth metals (AEMs, including Ca, Sr and Ba) on iron ore not only 
effectively absorbed HCl (Liu et al., 2021a; Wang et al., 2020a, 2020b) 
and H2S (Wang et al., 2021) from MSW syngas or flue gas, but also 
enhanced the reactivity of iron ore with syngas through the electron 
donation effect. The HCl removal ability and the reactivity enhancement 
of AEMs followed the order of Ba > Sr > Ca (Liu et al., 2021b). More 
recently, it was found that the Ba and Sr addition on CuO/MgAl2O4 
caused the formation of Cu2MgO3 slightly reducing the redox activity of 
OC, but exhibited negligible effects on their reactivity with MSW syngas 
(Liu et al., 2022). In short, AAEMs facilitated effective removal of acidic 
gases, and exhibited positive effects on the reactivity of Fe-based OCs 
with fuels by acting as electron donors (Aika et al., 1990), but they might 
affect the Cu-based OCs indirectly depending on the type of supports. 
In case of the widely-used CuO/Al2O3 OCs, there are few studies 
focusing on the effects of AAEMs on their reactivity. It was evidenced 
that the doping of Na + could prevent the formation of CuAl2O4 spinel by 
the reaction between CuO and Al2O3 during synthesis, leading to its 
better reactivity with fuels (Imtiaz et al., 2016). K+ incorporation in 
CuO/Al2O3 could also reduce the propensity of coke deposition (Imtiaz 
et al., 2012a). However, alkali metals are not ideal sorbents for 
high-temperature acidic gas removal due to the volatilization of pro­
duced NaCl and KCl (Ge et al., 2019), whereas the effect of AEMs on the 
reactivity and stability of CuO/Al2O3 OCs is still unknown. In addition, 
the aluminates of AEMs can also be formed during high-temperature 
calcination (Garc´es et al., 2012; Sathaporn and Sutham, 2012; Shiri 
et al., 2014) in synthesis process, meaning that AEMs can possibly have 
similar effects on CuO/Al2O3 as alkali metals, avoiding the formation of 
CuAl2O4 spinel. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effect of 
AEMs doping on the reactivity and stability CuO/Al2O3 OCs during cy­
clic CLC process. CuO/Al2O3 OCs with and without the loading of Ca, Sr 
and Ba were prepared by solid-state synthesis, and their reactivity and 
stability in the CLC of syngas were evaluated by thermogravimetric 
analysis (TGA) and fluidized bed tests. The morphology and phase 
evolution of OCs and possible mechanism of AEMs during CLC process 
were discussed by analyzing their physiochemical characteristics. 
2. Materials and methods 
The OCs were prepared by solid state synthesis. The feedstock CuO 
(Sigma, <10 μm), γ-Al2O3 (commercial catalyst support) and AEM car­
bonates (i.e., CaCO3, SrCO3 and BaCO3) in certain ratios were mixed in a 
beaker, then the mixtures were further mixed with deionized water 
(solid:water = 5 g:1 ml) and ground in a planetary ball mill (Retsch 
PM100) for 30 min. The generated paste was dried and calcined at 
950 ◦C for 5 h. Subsequently, the calcined materials were crushed and 
sieved to 150–350 μm. OCs with 40 wt%, 30 wt% CuO and different Ca 
contents were prepared. The prepared OCs with labels are listed in 
Table 1. 
The redox activity and stability of the OCs during CLC were evalu­
ated in a fluidized bed reactor equipped with a gas switching system 
(Fig. 1). The reactor was a quartz tube with the inner diameter of 11 mm. 
The gas reactants were controlled by a group of mass flow controllers 
(MFCs), followed by pre-heating to 100 ◦C, and the gas products were 
analyzed in a gas analyzer (SICK, GME 810) after filtration and 
condensation. In each run, 5 g of OC was loaded in the reactor, and 
heated to 850 ◦C in air atmosphere to avoid the decomposition of Cu 
species (e.g., 4CuO → 2Cu2O + O2). The reduction stage lasted for 5 min, 
and the gas reactant (total flow 375 ml/min) was a simulated syngas 
stream containing 13.3 vol% CO, 13.3 vol% H2, 13.3 vol% CO2, 3.2 vol% 
CH4 and 56.8 vol% N2. The OC was oxidized by 400 ml/min air for 10 
min. Between reduction and oxidation stage, the reactor was purged 
with 300 ml/min N2 for 3 min. After multiple-cycle reaction, the OC was 
cooled down at air atmosphere to avoid its decomposition.

3. Results and discussion 
In this section, the findings on the effects of AEMs on CuO/Al2O3 OC 
were discussed through material characterizations and reactivity tests, 
and the mechanism of this effect was further discussed. 
3.1. Characterizations of fresh OCs 
The crystalline phase composition of the fresh OCs is shown in 
Fig. 2a. 4Cu6Al was mainly composed of CuAl2O4 (PDF #33–0448), 
while CuO and Al2O3 were not observed. With the loading of Ca, Ca–Al 
mixed oxide CaAl4O7 (PDF #23–1037) was formed, and the Cu species 
included CuO (PDF #45–1548) and CuAl2O4. Similarly, the doping of Sr 
and Ba resulted in the formation of Sr–Al oxide SrAl2O4 (PDF 
#34–03479) and Ba–Al oxide BaAl12O19 (PDF #26–0135). Therefore, 
the formation of the aluminates inhibited the formation of CuAl2O4 
spinel from CuO. 
Fig. 2b shows the CO-TPR profiles of the fresh OCs. The reduction 
peaks of 4Cu6Al were located at 560 ◦C and 680 ◦C. With the loading of 
AEMs, a reduction peak at 200–300 ◦C was observed, besides the 
reduction peak at >500 ◦C. According to the reported reduction tem­
perature of CuO by CO (160–240 ◦C) (Jin and Datye, 2000; Lee and 
Huang, 2014; Li et al., 2009) and XRD patterns, the reduction peaks at 
low-temperature (200–300 ◦C) and high-temperature (>500 ◦C) were 
respectively attributed to the reduction of CuO and CuAl2O4 to Cu0. 
Therefore, the doping of AEMs enhanced the redox activity of OCs by 
inhibiting CuO converting into CuAl2O4. In addition, compared with 
4Cu5Al1Ba and 4Cu5Al1Ca, 4Cu5Al1Sr had a smaller reduction peak at 
200–300 ◦C, and the intensity ratio CuO/CuAl2O4 was lower corre­
spondingly. Due to the formation of aluminates with different AEM/Al 
molar ratios (Ca:Al = 1:4, Sr:Al = 1:2, Ba:Al = 1:12), the CuO amount of 
OCs followed the order of 4Cu5Al1Ba > 4Cu5Al1Ca > 4Cu5Al1Sr. 
3.2. Effect of AEM on the reactivity of OC in fluidized bed 
The evolved gas products using 4Cu6Al, 4Cu5Al1Ca, 4Cu5Al1Sr and 
4Cu5Al1Ba from selected cycles are shown in Fig. 3. CH4 was selected to 
present the reactivity due to much higher activation energy than CO and 
H2 during combustion (Monazam et al., 2013, 2015). At the 1st cycle, 
CO2 concentrations did not decline until the input gas switched to N2, 
suggesting that nearly all the combustible gases were converted to CO2 
and H2O. CH4 concentration started to increase after several minutes of 
reduction stage due to the consumption of high-activity lattice oxygen in 
OCs. At the 5th cycle, CO2 concentration started to decrease before the 
end of reduction stage when 4Cu6Al was used, indicating that the ox­
ygen capacity of 4Cu6Al had an obvious decline. In the meantime, CH4 
concentration started to increase at the early reduction stage, suggesting 
the activity of 4Cu6Al decreased significantly. The decrease of CH4 
concentration at late reduction stage could be attributed to the reaction 
temperature increase causing by the exothermic nature of Cu-based OC 
reduction, which facilitated the conversion of CH4 to CO2 in kinetics 
(CO2 concentration started to decrease after ~50 s). In addition, the O2 
concentration started to increase earlier than that with other OCs during 
oxidation stage, although its reduction level was almost the same as 
other OCs (same CO2 profile as other OCs), suggesting the reactivity of 
reduced 4Cu6Al with O2 also declined. Nevertheless, the gas profiles 
using other OCs were still similar to those at the 1st cycle. At the 20th 
cycle, the variations of CO2, O2 and CH4 concentrations using 4Cu6Al 
and 4Cu5Al1Ba were similar. Thus, 4Cu5Al1Ba was also encountering 
the deactivation issue. Correspondingly, the 4Cu6Al and 4Cu5Al1Ba 
exhibited sticking behavior between particles according to pictures in 
Fig. S1. Therefore, the deactivation of OCs was attributed to the 
agglomeration of particles. During 30–50 cycles, 4Cu5Al1Sr gradually 
deactivated according to CH4 concentrations variation at reduction 
stage. The particle bridging did not appear after 20 cycles but was 
obvious after 50 cycles (Fig. S1). For 4Cu5Al1Ca, the redox activity was

The average CH4 concentrations and CO2 selectivities during 
reduction stage using different OCs are shown in Fig. 4. Due to the 
agglomeration and deactivation of 4Cu6Al, the unconverted CH4 
amounts rapidly increased at the initial 5 cycles. Correspondingly, the 
CO2 selectivity declined sharply from ~99% to ~84% at 1-5th cycle, due 
to the decreased CO conversion degree (according to the higher average 
CO concentrations in Fig. S2), and remained almost unchanged during 
the remaining cycles. For the OCs with Ba and Sr, CH4 concentrations 
gradually decreased during the initial 15 cycles, then sharply increased 
due to the agglomeration. The variations of CH4 amounts and CO2 se­
lectivities exhibited that 4Cu5Al1Sr and 4Cu5Al1Ba started to agglom­
erate at 20-25th and 15-20th cycle, respectively. After getting 
agglomerated, their CO2 selectivities and unconverted CH4 amounts 
were stable and comparable. The CO2 selectivities of both OCs were 
around 90–95%. Regarding 4Cu5Al1Ca, CH4 average concentrations did 
not present any remarkable increase during 50 cycles, and the CO2 se­
lectivities were ~99.5%. In summary, 4Cu6Al, 4Cu5Al1Ba, 4Cu5Al1Sr 
and 4Cu5Al1Ca started to deactivate at 1-5th, 15-20th, 20-25th and >50 
cycles, respectively, which agrees well with increasing defluidization 
resistance discussed above. 
To discuss the effect of alkali earth metal addition on agglomerated- 
free OCs, the OCs with lower CuO (30 wt%) content were evaluated. As 
can be seen in Fig. S3, CH4 concentrations were not observed at the early 
reduction stage, indicating that the OCs did not suffer any deactivation 
resulted from agglomeration. Among different OCs, there was no sig­
nificant difference for the variations of CO2, CH4 and O2 concentrations. 
Fig. 4c illustrates that all the OCs had comparable unconverted CH4 
amounts during 50 cycles, indicating the negligible influence of AEMs 
on the redox activity. According to Figure 4d, 3Cu7Al, 3Cu6Al1Ca and 
3Cu6Al1Sr had comparable CO2 selectivities, while 3Cu6Al1Ba had 
slightly lower CO2 selectivities than other OCs, suggesting its lower 
oxygen capacity for syngas combustion. Therefore, except Ba, AEMs had 
negligible influence on the reactivity with syngas for agglomeration-free 
CuO/Al2O3 OC. It is remarkable that although the CO2 selectivities were 
lower than those in Fig. 4b due to lower Cu content, the unconverted 
CH4 amounts were comparable to the free-agglomerated cases in Fig. 4a. 
This suggests that low CuO content mainly influenced the oxygen 
transport capacity, instead of the reactivity with CH4. 
Considering the above results, Ca doping had the best anti- 
agglomeration effect on CuO/Al2O3 OC, thus the Ca loading content 
was further studied. According to Fig. 4e, the unconverted CH4 content 
using OCs with different Ca contents followed the order of 4Cu3Al3Ca >
4Cu4Al2Ca > 4Cu5Al1Ca ≈4Cu55Al05Ca, and 4Cu3Al3Ca and 
4Cu4Al2Ca had a sharp increase during 5-10th cycle. From the 
perspective of gas concentration variation (in Fig. S4), 4Cu3Al3Ca and 
4Cu4Al2Ca also exhibited the relatively lower reactivity with CH4. CH4 
signal gradually shifted to earlier reduction stage for 4Cu3Al3Ca and 
4Cu4Al2Ca at the initial 30 and 20 cycles, respectively. Correspond­
ingly, the 4Cu3Al3Ca suffered from serve sintering issues after 20 or 50 
cycles (see Fig. S1). Therefore, high Ca loading amount resulted in 
agglomeration and deactivation issues for CuO/Al2O3 OC. It also led to 
the lower CO2 selectivities (Fig. 4f) of 4Cu3Al3Ca and 4Cu4Al2Ca, 
which were both 98–99% during 50 cycles. The worse performance of 
4Cu3Al3Ca and 4Cu4Al2Ca was due to the insufficient Al2O3 support for 
Cu-based OCs with lower porosity, and CaO/CaCO3 was not an effective 
support for Cu-based OCs. 
It is noted that the unconverted CH4 amounts had a slight decrease 
with the cycle number, except at the stage that the OCs were getting 
sintered. Other studies also exhibited increasing reduction rate of Al2O3/ 
CuO with cycle number (Imtiaz et al., 2012b). One possible reason is 
that the gradual segregation of CuO from CuAl2O4 enhanced the kinetics 
of reduction, though the segregation could lead to the surface CuO 
enrichment forming the dense surface thereby weakening its contact 
with gas reactants. 
According to the reactivity tests, although AEM species are not 
common supports for OCs due to lower Tammann temperature, the 
substitution of small amounts of Al2O3 with AEMs could enhance the 
stability of CuO-based OCs and alleviate the defluidization during 
multiple redox cycles.

3.3. TGA analysis on the reactivity of OCs 
Isothermal TGA was applied to investigate the effect of AEM on CuO/ 
Al2O3 redox activity through analyzing the oxygen capacity and reaction 
rate, and the results are shown in Fig. 5 and Table 2. In N2 atmosphere, 
4Cu6Al and 3Cu7Al had a slight mass loss owing to the low kinetics of 
CuAl2O4 decomposition into Cu + -containing compounds and release of 
gaseous oxygen. The oxygen uncoupling capacities of OCs with AEMs 
were much higher than that of 4Cu6Al and 3Cu7Al, due to the presence 
of CuO according to the XRD results (Fig. 2). At CO/N2 atmosphere, the 
mass loss rate curves showed that there was an additional peak at the 
early stage of reduction after AEM loading. This suggests that the for­
mation of CuO also accelerated the reaction between OCs and CO, 
indicating the enhancement of redox activity of OCs. Comparing 
different AEMs loading, the oxygen uncoupling capacities of Sr-doped 
OCs were significantly lower than those with Ca and Ba doping. It was 
resulted from the weaker interaction of Sr with CuO/Al2O3, according to 
the discussion in the XRD and TPR results (Fig. 2). 
For the OCs with different Ca doping amounts shown in Fig. 5, higher 
Ca doping led to the larger oxygen uncoupling capacity and total oxygen 
capacity. It was attributed to the higher relative Cu weight fraction since 
more CaCO3 decomposed during calcination process. In addition, higher 
Ca loading amount led to OUC/TOC ratio close to 1:2, suggesting CuO 
was gradually becoming the main redox active phase instead of 
CuAl2O4. This is consistent with the XRD patterns (Fig. S7), where the 
diffraction peaks of CuAl2O4 gradually disappeared with the higher Ca 
content. 
3.4. Physicochemical property variations of OCs 
Fig. 6 illustrates the surface morphologies of different OCs after 50 
redox cycles. The surface of 4Cu6Al particles was covered by a dense 
layer mainly composed by Cu species according to the EDS mapping. 
Compared to the fresh 4Cu6Al OC (see Fig. S5) in which Cu and Al were 
almost evenly distributed, the Cu species tended to aggregate on the 
particle surface, acting as sticking bridges between particles, thereby 
leading to agglomeration and defluidization during redox cycles. With 
the doping of Ca (shown in Fig. 6b), the surface did not exhibit the dense 
structure composed by Cu species. This indicates that Ca doping effec­
tively alleviated the enrichment of Cu/CuO on the particle surface thus 
avoiding the agglomeration and defluidization. With Sr doping, “lumps” 
with sizes 2–4 μm formed by CuO were generated on the surface, and a 
significant segregation of CuO and Al2O3 grains was observed in EDS 
mapping. It still faced defluidization issues (see Fig. S1) although the 
CuO agglomeration seemed not severe as 4Cu6Al. The surface of 
4Cu5Al1Ba particles was also covered by CuO, suggesting the agglom­
eration after 50 redox cycles. Similarly, significant CuO segregation and 
enrichment were observed on the 4Cu3Al3Ca surface. The surface 
morphologies of cycled OCs further suggest that the AEMs had the 
function of alleviating the agglomeration of CuO/Al2O3 OC during redox 
cycles. 
Fig. 6f shows the surface areas and pore volumes of fresh and used 
OCs. The doping by AEMs significantly decreased the surface area and 
pore volume of OCs compared to 4Cu6Al. This was due to the substi­
tution of the high-porosity Al2O3 by the AEMs. Nevertheless, the 
porosity of 4Cu6Al declined sharply after some redox cycles, with the 
surface area decrement of 69.3% after 20 cycles. With the doping by 
AEMs, the surface areas were decreased by 53.0%, 34.1% and 48.5% for 
4Cu5Al1Ca, 4Cu5Al1Sr and 4Cu5Al1Ba, respectively. However, the 
surface area and pore volume of 4Cu6Al were still higher than those of 
AEM-doped OCs after 50 cycles, although it exhibited more severe 
agglomeration on the surface morphology. The possible reason was that 
the physical adsorption (in N2 adsorption characterization) was domi­
nated by Al2O3 support due to its high porosity as feedstock, and there

For the Cu-based OCs, the agglomeration tendency during multiple 
redox cycles is relevant to the Kirkendall effect (Mishra and Li, 2018; 
Smigelskas, 1947). During redox reaction, metal atoms/cations tend to 
diffuse outward to contact with reducing gas or oxygen, if the structure 
has a relatively high energy barrier for the diffusion of oxygen anion,

In supported Cu-based OCs, due to the difference of diffusion rates be­
tween Cu2+/Cu0 and the support, Cu species tend to migrate outward 
during both reduction and oxidation stage, resulting in Cu/CuO 
enrichment on the surface and even the formation of sintered bridges 
between particles (Donat et al., 2018; Imtiaz et al., 2021). In the current 
study, the EDS mapping (Fig. 6) exhibited the accumulation of Cu spe­
cies (without Al) on the surface of particles, confirming the outward 
diffusion mechanism of the OC agglomeration. However, it is difficult to 
compare the migration degree of Cu/CuO with and without the doping 
of AEMs, and conclude whether AEMs alleviated the agglomeration by 
inhibiting Cu/CuO migration, two methods were used to quantify the 
Cu/CuO migration degree of the OCs.  
(1) Since Cu/CuO migration led to the enrichment of Cu element on 
the particle surface, the migration degree can be quantified by its 
enrichment degree (ED), which is evaluated by comparing CuO 
content (wt.%) on the OC surface before and after redox cycles: 
ED = R50,CuO −R0,CuO
(6)  
where R0,CuO and R50,CuO are the CuO contents of OCs on the surface 
before and after 50 cycles, and obtained from EDS results of the whole 
OC particles, wt.%. Due to the large errors in EDS results, 6 particles 
were sampled to measure the elemental compositions on the surface 
(~1000 nm penetration depth under 20 kV electron acceleration 
voltage).  
(2) The outward migration of Cu2+/Cu0 during reduction (CuAl2O4 
+ fuel → Cu0 + Al2O3 + CO2 + H2O; CuO + fuel → Cu0 + CO2 +
H2O) and oxidation (Cu0 + Al2O3 + O2 → CuAl2O4; Cu0 + O2 → 
CuO) stage led to the uneven distribution of Cu2+ and Al2O3, 
hence CuAl2O4 was unable to be regenerated completely after 
oxidation stage. This resulted in the segregation of CuO from 
CuAl2O4 spinel. The segregation behavior was observed in XRD 
patterns (Fig. 7), TPR (Fig. 8) and isothermal TGA results (Fig. 9). 
Since CuO segregation from CuAl2O4 is caused by the migration 
of Cu/CuO, the migration degree can be evaluated by comparing 
the amount of CuO segregated from CuAl2O4 before and after 
redox cycles: 
SD = m1,CuO −m0,CuO
m0,CuAl2O4
× 100%
(5)  
where SD is the segregation degree, %; m0,CuAl2O4 and m0,CuO are the mass 
of CuAl2O4 and CuO in fresh OCs, %; m1,CuO is the mass of CuO after 
redox cycles, %. Due to the extremely low conversion of CuAl2O4 
decomposition releasing O2, the mass of CuO and CuAl2O4 are respec­
tively calculated as the OUC and the difference between TOC and OUC. 
Both quantification methods have disadvantages in evaluating the 
Cu/CuO migration degree. The segregation degree did not describe the 
migration of free CuO, and the enrichment degree only described the 
migration of individual particles. Therefore, both methods were com­
bined to explain the effect of AEMs on the Cu/CuO outward migration. 
Fig. 10 compares the segregation and enrichment degrees of different 
OCs during redox cycles. With the cycle number, the CuO segregation 
gradually slow down. Ca-doped OCs exhibited lower CuO segregation 
degree than CuO/Al2O3 during 50 redox cycles, while Sr and Ba-doped 
OCs had higher segregation degrees after 50 cycles. The order of 
segregation degrees (4Cu5Al1Ca < 4Cu6Al < 4Cu5Al1Ba < 4Cu5Al1Sr) 
was consistent with that of surface enrichment degree (exactly, the 
enrichment degrees of OCs were comparable while considering the large 
errors). Therefore, AEM-doped OCs did not illustrate significantly lower 
Cu/CuO outward migration degree than CuO/Al2O3. The agglomeration 
resistance was not mainly attributed to the inhibition of AEMs on Cu 
outward migration. 
According to Fig. 10a, the surface CuO content of fresh 4Cu6Al was 
close to theoretical value (40 wt%, calculated by the feedstock compo­
sition of OCs), while that of fresh AEM-doped OCs was much lower than 
the theoretical values, as well as that of 4Cu6Al. At the same time, the 
surface AEM content was much higher than the theoretical values. This 
suggests that the calcination in the synthesis process drove the outward 
migration of AEM species and inward migration of Cu species, which 
eliminated the surface contact of Cu between OC particles thereby 
preventing the OCs from agglomeration and sintering during redox cy­
cles. The behavior is described in Fig. 11. The directional migration 
might be driven by the minimization of the surface energy of the whole 
system (Li et al., 2013). However, due to the deficiency of surface energy 
data of Cu and Ca species (i.e. CuO, CuAl2O4, AEM carbonates/ox­
ides/aluminates), the insight of the elemental directional diffusion need

4. Conclusion 
The effect of AEMs (Ca, Sr and Ba) on the performance of CuO/Al2O3 
OCs on CLC process was investigated. The doping of AEMs on CuO/ 
Al2O3 exhibited improvement of redox activity, stability and agglom­
eration resistance. XRD and TGA analysis showed that AEMs doping on 
CuO/Al2O3 led to the formation of aluminates of AEMs, thereby 
improved the redox kinetics by inhibiting CuO converting into CuAl2O4 
spinel. Fluidized bed test illustrated that AEMs doping enhanced the 
stability and alleviated the agglomeration issues of CuO/Al2O3 during 
multiple redox cycles. 4Cu6Al faced severe agglomeration and defluid­
ization issues at the initial 5 cycles, while the OCs with Ba, Sr and Ca 
doping delayed the agglomeration to 15-20th, 20-25th and >50th cycle,
