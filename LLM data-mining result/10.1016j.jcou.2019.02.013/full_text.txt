A B S T R A C T
Coupling Chemical Looping Reforming of methane with CO2-splitting reactions represents an attractive route to
achieve continuous syngas and CO productivities with reduced carbon footprint in a cyclic two-steps process.
Both reactions are performed in the presence of a metal oxide, which switches from reduced to oxidized in
successive stages. In this work, La1-xSrxFeO3 perovskites were selected, because the Sr/La ratio can be varied to
tune the redox capacity of the system. Reactivity of these materials were tested in isothermal cycles at 850 °C
with the aim of simultaneously maximize the conversion of CH4 and ensure the re-oxidation of the solid.
Although the highest redox capacity is observed for the material with higher Sr-content (x = 0.9), the best
performance is obtained for La0.9Sr0.1FeO3, which exhibits notable overall syngas yields per cycle: 12.8 mmol
H2 g−1 and 15.9 mmol CO g−1. Analyses of the reactivity of these perovskites reveal the important contribution
of both methane cracking and Boudouard reaction on the production of syngas, particularly for materials with
low Sr/La ratio. These two processes operate, respectively, in the reduction and oxidation stages, in such a way
that a signiﬁcant deactivation of the perovskites by carbon deposition is avoided. These ﬁndings highlight the
inﬂuence of aspects other than redox properties on the high temperature reactivity of La1-xSrxFeO3 perovskites in
cycles alternating CH4 and CO2. On the other hand, materials with high La/Sr ratio experienced signiﬁcant
changes in the phase composition after prolonged reduction in CH4. Nevertheless, these transformations are
reversible and, after re-oxidation, the recovered perovskite remains highly active, emphasizing the stability of
these reactive solids.
1. Introduction
Currently, the production of liquid fuels for the transport sector still
relies in more than 80% on fossil resources and, accordingly, environ-
mental problems linked to CO2 emissions tend to increase in parallel
with the growing millage of all kinds of vehicles [1]. Technological
alternatives for coping with this situation are focused on obtaining
sustainable fuels from abundant resources, in order to achieve a more
environmentally-friendly energy system. In this way, the development
of suitable transformation routes that can simultaneously integrate the
use of renewable energy and the valorization of greenhouse gases
streams are ideal for this purpose.
Recently, signiﬁcant advances towards this goal have been achieved
using diﬀerent metal oxides as CeO2 or perovskites, as oxygen carriers
in thermochemical cycles for CO2 splitting powered by concentrating
solar energy [2–5]. Although this process primarily produces CO and
H2, liquid fuels can be obtained subsequently by feeding the obtained
gas blend to well-established Fischer-Tropsch units [6,7]. However,
despite the remarkable high solar-to-chemical energy yield of this
technology, which reached 5.25% for CO2 splitting with an optimized
4 kW solar reactor [3], the complexity of operating at temperatures as
high as 1500 °C and applying high radiation ﬂuxes, remain as an im-
portant hurdle for a wide commercial implementation of this route for
solar fuels production. Alternatively, hybridization of thermochemical
cycles with abundant hydrocarbons such as methane can provide an
interesting trade-oﬀ, reducing the severity of the operation conditions
and keeping the overall sustainability of the process attractive, despite
increasing the overall carbon footprint of the process due to the in-
troduction of this fossil feedstock.
With this aim, the concept of chemical-looping reforming of me-
thane (CLRM) coupled to CO2 or H2O splitting reaction in successive
cycles has arisen as a promising route for renewable fuel production
[2,8]. In this cyclic reaction scheme, ﬁrst methane is converted into H2
and CO with the oxygen provided by the endothermic reduction of the

Due to the reducing capacity of methane operation temperatures of

direct CO2 and H2O splitting under concentrating solar power.
Furthermore, in contrast to the conventional catalytic reforming of
methane, this process is less sensitive to coke formation because cycles
are self-regenerative. Accordingly, CLRM does not require an excess of
oxidizer for preventing carbon deposition and avoiding the corre-
sponding energy penalty [5,6]. Nevertheless, deactivation can still pose
limitations in many oxide systems, not only because of incomplete coke
removal, but also due to structural and/or morphological modiﬁcations
induced by the thermal cycles. Therefore, the materials used as oxygen
carriers for this process must be carefully selected to have an improved
stability. Interestingly, solarization of the CLRM route is possible and a
theoretical solar-to-chemical eﬃciency as high as 54% has been esti-
mated [10]. These positive characteristics have recently boosted the
interest of this route for solar fuel production [11].
The principal challenges of combining CLRM with CO2 splitting are
achieving high conversions, while maximizing H2 and CO selectivity
and reducing carbon deposition [8]. Therefore, the development of
adequate redox systems must be focused on oxides with elevated
structural stability and good redox capacity, providing high oxygen
exchange during the reactions. Crucially, these oxides must be also
thermodynamically capable of splitting H2O and CO2 in the reduced
state or, in other words, the ΔG of reaction (2) must be negative at
temperatures lower than those required for the activation of reaction
(1). Several materials, which have been also utilized in the thermo-
chemical redox cycles for CO2 and H2O splitting [12–18], accomplish
these characteristics and they have been tested in the CLRM process
with promising results. These oxygen carriers include transition metal
oxides [19,20], ceria-based oxides [9,21,22], mixed metal oxides such
as

these modiﬁcations of the composition can be used to modulate its
properties to better match the targeted application. Furthermore, it is
worth mentioning that the temperature of reduction step (the one with

such as La1-xSrxMn1-xAlxO3 than for ceria, which is currently the
benchmark material for thermochemical cycles for solar fuels produc-
tion [32,33].
Interestingly, perovskites from the family La1-xSrxFeO3 (LSF) have
shown an excellent performance for Chemical Looping Reforming of
methane. Recently, this process has been also combined with H2O
splitting at temperatures in the 900–1000 °C range [25,28,30,31]. LSF
materials present high redox capacities, which has been shown in
previous reports that can be enhanced with increasing the Sr2+content
[34]. Using these oxides, methane reforming was studied at lab scale by
temperature programed reduction achieving conversions up to 90%
[35,36] and also using circulating beds for continuous operation [37].
These assays show the high stability and elevated selective for syngas
production [38]. Tests combining CLMR with water splitting were
carried out in lab scale facilities using either thermogravimetric ana-
lysis or pulse reactions. Results obtained with these tests provide a
proof of concept, but are not ideal to achieve high syngas production.
In this work the coupling of CLMR with CO2-splitting using La1-x
SrxFeO3 perovskites was explored systematically using a ﬁxed-bed,
continuous-ﬂow reactor operating isothermally at 850 °C. In this study
particular attention has been paid to the stability of the solids and to the
role of Sr2+ doping on the redox properties of the materials, in order to
establish possible correlations with syngas production during CLRM
and CO2 splitting cycles. Furthermore, the observed variations on the
selectivity provide valuable information about the contribution of ad-
ditional processes to syngas production, such as methane decomposi-
tion and the disproportionation of CO (Boudouard reaction).
2. Experimental section
2.1. Perovskites synthesis
A modiﬁed Pechini method [39,40] was used for the synthesis of
perovskites with compositions La1-xSrxFeO3 (x = 0; 0.1; 0.3; 0.5; 0.7;
0.9; 1). The procedure begins with the addition of the corresponding
metal nitrates, La(NO3)3·6H2O (Sigma Aldrich, 99% purity), Sr(NO3)2
(Sigma Aldrich, 99% purity) and Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (Scharlau, 98% purity)
in a citric acid (CA) solution in distilled water in the amount required to
obtain a molar ratio of 2:1 (CA:Metal). The solution was stirred at 70 °C
for 3 h. After that, ethylene glycol (EG) was added to the solution in a
3:2 ratio (CA:EG) and the mixture was stirred at 90 °C for 2 h. The re-
sulting gel was dried at 200 °C (heating rate: 2 °C min−1) for 3 h. The
obtained resin was ground and subsequently calcined at 1100 °C (5 °C
min−1) for 10 h under static air. The so-obtained samples were labelled
according to the Sr content, X, as L(10-X)SXF.
2.2. Characterization
Elemental analyses of the samples were carried out by Inductively
Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry, (ICP-OES) using a
Perkin Elmer Optima 3300 DV equipment, after the digestion in acidic
media of the solids, using an Anton Paar Multi-wave 3000 microwave
oven. Speciﬁc surface area, SBET, was calculated from the N2 adsorp-
tion-desorption at 77 K (-196 °C) using a Quantachrome QuadraSorb-S
equipment, after degassing the samples at 250 °C for 12 h. The crys-
talline structure was analyzed with a Philips PW 3040/00 X’Pert MPD/
MRD X-ray diﬀractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) at a
scanning rate of 0.2° s−1 in the range of 2θ = 20-80°. SEM images were
obtained by using a XL 30 ESEM Philips microscope. Raman spectra of
the selected solids were acquired with a Renishaw micro-Raman System
1000, provided with a LEICA DMILM microscope with a 20× objective
lens, using an Argon laser (514 nm) for excitation, accumulating 10
scans with 30 s acquisition time.
Redox capacity of the samples was studied by thermogravimetric
analysis using a SDT Q-600 from TA Instruments, placing ˜20 mg of
powdered sample into 90 μl alumina crucibles. The samples were he-
ated to 1000 °C at 20 °C min−1 in an 100 ml min−1 Ar ﬂow (reduction
step) and then cooled down to 600 °C. Then, pure air (Praxair, 99.99%)
was injected (oxidation step). Extended 10-cycles experiments were
performed under the same conditions.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Physicochemical characterization of the LSF perovskites
Synthesized LSF perovskites were characterized by diﬀerent tech-
niques in order to determine the eﬀect of Sr2+ introduction in their
physicochemical properties. Composition of the LSF samples was de-
termined by ICP-OES analyses, conﬁrming that metal proportions of the
perovskites are very close to the targeted composition, although a slight
excess of Fe was observed in most of the samples, as it is detailed in
Table S1. On the other hand, the N2-isotherms revealed a very low
adsorption capacity with BET area values lower than 1.5 m2 g−1 in all
cases. These results are consistent with the expectations for these
oxides, which were annealed at very high temperature.
X-ray diﬀraction patterns of the perovskites are plotted in Fig. 1 and
they conﬁrm the formation of pure phases, without any observable
segregation of single metal oxides. Partial substitution of La3+ by Sr2+
induces changes in the crystalline structure due to the diﬀerences in the
ionic radii of these cations (0.136 nm for La3+ and 0.144 nm for Sr2+)
[42] and, more crucially, to the diﬀerences in the oxidation state.
Consequently, in order to maintain the electroneutrality of the lattice
both, formation of oxygen vacancies and partial oxidation of Fe3+ to
Fe4+ (0.0645 nm and 0.0585 nm, respectively) take place [43]. These
changes induce structural modiﬁcations that are revealed by the dif-
ferences in the shape of the peaks, and the displacement of the XRD
reﬂections to higher angles with increasing Sr content, as it can be
appreciated in the zoomed area of right panel of Fig. 1. This shift im-
plies a progressive contraction of the perovskite lattice, which can be
related to the increasing content of smaller size Fe4+ cation.
The progressive distortion of the perovskite lattice caused by
strontium-doping leads to a change of the crystalline symmetry, as it is
summarized in Table 1. Thus, LSF perovskites with Sr content between
x = 0-0.3 show orthorhombic structure (ICDD: 01-088-0641), while
materials with x > 0.3 present rhombohedral symmetry (ICDD: 01-082-
1962). Finally, SrFeO3 shows cubic structure (ICDD: 01-070-5776).
Similar behavior has been found in previous crystallographic study of
the La1-xSrxFeO3 system [44]. In addition, it can be observed that within
a given symmetry, the unit cell volume of La1-xSrxFeO3 perovskites
decreases with increasing the Sr content due to the formation of Fe4+
and oxygen vacancies, as mentioned before. Crystallite sizes of the
perovskites are in the 50–120 nm range as estimated by Scherrer
equation, but they do not show any apparent correlation with La/Sr
ratio.
SEM images of all the materials show agglomerates of several mi-
crons with irregular shapes, and no evident diﬀerences between them
can be appreciated (Figure S2). Particles of some of the samples present
channels with an average diameter of about 0.4 μm, which more likely
are formed due to gas released during the drying and calcination steps
of the synthesis procedure.
3.2. Oxygen storage capacity
As mentioned before, LSF perovskites present attractive redox ca-
pacities due to Sr2+ incorporation, which ease the formation of large
amount of oxygen vacancies that can favor oxygen exchange. In order
to evaluate the oxygen storage capacity (OSC) of these samples, ther-
mogravimetric tests under controlled conditions were performed.
Initially, the perovskites were heated in Ar ﬂow up to 1000 °C, followed
by an isotherm period of 30 min under the same atmosphere and ﬁnally
cooled down to 600 °C. Subsequently, the samples were exposed to air
ﬂow for re-oxidation. TGA results obtained for the diﬀerent LSF per-
ovskites are displayed in Figure S3. The initial mass loss event taking
place below 150 °C corresponds with the release of water and other
adsorbed impurities during the storage of these materials. Eﬀective
reduction of perovskites in inert gas stream starts above 400 °C in all
cases, although the onset temperature progressively increases, while the
weight loss diminishes with decreasing Sr content. In the extreme, the
samples with lower Sr content show a rather limited oxygen storage
capacity. For most of these materials oxygen is released continuously
until the ﬁnal temperature of 1000 °C is reached. A slow weight gain is
observed during the cooling down to 600 °C due to presence of O2 traces
(pO2 ˜500 Pa or 5·10−3 bars) in the Ar ﬂow, while the re-oxidation in air
is very quick and almost complete.
The non-stoichiometry change, Δδ, was calculated using Eq. (3)
from the thermogravimetric data of each sample, assuming that the
initial mass after low temperature desorption corresponds to δ = 0.
Results obtained for both reduction and oxidation steps are displayed in
the bar diagram of Fig. 2.
The amount of O2 interchanged by these perovskites during oxida-
tion and reduction is included in Table S2. These results corroborate
that the reduction capacity is noticeably enhanced with increasing the
Sr2+ content. On the other hand, while re-oxidation is basically com-
plete for all the samples within experimental error.
According to these data, the highest oxygen storage capacity under
these conditions correspond to the LSF perovskites with x ≥0.7, which
present δ higher than 0.35 for L1S9F sample. These values are com-
parable to those previously reported for LSF system [30] and sig-
niﬁcantly higher than for other redox perovskites. Those large non-
stoichiometry values are consistent with the charge imbalance gener-
ated from the partial substitution of La3+ by Sr2+, which results in an
enhancement of the oxidation capacity.
Assays consisting in 10 cycles under the same conditions were
performed with the materials that showed higher redox capacity in
order to determine the long term stability of the OSC. Fig. 3 displays the

This process leads to the formation of CO2 and H2O in approximately

with the more reactive surface oxygens. Further increment of the

Krenzke et al. [8] have compared the equilibrium composition for
the partial oxidation of methane as a function of the temperature and
the CH4:O2 ratio in homogeneous conditions without any catalyst.
Higher methane conversion is reached at higher temperatures and
lower ratios (larger O2 concentration), although under those conditions
CO2 and H2O, rather than syngas, are produced [11]. In the context of
CLRM, the reactivity of the redox solid can modulate the selectivity to
those processes as a consequence of the diﬀerences in the lability of the
oxygen available in the crystalline lattice. In this regards, Dai et al.
[35,45] found two diﬀerent types of oxygen by XPS analysis of the LSF
perovskite. As mentioned above, the ﬁrst one is strongly reactive
oxygen (or weakly bound oxygen), which is present in the surface and it
is consequence of the progressive oxidation of Fe3+ to Fe4+ by Sr
doping to compensate the charge imbalance. The second type of O2−
corresponds to more strongly bound species of the bulk, which can only
react when Fe3+ is further reduced. In the present case, due to the
higher reactivity of the oxide anions generated by introducing Sr in the
LaFeO3 lattice, the incorporation of this alkaline earth element facil-
itates the total oxidation of methane, yielding selectively CO2 and H2O
at the initial reduction step. Nevertheless correlation between the
oxygen storage capacity obtained from the thermogravimetric tests and
the intensity of the low temperature reduction in methane is not com-
plete. This suggests than additional factors such as diﬀerences in the
crystalline structure or surface area may also aﬀect the reactivity of LSF
perovskites.
3.4. Chemical looping reforming and CO2 splitting cycles
In order to evaluate the eﬀect of Sr2+doping on syngas production,
all the LSF samples were submitted to ﬁve redox cycles at 850 °C,
swinging the gas composition between 5% CH4 and 5% CO2 in Ar.
Working at isothermal conditions was preferred because it presents
advantages from the point of view of energy eﬃciency for solar fuels
production [12]. Previous studies have shown that methane conver-
sions up to 90% are achieved at temperatures of 900 °C using the L9S1F
perovskite [36], while basically full conversion can be obtained over LF
at the same temperature depending on space velocity [31]. On the other
hand, oxidation of the partially reduced perovskite with CO2 is rapid for
temperatures higher than 600 °C. Accordingly, it is expected that high
conversion for both methane and CO2 splitting can be achieved under
the conditions selected for these tests.

production of H2 was detected. Accordingly, the empty reactor pre-
sented some minor activity for methane decomposition but lower than
7% of the production obtained when the redox oxides were loaded. In
order to account only for the contribution of the perovskite, all these
results have been corrected by subtracting the activity of the blank
assay.
Time evolution of methane conversion during the redox cycles is
depicted in Fig. 6 for all the LSF perovskites. Redox cycles produce CO,
H2, along with minor amounts of CO2 and H2O during the reduction of
the perovskite in presence of methane. Following CLR of methane, CO2
is introduced in the second step of the cycles (appearing as the intervals
of 0% conversion in Fig. 6) to re-oxidize the perovskite and recover the
initial state. Accordingly, additional CO is produced during the re-oxi-
dation of the solid with CO2.
Sharp diﬀerences are observed in the behavior of these solids, in
such a way that those perovskites with low Sr2+ concentration show
higher methane conversion, reaching a maximum close to 85% for
L9S1F. In contrast, LSF samples with Sr content x ≥0.5 show a quite
limited reactivity, with conversion of methane lower than 20%.
Furthermore, the time evolution of methane conversion in the CLRM
intervals is also sharply diﬀerent depending on the perovskite compo-
sition. In this way, samples enriched in Sr show an initial maximum just
after methane introduction and subsequently conversion levels oﬀor
show a slight decrease, as it can be appreciated in Fig. 6B. The per-
ovskites with x < 0.3, except L9S1F, also show an initial peak of me-
thane conversion but, in contrast with the oxides with larger Sr content,
they subsequently show a continuous increase of methane consump-
tion. According to the MS analyses, the initial maximum of methane
conversion is partially due to the complete oxidation of methane, be-
cause it is accompanied by CO2 and H2O release. The selectivity of this
process may reach almost 50% for a very short period.
Diﬀerences in the behavior of the perovskites with varying Sr con-
tent are consistent with the results of CH4-TPR, and points out to the
existence of oxygen species with distinct reactivity. Partial oxidation of
methane takes place by surface reaction with the oxide ions diﬀused
thought the solid. In this respect, it appears that the presence of oxygen
ions with moderate reactivity may increase the selectivity to syngas
[30,37].
Fig. 7 plots the productions of H2 and CO for the diﬀerent per-
ovskites as a function of the successive reduction cycles in CH4. These
graphs reveal that three perovskites stand out among the other samples,
showing CO and H2 productions 3–6 times higher than the rest of the
oxides: LF, L9S1 and L7S3F. The highest syngas production corresponds
to the perovskite L9S1F, which yields up to 12.8 mmol H2 g−1 and
4.7 mmol CO g−1 per cycle. In contrast, perovskites with elevated Sr/La
ratio show more modest syngas productions, which reach the lowest
rate for sample L1S9F. On the other hand, the perovskite without lan-
thanum present an activity that is only slightly lower than that of
L5S5F. As it is displayed in Fig. 7, generation of syngas is higher in the
ﬁrst cycle in most of the cases.
This behavior indicates some structural and morphological changes
of the solid occur during the ﬁrst thermal cycle. Interestingly, per-
ovskites with lower Sr content show a moderate increment of the syngas
production from the second cycle onward. The reasons for that behavior
are related to the variation in the contribution of the diﬀerent reaction
pathways leading to CO and H2 production. In this way, the H2/CO
ratios obtained during methane reforming vary from 2.5 to 3 for all the
perovskites. Those values are signiﬁcantly higher than the expected
stoichiometric value of 2. This fact suggests that additional secondary
reactions are contributing to increase the yield of hydrogen. In this
respect, carbon deposition by methane decomposition could account for
these results because this process also yields hydrogen according to:
CH4 (g) →C(s) + 2 H2 (g)

example of the results obtained from these calculations for the per-

In addition, evidence of carbon deposition is also observed after
reaction, particularly for the perovskites with lowest Sr-content, as it
will be discussed later. As carbon can catalyze methane decomposition

samples with high Sr/La ratio, the relevance of methane decomposition
is much more limited, as it can be deduced from the H2/CO ratio closer
to 2.

3.5. Characterization of the perovskites after the redox cycles
After the redox cycles at 850 °C, the solids were cooled down in CO2
atmosphere in order to recover them in the initial oxidized state of the
perovskite. Diﬀraction patterns of samples after 5 cycles indicate that
the oxides with Sr content < 0.9 maintain the structure, showing dif-
fraction patterns characteristic of pure perovskite, as shown in Fig. 11a.
However, a more careful analysis reveals certain shift to lower 2θ an-
gles, as shown in the detail of Fig. 11b for the case of L7S3F sample.
This means that the perovskites have suﬀered a small increase of the
cell parameter due to the formation of oxygen vacancies. On the other
hand, perovskites L1S9F and SF have initially rhombohedric and cubic
structures, respectively, while after reaction they were transformed into
the orthorhombic brownmillerite Sr2Fe2O5 phase (ICDD: 01-071-3590).
In the case of L1S9F, a segregated LaFeO3 perovskite is also observed.
Nevertheless, it is important to note that despite these structural
changes, these solids still present high redox capacity, as could be
veriﬁed in the cycling reactions [38].
On the other hand, SEM images of the perovskites after the cycles
show, for most of the samples, a tendency to increase the porosity of the
particles. An example of that morphological evolution is shown in the
images of Fig. 12, which correspond to the L9S1 perovskite following
the 5 cycles in CH4 and CO2 atmospheres. This material displays a foam-
like appearance, showing a high density of channels with an average
diameter of about 0.5 μm. These open structures are most likely formed
due to the release of gases and they can favor a good reactivity of these
materials.
3.6. Exploring the reactivity of the fully reduced perovskites
In order to further understand the structural evolution of these
materials under reaction conditions, a long term test in CH4 ﬂow at
850 °C was performed with sample L9S1F, with the aim of achieving the
complete reduction of the perovskite. The evolution of the composition
of the outlet gas stream was followed by mass spectrometer (see Figure
S5). These results show more intense reduction event at 850 °C, which is
evidenced by an important consumption of methane for about 40 min,
which occurs with the simultaneous production of syngas and lower
concentrations of CO2 and H2O. A more detailed analysis of these data
reveals that there are at least two diﬀerentiated contributions to me-
thane conversion. The initial one should correspond to partial oxidation
of methane, while subsequently the H2/CO ratio increases above the
stoichiometric value of 2. This indicates that the contribution of me-
thane decomposition becomes progressively more important with the
time on the CH4 stream, as previously discussed.
In order to determine the structural changes induced by methane
reduction, Raman and XRD analysis of the solid recovered after this
treatment was undertaken. To avoid any possible re-oxidation, the
sample was cooled down to room temperature in methane ﬂow. Raman
spectra of the fresh and fully reduced perovskite are displayed in
Fig. 13.
Untreated perovskite shows only a broad and poorly deﬁned feature
centered at around 1050 cm−1, which could be related to surface car-
bonates. Following methane treatment this band becomes more intense
and slightly shifts to 990 cm−1, while new prominent features appear at
1340 cm−1 and 1570 cm−1, which can be assigned, respectively, to the
D and G bands of graphite [53]. The less intense band D is characteristic
of more disordered structures, while the higher intensity of band D
denotes the predominance of well-ordered sp2 structures of graphite.
Formation of these carbon deposits is consistent with methane de-
composition, as suggested by the extensive hydrogen release and, as
mentioned above, it can also catalyze further methane decomposition
[44].
Fig. 14 shows the comparison between the pattern of the sample
L9S1F before and after reaction and the diﬀerent phases identiﬁed.
The XRD results clearly indicate that the perovskite has been de-
composed into La2O3, Fe3C, graphite and SrCO3. Formation of this last
phase is most likely consequence of the secondary reaction of the CO2
released in the process with SrO produced by decomposition of the
perovskite. On the other hand, production of metal carbides by me-
thane treatment at high temperature has been also reported for Mn2O3/
YSZ [54]. Considering the catalytic properties of carbides it can be
speculated that they play an active role in CO2 reduction [55], although

under extended treatments in methane atmospheres at high tempera-
ture, which leads to the segregation of the single oxides and iron car-
bide, as well as to the formation of graphite. Nevertheless, an adequate
strategy for optimizing the duration of the cycles of operation can allow
controlling the CO/H2 ratio and, at the same time, can avoid the de-
gradation of the LSF perovskites. In particular, under the conditions
used on the present assays, shorter periods of about 15 min can be
adequate to reach high and stable productions of syngas, taking full
advantages of the redox properties of the with LSF perovskites, and
eluding an excessive built-up of carbon deposits. Surprisingly, despite
this structural degradation the initial perovskite is fully recovered fol-
lowing re-oxidization at high temperature. Even more remarkable is the
fact that activity of this reconstructed material is even higher than that
of the original perovskite. Accordingly, the LSF system appears as really
attractive in order to further develop a more sustainable syngas pro-
duction based on alternative cycles of methane partial oxidation and
CO2 splitting.
4. Conclusions
This work shows that perovskites of the La1-xSrxFeO3 (LSF) system
can be synthesized as single phases by the Pechini method following
calcination at 1100 °C. Speciﬁc surface areas of these mixed oxides
present very low values (< 1.2 m2 g−1), as expected for materials
synthetized at high temperature. Despite this fact, the notable redox
capacity
of
the
LSF
perovskites
have
been
demonstrated
with
thermogravimetric analysis, obtaining elevated reduction degrees by
heating in Ar up to 1000 °C (Δδ = 0.37 for the L1S9F perovskite). These
data show that incorporation of Sr2+ into the perovskites promotes the
oxygen storage capacity of these materials. This eﬀect is proportional to
the level of doping up to x = 0.7, but for higher Sr contents this eﬀect
levels oﬀ. This enhancement of the redox performance can be attributed
to the easy reduction of Fe4+ to Fe3+ in these doped perovskites.
Furthermore, these materials preserve their structural stability fol-
lowing the oxidation in air and, accordingly, they appear as very pro-
mising materials for chemical looping processes.
LSF perovskites were tested in ﬁve redox cycles for syngas pro-
duction by alternating methane reforming with CO2-splitting reactions
at 850 °C in isothermal conditions. The perovskites with x ≤0.3 pre-
sented the best results, with syngas yields 3–6 times higher than the
others, overall reaching values of 12.8 mmol H2 g−1 and 15.9 mmol CO
g−1 for the L9S1F material. On the other hand, the selectivity to hy-
drogen production increased with the Sr2+ content due to the im-
portant contribution of methane decomposition, while the reverse
Bourdouard reaction allows the removal of deposited carbon during
CO2 splitting. These alternative reactions pathways can justify the lack
of correlation between the redox capacity of the LSF and the syngas
yield. Furthermore, these processes contribute to boost the performance
of LSF perovskites with lower Sr content.
After reactions, characterization with XRD showed that perovskites
with x > 0.9 maintained their crystal structure, but L1S9F and SF
samples were transformed into Brownmillerite Sr2Fe2O5, with L9S1F
showing an additional segregated LaFeO3 phase. Further structural in-
stabilities, leading to the segregation of the single oxides of La and Sr, as
well as the formation of Fe carbide and graphite are found following
prolonged reduction in methane. Remarkably, the perovskite phase can
be recovered when reused in CH4-CO2 cycles, where the performance is
even enhanced. Therefore, setting up cycles with higher frequency can
be advisable for better control of the H2/CO ratio and achieving an
improved stability of the production, but the resilience of the LSF
perovskite facilitates reactivity recovery even following strong devia-
tions of the optimal operation conditions.
In summary, LSF perovskites appears as really attractive system in
order to develop a more sustainable syngas production based on al-
ternative cycles of methane partial oxidation and CO2 splitting.
