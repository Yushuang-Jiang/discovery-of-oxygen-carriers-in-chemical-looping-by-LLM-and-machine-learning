A B S T R A C T   
Biomass chemical looping gasification (BCLG) is a promising technology utilizes lattice oxygen for partial 
oxidation to produce high-value fuels. But in the field of BCLG, oxygen uncoupling chemical looping gasification 
(OU-CLG) is a novel concept that introduces molecular oxygen to optimize traditional gas-solid reactions. In this 
work, based on the most representative oxygen uncoupling elements successfully doped into La-Fe-O oxygen 
carriers, the results showed that the reaction performance not be positively correlated with the degree of oxygen 
uncoupling, only Co doping exhibited a better gasification performance than LaFeO3 did. Further, Co-LF differs 
from conventional LF in that it performs better in ex-situ gasification than in-situ gasification, with a total gas 
yield and carbon conversion efficiency of 1078.70 ml/g and 75.15% respectively. Further characterizations 
revealed that Co doping led to changes in the physical structure and reducibility. More importantly, enriched 
lattice oxygen and oxygen vacancies induced the formation of disordered reactive oxygen species in perovskite 
lattice, which promote the further oxygen uncoupling and the formation of oxygen uncoupling vacancies in ex- 
situ gasification. Moreover, doped but failure to promote the CLG might due to oxygen scarcity, impurity and 
elemental catalytic properties. Finally, a clear reaction mechanism regarding OU-CLG was proposed.   
1. Introduction 
Growing concerns about fossil fuel depletion, global warming and 
energy security are the primary motivations for exploring alternative 
fuels and efficient chemical methods. Renewable energy offers a valid 
solution to rationalize the present and future energy structure, and 
biomass is considered a promising substitute for fossil fuels featuring 
carbon neutrality, abundant reserves and renewability [1,2]. Among the 
thermo-chemical methods for biomass conversion, chemical looping 
gasification (CLG), as a new thermal conversion method, outperforms 
the traditional ways in several aspects [3,4]: (ⅈ) it can save costs by 
avoiding the use of expensive pure oxygen as the gasification agent; (ⅈⅈ) 
it produces syngas with higher purity by avoiding the use of air that 
causes N2 dilution; (ⅈⅈⅈ) it realizes energy circulation between air reactor 
and fuel reactor to a certain extent. 
In general, oxygen carriers (OCs) used for the complete oxidation of 
biomass are suitable for chemical looping combustion (CLC) for heat 
generation, while those tend to incompletely oxidize biomass are suit­
able for chemical looping gasification (CLG) for syngas production. As 
shown in Fig. 1, the reactions involving molecular oxygen are 
subdivided into oxygen uncoupling chemical looping combustion (OU- 
CLC) and oxygen uncoupling chemical looping gasification (OU-CLG) 
[5,6]. Oxygen uncoupling refers to the characteristic that oxygen car­
riers release molecular oxygen at a specific temperature and oxygen 
partial pressure to participate in the reaction process [7]. Compared to 
conventional CLG, which focuses on the catalytic cracking of tar mole­
cules by oxygen carriers and utilizes lattice oxygen to achieve partial 
oxidation, OU-CLG has the advantage of introducing molecular oxygen 
to optimize the conventional gas-solid reaction and improve the low 
efficiency of the solid-solid gasification reaction due to heat and mass 
transfer limitations [8,9]. In addition, due to the very high reactivity of 
oxygen uncoupling materials, the amount of metal oxide material 
required for system applications would be lower, realizing smaller 
reactor size and lower costs [10]. As a new concept and process, research 
in this area has recently been carried out by some researchers. Wang 
et al. used Cu–Fe oxygen carriers for OU-CLG and explored that the 
critical factors affecting the oxygen decoupling ability of OC are tem­
perature, heating rate and oxygen partial pressure, and dissected the 
oxygen decoupling phenomenon by combining the results of DFT cal­
culations and kinetic analysis [9]. Zhang et al. investigated OU-CLG

based on lignocellulosic biomass using different mixing ratios of CuO as 
oxygen carriers, and rigorously performed product distribution analysis 
and kinetic analysis [11]. Moreover, Yan et al. used Mn-doped Ca–Fe 
OC for OU-CLG of digestate demonstrating that the oxygen uncoupling

activating phenomenon of CO2 molecule at oxygen uncoupling vacancy 
[7,12]. OU-CLG is a technique that is gradually gaining widespread 
attention from related researchers. 
The selection of OCs is essential in the chemical looping process 
(CLP) in regard to selectivity for syngas production, reducing pollutant 
emissions and enhancing stability performance [13,14]. Numerous 
studies have studied that the reactivity of OCs can be improved by 
doping with transition metals (Ni, Mn, Sr, Ca, et al) [15–18]. However, 
thermodynamically, only a limited number of metal oxides can match 
the requirements of oxygen uncoupling processes: CuO/Cu2O, Mn2O3/ 
Mn3O4, and Co3O4/CoO [10,19]. Unfortunately, such mono-metal ox­
ides have some flaws if directly used as OCs, for instance, low melting 
temperature, low resistance to agglomeration, and environmental 
pollution. 
Nowadays, perovskite-type oxides and doping strategies have been 
widely proposed and applied in order to improve reactivity and cycling 
stability. Perovskite-type oxides are generally in the form of ABO3, in 
which A can be a rare-earth and alkali metal, and B is usually a transition 
metal [20,21]. Typical examples are BaMnO3, CaMnO3, and LaFeO3, 
which are used in the fields of hydrogen production, chemical looping 
combustion, CO2 reduction and so on [21–23]. Out of the various 
perovskite-type oxides, LaFeO3 is a relatively basic oxygen carrier and 
gathers the merits of both perovskite and iron oxide, namely high oxy­
gen transport capacity and capability of hosting high concentration of 
alkaline active sites and oxygen vacancies [24,25]. Another attractive 
characteristic of LaFeO3 is that different perovskite frameworks can be 
derived through doping of the overall LaFeO3 structure or partial sub­
stitution of the A- and/or B-site cations. In this way, the active compo­
nents can be embedded into the bulk phase of materials while 
maintaining their basic crystal structure [26]. In recent studies, Yan 
et al. used Mn-doped Ca–Fe OC for OU-CLG of digestate [7,12], 
demonstrating that the oxygen uncoupling capacity indeed can improve 
the selectivity of syngas and explaining the activating phenomenon of 
CO2 molecule at oxygen uncoupling vacancy. These prove that modi­
fying the oxygen uncoupling capacity of OCs is a feasible and promising 
method to improve CLG performance. 
Therefore, this work took the widely reported LF as the basic oxygen 
carrier and attempted to optimize the conventional gasification pro­
cesses by introducing highly active molecular oxygen through the 
doping of the three most representative oxygen uncoupling elements 
(Cu, Mn, Co). Besides, the suitable oxygen uncoupling doping elements 
for LaFeO3 were screened, and the effects of temperature and configu­
ration conditions on the gasification performance of the OCs were also 
illustrated. Eventually, the interaction mechanism of oxygen uncoupling 
capacity‑oxygen vacancy activity-gasification performance of M-LF (M 
= Cu, Mn and Co) was explored. This study is expected to further the 
understanding of the novel concept of OU-CLG and realize highly effi­
cient and clean utilization of biomass resources. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
2.1.1. Biomass feedstock 
Pine sawdust (PS), from the locality of Nanjing (China) was used as 
fuel biomass feedstock. The biomass was crushed and sieved to obtain 
particles in the range 0.18–0.43 mm, after which it was dried for 24 h at 
105 ◦C and kept for later use. The proximate and ultimate analyses of the 
sawdust were shown in Table 1. 
2.1.2. Synthesis of oxygen carriers 
The perovskite-type OCs were synthesized by the sol-gel auto­
combustion method. Firstly, the required amounts of La(NO3)3⋅6H2O, 
Mn(NO3)2⋅4H2O, Cu(NO3)2, Fe(NO3)3⋅9H2O and Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O were 
mixed in the molar ratio of M:La:Fe were 1:1:1(M-LF, M = Cu, Mn, Co) 
and 0:1:1(LF) respectively, and dissolved in distilled water. In order to 
facilitate the chelation process of metal ions, a certain amount of citric 
acid and ethylene glycol were added to the solution to a molar ration of 
citric acid: glycol: metal cations were 1.5:1.5:1. Subsequently, to 
enhanced the homogeneity of the gel and the purity of phase, aqueous 
ammonia was dropped into the resulted solution to adjust the pH value 
= 8.0. The resulting mixture was stirred at 500rpm on a magnetic stirrer 
and evaporated at 80 ◦C for 1h until a viscous gel was formed. A sponge- 
like precursor was then formed after drying at 200 ◦C for 2 h. Thereafter, 
the samples were heated at 900 ◦C for 6 h at a 10 ◦C/min ramp in a 
muffle furnace. Ultimately, the synthesized solid was crushed and sieved 
to particles under 80 mesh and obtained the final OCs. 
2.2. OCs evaluation 
2.2.1. Oxygen uncoupling performance test 
The oxygen release capability of OCs was analyzed by thermogra­
vimetric analysis using a TGA/DSC from Switzerland Mettler Toledo. 
Firstly, First, 5–8 mg of fresh OCs were evenly spread on the bottom of 
the alumina crucible and then placed into the TGA, and the temperature 
was increased from 30 ◦C to 1000 ◦C at a rate of 10 ◦C/min, while 50 ml/ 
min of pure argon was introduced. For evaluation of the oxygen 
uncoupling stability of OCs, similarly, 5–8 mg of fresh OCs were placed 
in TGA, and the temperature was increased from 30 ◦C to 900 ◦C at a rate 
of 10 ◦C/min, accompanied by 50 ml/min of pure argon atmosphere, 
and maintained 900 ◦C for 20 min. After that, by alternating air (50 ml/ 
min) and argon (50 ml/min) atmosphere under isothermal condition 
(900 ◦C) to simulated OCs deoxygenation and obtain oxygen. A total of 
5 cycles were performed, with each half cycle lasting 20 min. 
2.2.2. Experiment setup and procedure 
The biomass chemical looping gasification experiments were carried 
out in a fixed-bed reactor setup, as schematically depicted in Fig. 2, 
consisting of a U-type tube made of quartz and a K-type thermocouple 
with PID controller controlling the temperature. Take a typical in-situ 
gasification experiment as an example, at first, the mixture samples of 
PS (150 mg) and oxygen carriers (150 mg) are pre-assembled in a cru­
cible and placed on the side of the reactor for subsequent experiments. 
N2 was then introduced into the reactor at a rate of 50 ml/min to 
eliminate air and maintain the inert environment. After reaching the 
target reaction temperature, the crucible was promptly dropped for the 
experiment. The gas was collected using the drainage method within 20 
min of the process and identified via gas chromatography (Agilent Gas 
Chromatograph, 7890B). After the reduction stage, N2 was purged for 
20 min to exclude residual gas and then the carrier gas air was at a rate 
of 100 ml/min for 30 min for the oxidation stage. 
The detailed experiment setup and procedure of ex-situ gasification 
experiment were described in the previous work [13]. Noteworthy, the 
most obvious difference from the in situ gasification experiments is that 
in the in situ experiments, the biomass and OCs were separated. The OCs

2.3. Characterization of oxygen carriers 
Structure and crystallinity of the samples were characterized by an 
XRD diffractometer (RigakuSmartlab) using Cu Kα radiation (1.5406 Å) 
in 2θ scanning range 5–90◦with a scan speed of 10 deg. min-1. Scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM) of the obtained samples was performed using

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characterization of as-prepared OCs 
3.1.1. Oxygen uncoupling performance test 
The criterion for evaluating the oxygen uncoupling capability of an 
oxygen carrier is whether it releases gaseous oxygen at a certain tem­
perature and oxygen partial pressure. Therefore, the oxygen uncoupling 
ability and stability of the as-prepared OCs were evaluated by switching 
air and inert gas in TG. As seen in Fig. 3(a). The mass change curves of 
four samples from 30 to 1000 ◦C under a argon atmosphere were 
compared, and they can be split into three stages. For stage 1 (30 ◦C- 
150 ◦C), all oxygen carriers had weight loss, which may be caused by the 
release of water and other adsorbed impurities. In stage 2 
(150 ◦C–500 ◦C), LF had more observable mass loss than other OCs, 
which may be relevant to the continuous release of adsorbed oxygen, 
while it exhibited no drastic loss of mass in stage 3 (500 ◦C–1000 ◦C), 
during which, however, Cu-LF, Co-LF and Mn-LF all showed significant 
weight loss. More specifically, Mn-LF started to lose mass at a stable rate 
at around 520 ◦C and eventually remained at 98.397% of its mass, while 
Co-LF and Cu-LF demonstrated drastic mass loss at around 800 ◦C, and 
residual mass accounted for 97.593% and 97.073% respectively. This is 
attributed to the spillover of part of the internal lattice oxygen driven by 
high temperature, after the adsorbed oxygen that occupies the oxygen 
vacancies was removed from the surface. 
The stability of the oxygen uncoupling characteristics can be evalu­
ated by isothermal cycling experiments with switching between argon 
and air atmospheres. As shown in Fig. 3(b), M-LF lost weight rapidly in 
argon atmosphere and regenerated rapidly in air atmosphere. It can be 
inferred that the doping of different elements changes the microstruc­
ture of peroxides to endow peroxides with oxygen uncoupling ability. 
On top of that, it can be observed that the rate of oxygen-release of Co-LF 
was much faster than that of oxygen-gain during a cycle, and such 
efficient oxygen release may improve the reactivity [27]. However, LF 
does not show a periodic fluctuation in mass under the switch in at­
mosphere. In our previous study, some conventional oxygen carrier such 
as NiFe2O4 also exhibits thermal stability like LF [13]. In summary, the 
as-prepared M-doped LF (M = Cu, Mn, Co) have well-defined oxygen 
characteristics and cycle performance, and follow the oxygen uncou­
pling ability order in Cu-LF > Co-LF > Mn-LF, which meets the 
expectation. 
3.1.2. XRD 
The crystalline phase of the as-prepared OCs was examined by XRD 
patterns and shown in Fig. 4. The diffraction peaks of LF were perfectly 
attributed to LaFeO3 (JCPDS No.75–0541), which were assigned to 
orthorhombic structure with pm3m space group. For the samples of Mn- 
LF and Co-LF, the OCs still kept the perovskite structure without any 
other oxides containing La, Fe and Mn/Co, indicating the high purity of 
the products. However, CuO and La2CuO4 were found in the diffraction 
peak of Cu-LF, which may result from the excess overflow of Cu2+ and B- 
site deficiency [28,29]. It is worth mentioning that, CuO is commonly 
used in chemical looping combustion due to its superior oxygen storage 
and transport capacity to facilitate combustion rate and carbon capture 
efficiency. This may contradict our original intention, as it is not bene­
ficial to the CLG field that partially oxidizes biomass to obtain syngas 
[28]. When Co or Mn element was doped, Co3+ cation (0.61 Å) and 
Mn3+ cation (0.53 Å) were both smaller than Fe3+ cation (0.645 Å). 
Smaller B-site elements drove the perovskite structure to contract in­
ward, causing the diffraction peaks to shift upward. In contrast, Cu2+
cation (0.73 Å) was larger than Fe3+ cation (0.645 Å), causing the 
diffraction peaks to shift downward. Researchers found that the sub­
stitution of metal ions for a part of Fe3+ would cause an electronic un­
balance, resulting in the electrical valence compensation to retain the 
charge neutrality [30,31]. Meanwhile, the distorted perovskite structure 
can also adjust the charge balance by generating oxygen vacancies, 
which could promote the activity of OCs [32,33]. 
Table 2 shows the structural parameters of the perovskites obtained 
from XRD analyses. The calculated lattice constants of all oxygen car­
riers did not change significantly, indicating that the perovskite struc­
ture was maintained. The doping of metal ions changed the average 
crystallite size and cell volume of oxygen carriers to different degrees. 
However, it is worth noting that doping of Co to LaFeO3 led to the 
shifting of diffraction peaks toward a lower angle direction and smaller 
average crystallite size and cell volume, but in theory it should be the 
opposite, since Co3+ (0.61 Å) is larger than Mn3+ (0.53 Å), which 
revealed that higher-valence Co ions were induced to form, and Co-LF 
had a more compact crystal structure. Taken together, it can be 
referred that Co-LF probably had a higher lattice oxygen concentration. 
In addition, it is clearly observed that the intensity of diffraction peaks of 
Co-LF is significantly lower compared to that of other OCs. The decrease 
of crystallinity can be illustrated by the Jahn-Teller effect, so Co doping 
may have caused a change in tolerance factor, which led to a transition 
from orthorhombic to other structures [34]. This slight tendency of 
transferring to other crystalline forms might be related to the conversion 
of lattice oxygen to active oxygen species as will be mentioned later.

3.1.3. SEM 
To analyze the morphologic of different elements doped LaFeO3 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of these samples were 
carried out and presented in Fig.5. It can be seen that the LF nano­
particles synthesized by the autocombustion method have excellent 
dispersion and are quite homogeneous. More notably, after heteroge­
neous doping, Mn-LF and Co-LF retain their original dense layered 
structure, while Cu-LF exhibits larger nanoparticles and relatively 
agglomerated bulk structure, while the significantly larger pores of OC 
may not be conducive to the adequate contact between biomass pyrol­
ysis products and active sites [35]. Comparing all the samples, the sur­
face morphology of Co-LF formed the most dense layered or film-like 
structure, which can indicate the homogeneity and fineness of its 
nanoparticles on the one hand. On the other hand, it can indicate that it 
possesses the overall structural specificity, which can indirectly predict 
the positive improvement of this heterogeneous doping. 
3.1.4. XPS 
Fig. 6 depicts the wide XPS survey spectrum and high-resolution O1s 
and Fe 2p spectra for all samples, which indicates the presence of La, Fe, 
O and doped elements. Further, the asymmetric O1s core-level spectrum 
for perovskite samples can be divided into lattice oxygen (OI), chem­
isorbed oxygen (OII), and physoisorbed oxygen (OIII) [13,36]. When LF 
was doped with different elements, the three oxygen species did not 
have a significant change in the binding energy, but their content 
exhibited obvious changes, especially lattice oxygen, which indicated 
that the chemical environment around the active components has 
changed. Generally speaking, doping with metal ions of different par­
ticle sizes would change initial metal-O local environments, lower the 
oxygen vacancy formation energy and promote lattice oxygen transfer 
during reaction. Besides, the Fe 2p spectra of as-prepared OCs consisted 
of two spin–orbit doublets of Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2, located at 710.28 
eV and 724.78 eV, and the satellite peak at ~718 eV and the asymmetric 
peak shape together revealed the existence of iron ions with two 
chemical environments in the perovskite. In the fitted Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 
2p1/2 spectra, the two peaks corresponded to Fe3+ (710.08 and 723.48 
eV) and Fe2+ (711.58, 714.18, 725.18 and 728.08 eV) states respectively 
[37,38]. Normally, when the perovskite structure is maintained, the 
concentration of oxygen vacancy can be reflected by the abundance of 
Fe2+ ions in the system [39], since each oxygen vacancy created is 
inevitable to leave two electrons, reducing Fe3+ into Fe2+ [40]. 
Accordingly, we have analyzed the concentration of Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions 
in as-prepared OCs and listed them in Table 3. As we can see, except for 
Cu-LF, other doped perovskites showed higher Fe2+ concentration than 
LF, confirming that the doping of different elements accelerated the 
reduction of Fe3+ through the charge compensation effect and then 
promoted the formation of oxygen vacancies [29,41]. The doping of Co 
raised the total oxygen content to 115% of that of LF, and increased the 
OI/(OII+ OIII) and Fe2+/(Fe3++Fe4+) from 1.10 and 0.67 to 1.60 and 
1.22. The increased lattice oxygen and oxygen vacancies together 
created conditions for further oxygen uncoupling, but also accelerated 
the electrons exchange rate between biomass and OCs, hence promoting 
the redox performance of oxygen carriers [26].

510 ◦C in the low-temperature stage, which may be due to the release of 
adsorbed oxygen that occupied the oxygen vacancy in the surface and 
the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+. These results are consistent with the 
literature [42,43]. In the high-temperature stage, the curve of LF 
appeared to a manifest peak at around 680 ◦C, and then emerged a 
reduction band starting from 800 ◦C, which may result from the 
migration-release of internal lattice oxygen [44]. As for Mn-LF, it

different doping metals may have different effects on the Metal-O bond 
strength [12,29], as well as different synergistic effects with the iron- 
based oxygen carriers to make the differences in the reducibility. 
3.1.6. EPR 
The presence of oxygen vacancy vacancies can be further confirmed 
by EPR technology, as shown in Fig. 8, the symmetric peaks centered at 
g ≈2.003 represent the presence of single-electron-trapped oxygen 
vacancies [47,48]. The EPR curves of as-prepared OCs showed the same 
trend, and the EPR signal intensity of the OCs followed the sequence of 
Co-LF > Cu-LF > Mn-LF > LF, which indicated that the different metal 
doping largely strengthened the EPR signals of the as-prepared OCs. The 
amount of adsorbed oxygen on the perovskite surface was undoubtedly 
higher as there were more oxygen vacancies. More importantly, the 
concentration differential between the inside and outside can foster the 
continuous outflow of lattice oxygen to the surface after removing the

3.2. Gasification tests 
3.2.1. Screening of perovskites for OU-CLG 
Through the TG analysis, metal-doped LaFeO3 of Cu, Mn, and Co had 
acquired the oxygen uncoupling ability. However, the properties of 
oxygen uncoupling characteristics may be caused by different factors. In 
this part, the fixed-bed reactor was used to explore the oxygen uncou­
pling properties. Fig. 10 shows the gas yields of each component and 
Fig. 9 illustrates comparison of gasification efficiency and carbon con­
version efficiency with different oxygen carriers during the CLG process 
in the temperature range of 750–950 ◦C. As the temperature rose, the 
yield of H2 and CO increased while the yield of C2Hm gradually 
decreased. The total gas yield, the gasification efficiency and carbon 
conversion efficiency were also improved with increasing temperature. 
This can be attributed to the fact that the high temperature facilitates the 
pyrolysis of biomass and the cracking of macromolecular tar [26]. In 
addition, high temperature can promote the rightward shift of a series of 
endothermic reactions such as the Boudouard reaction, partial oxidation 
of methane (POM) and the water-gas shift reaction, resulting in higher 
yields and efficiencies. More specifically, Co-LF stood out among the 
other samples at all reaction temperatures, with the total gas yield 
reaching 978.89 ml/g and 1098.58 ml/g at 900 ◦C and 950 ◦C, respec­
tively, higher than 869.07 ml/g and 930.86 ml/g of LaFeO3. On the 
other hand, the gasification performance of Cu-LF and Mn-LF fell short 
of expectations as they were both worse than that of LF. Furthermore, in 
terms of component changes, the CO2 yield of Cu-LF increased contin­
uously as the temperature rose, and its CO yield is the lowest among the 
as-prepared OCs. The presence of CuO and excessive oxygen uncoupling 
properties contribute to the excessive oxidation of biomass volatiles 
causing the formation of CO2 consequently. 
Assuming that the effect of pyrolysis gas atmosphere on oxygen 
uncoupling is not considered, the results of previous thermogravimetric 
experiments had indicated that Co-LF would release molecular oxygen 
at around 800 ◦C. The CO2 yield of Co-LF (146.52 ml/g) was indeed 
slightly higher than that of LF (128.74 ml/g) at 800 ◦C due to the strong 
oxidizing property of molecular oxygen. However, at temperatures 
higher than 800 ◦C, the CO2 yield of Co-LF is lower than that of LF. On 
the other hand, The CO yield of Co-LF (418.18 ml/g) was higher than 
that of LF (334.35 ml/g) at 900 ◦C even under the extreme oxidizing 
condition caused by oxygen uncoupling, which indicates that Co-LF may 
have a strong affinity toward CO2 adsorption and the enriched oxygen 
vacancy on its surface is capable of activating CO2 molecule. This may 
also be related to that LaFe perovskite promotes the dissociation of C–O 
bonds and carbonyl (C=O) through Co doping [12,54]. Moreover, 
among the four oxygen carriers, Co-LF showed the most superior

3.2.2. Ex-situ experiment for further oxygen uncoupling 
In general, different types of catalyst-fuel configurations will have 
different effects. For in-situ configuration [57,58]: (ⅈ) Provide intimate 
physical contact between biomass with derived pyrolysis gas and OCs; 
(ⅈⅈ) Fundamentally improve gas quality through in-situ catalysis of 
biomass tar; (ⅈⅈⅈ) Allow interaction between partially activated coke and 
OCs; (ⅈv) Produce less solid-phase products; (v) Had higher probability of 
causing impurity clogging coverage and OCs poisoning. While for ex-situ 
configuration: (ⅈ) Had complete biomass pyrolysis gas-OCs catalytic 
reaction；(ⅈⅈ) Possessed higher degree of deoxidation；(ⅈⅈⅈ) Be more 
liable for surface carbon deposit due to hydrocarbon decomposition. 
Based on the consideration of the oxygen decoupling temperature 
interval of the key samples and the results of the gasification tests at 
different temperatures, 900 ◦C was chosen as the gasification tempera­
ture for ex-situ experiment for further oxygen uncoupling. The results of 
in-situ and ex-situ tests of LF and Co-LF were compared to further 
investigate the effect of oxygen uncoupling effect on biomass gasifica­
tion, as shown in Fig. 11. The in-situ configuration of LF showed higher 
gas yield and gasification efficiency, which may be due to better contact 
between OC and biomass fuel promoting the catalysis of pyrolysis gas 
and tar. On the contrary, it is interesting to note that Co-LF with oxygen 
uncoupling capacity has the higher gas yield and gasification efficiency 
in the ex-situ experiments, with the total gas yield reaching 1078.70 ml/ 
g at 900 ◦C. The opposite experimental phenomenon worth further 
discussion of the mechanism of oxygen uncoupling characteristics, 
because during the ex-situ test, Co-LF was placed in one side of the 
reactor in advance and the temperature was raised to the target tem­
perature before the biomass was injected to start the reaction. In other 
words, before the start of the reaction, the oxygen carrier had already 
lost 2% of its oxygen fraction consisting of adsorbed oxygen from surface 
impurities and a slight amount of lattice oxygen, but showed better 
gasification performance. 
The distribution of gas components under different configurations as 
shown in Fig. 11. It can be seen that Co-LF has a higher yield of H2 and 
CO and nearly zero yield of C2Hm under ex-situ configuration compared 
to in-situ configuration, which indicates that Co-LF can further catalyze 
and reforming of hydrocarbons under ex-situ condition. In the previous 
TG tests, Co-LF was able to release almost 2% of oxygen fraction under 
argon atmosphere, and in the H2-TPR, the hydrogen depletion peak was 
higher than that of LF, and the hydrogen depletion temperature lowered. 
Shen et al. also proved by TG tests that the weight loss of metal-doped 
perovskite in a hydrogen atmosphere was a dozen times more than 
that in a argon atmosphere. Accordingly, it can be inferred that the 
occurrence of further oxygen uncoupling is closely related to the at­
mosphere around OCs. The ex-situ experiments between biomass and 
OCs are equivalent to oxygen uncoupling under pyrolysis gas atmo­
sphere, and the presence of reducing atmosphere will inevitably lead to 
more active oxygen migration and release. When taking no account of 
the difference between in-situ and ex-situ configurations of OCs, the loss 
of about 2% oxygen consisting of surface adsorbed oxygen and a slight 
amount of lattice oxygen in a nitrogen atmosphere before the start of the 
experiments, instead, has a certain degree of activation of the oxygen 
uncoupling of OCs. It could be elucidated that the oxygen uncoupling

3.2.4. Reaction mechanism discussion 
Based on the experiments and analysis described above, the reaction 
mechanism of Co-LF in oxygen uncoupling chemical looping gasification 
is proposed as given in Fig. 15, which consists of four main stages: (1) 
Generation of oxygen vacancies. (2) Catalytic reaction and further ox­
ygen uncoupling on the surface. (3) Reactivation of the catalytic site at 
oxygen uncoupling vacancy. (4) Air regeneration. At the beginning of 
the reaction, the release of molecular oxygen and the formation of ox­
ygen vacancies, which resulted from the diffusion and overflow of ox­
ygen atoms after its intense vibration on the crystal surface, were driven 
by the force of oxygen uncoupling due to temperature and oxygen 
partial pressure [60,61]. The introduction of molecular oxygen enables 
the optimization of the conventional gas-solid reaction of LaFeO3 and 
better gasification performance. Next, the high concentration of lattice 
oxygen inside the OC will migrate to the surface for further oxygen 
uncoupling, driven by the continuous consumption of surface oxygen to 
generate oxygen vacancies and the reducing atmosphere of biomass 
pyrolysis products. As a result, the adsorption energy of reactants on the 
OC surface would be reduced, since the further generated oxygen 
uncoupling vacancies would lower the reaction energy barrier and 
enhance molecular activation [44]. In the dynamic process of formation- 
occupation of oxygen vacancies, catalytic pyrolysis reactions of biomass 
progressively became more active. Moreover, the vacancies could 
enable the regeneration of OCs by capturing oxygen atoms in biomass 
pyrolysis gas, while enhancing the adsorption-catalysis effect [61]. In an 
inert reaction atmosphere without a gasification agent such as H2O or 
CO2, this effect allows the oxygen uncoupling vacancy to be reactivated, 
so the C2Hm will be further converted and vacancies will catch the CO2 
and reduce it to CO to achieve higher gasification efficiency [62]. 
Finally, OCs were completely regenerated in the air reactor. 
The oxygen uncoupling of Co-LF is driven by the temperature and 
external atmosphere as well as the oxygen chemical potential gradient of 
its own lattice. In the in-situ tests, Co-LF provides molecular oxygen to 
participate in the gasification reaction system through lattice oxygen 
uncoupling, which improves the gasification performance of LaFe 
perovskite. In the ex-situ tests, the activated adsorption phenomenon 
and catalytic performance of oxygen uncoupling vacancies were 
magnified due to no interference of solid-solid and in-situ fixed carbon 
components, hence the opposite gasification performance of LF and Co- 
LF in the two different configuration conditions appeared. Most current 
studies have focused on chemical looping gasification coupled with CO2 
or H2O splitting utilizing strong residual valence forces of the unsatu­
rated bonds presenting on the surface atoms of reduced OC, leading to 
easier adsorption site formation on the particle surface [63–65]. OCs 
with oxygen uncoupling ability also generate similar adsorption cata­
lytic sites due to the continuous consumption of oxygen, so the precursor 
reactions of some products such as the break-up of C–O bond and hy­
droxyl were amplified in the ex-in tests, and showed higher yields of H2 
and CO in the fuel reactor. Similarly, researchers have conducted a 
number of studies using the deoxygenation properties of OC to produce 
high-quality syngas [66,67]. Hence, there is a great potential to utilize 
oxygen uncoupling characteristics and this study provides an unusual 
perspective for screening suitable OCs to facilitate the generation of 
syngas. Adjustment of the oxygen uncoupling capacity of OCs and 
investigation of the mechanism of oxygen uncoupling adsorption- 
catalysis reaction under gasification conditions such as water vapor/ 
CO2 atmosphere can be further studied in the future. 
4. Conclusion 
In this work, LaFeO3 was chosen as the fundamental OC, and the 
three most representative elements (Cu, Mn, Co) were used for doping to 
endow OC oxygen uncoupling ability. The applicability and reaction 
mechanism of M-LF (M = Cu, Mn, Co) for oxygen uncoupling chemical 
looping gasification were investigated, and the main conclusions of the 
study are outlined as follows: 
(1) CLG reaction performance is not proportional to oxygen uncou­
pling degree caused by different heterogeneously doped, only Co- 
LF showed the best gasification performance among all OCs. 
Further characterization showed that high concentrations of lat­
tice oxygen and oxygen vacancies are the origin and key to the 
OU-CLG properties, and the high mobility of lattice oxygen 
created the conditions for further oxygen uncoupling to occur.  
(2) The doping of Cu and Mn but did not promote CLG performance 
of LF may be due to oxygen scarcity, impurity and elemental 
catalytic properties. Although their perovskite structure is well 
kept, significant differences in the strength of the metal-O bonds 
as well as in their reducibility emerge. The result is poor catalytic 
performance and the presence of overoxidation.  
(3) The oxygen uncoupling property of Co-LF is further promoted in 
the ex-situ experiments of pyrolysis gas and OCs. The oxygen 
uncoupling vacancies generated can promote the adsorption of 
hydrocarbons and the rupture of C–O bond and hydroxyl, as a 
result, the total gas yield and carbon conversion efficiency 
reached 1078.70 ml/g and 75.15%, respectively, at 900 ◦C. 
Despite the phase segregation due to heat and mass transfer, Co- 
LF still showed good stability in 10 consecutive redox cycles.  
(4) The reaction process and mechanism of oxygen uncoupling 
property for biomass gasification were thoroughly analyzed. In 
brief, the introduction of a proper amount of molecular oxygen 
into the reaction system can optimize the traditional gas-solid 
reaction, while the adsorption-catalysis-regeneration process of
