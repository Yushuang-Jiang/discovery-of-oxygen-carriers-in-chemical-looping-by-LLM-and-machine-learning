A B S T R A C T
Manganese oxides are promising materials for thermochemical heat storage at high temperatures due to
their low price and toxicity. However, doping with other transition metals is needed for stabilizing the
oxidation kinetics, enhancing the material durability over prolonged charge-discharge cycling. As well,
this system presents a high thermal hysteresis if compared with other redox couples. Namely, metal
oxide reduction takes place at higher temperatures (ca. 200 C) than re-oxidation of the reduced phase.
Narrowing the hysteresis loop for the manganese oxide system means that heat is stored and released in a
closer range of temperatures, which could boost the energy storage efﬁciency.
In this work, it was studied the effect that co-doping of Mn oxides with Fe and Cu has on the redox
temperatures of both forward and reverse reactions. Materials were prepared by a variation of Pechini
method and characterized by XRD and SEM. The capacity to withstand several redox cycles was analyzed
by thermogravimetric analyses. It was found that addition of certain amount of both dopants narrowed
the thermal hysteresis of such redox couple, at the same time that stable reversibility was achieved.

1. Introduction
Solar radiation can provide enough energy to meet the world's
annual demand. However, efﬁcient use of this renewable resource
is technologically and economically challenging due to, among
other facts, its intermittence. In this respect, concentrated solar
power (CSP) plants can generate round-the-clock electricity
production with the incorporation of thermal energy storage
systems (TES) [1,2]. Based on the mechanism in which heat is
stored, three different types of systems are identiﬁed: sensible,
latent and thermochemical heat storage [3,4]. Sensible heat
storage is already commercially installed, whereas latent or
thermochemical systems are still under development. Thermo-
chemical 
heat 
storage (TCS), 
which 
stores 
energy 
through
reversible reactions, is the less mature of these technologies and
presents higher technical complexity than those based on sensible
or latent heat. On the other hand, TCS generally exhibits high
energy storage densities and high operational ﬂexibility, since a
plethora of reversible reactions is available for such purpose,
covering a wide range of temperatures. However, recent advances
in central receiver plants working with volumetric air receivers
have focused the attention in the development of TCS based on
reduction-oxidation cycles of multivalent metal oxides. Although
these CSP plants are still under development [5], future designs
will produce high temperature air (>1000 C) at the receiver, which
can be utilized both as heat transfer ﬂuid for the reduction and as
reactant for the oxidation. This fact would avoid the need of gas
storage as in the case of other TCS approaches based on gas–solid
reactions (e.g., decarbonation–carbonation or dehydration-hydra-
tion [6,7]). Namely, part of the hot air generated with sun radiation
is used to supply the energy required to perform the oxide thermal
reduction, releasing oxygen Eq. (1). By this high-temperature
endothermic process, surplus heat produced at the CSP plant is
partly stored by means of a chemical reaction (charge). During off-
sun periods, discharge proceeds by performing the reverse reaction
Eq. (2). In this case, air is used to oxidize the reduced form of the
metal oxide, releasing the stored heat due to the exothermic nature
of this reaction.

One of the most remarkable characteristics of redox cycles of
these metal oxides is that the forward and reverse reactions occur
at different temperatures, i.e., a thermal hysteresis exists. Namely,
the metal oxide reduction takes place at higher temperatures than
the re-oxidation of the reduced phase. In the case of Mn-based
redox couple, the temperature difference between reduction and
oxidation is of ca. 200 K [8], whereas for Co3O4/CoO is around 50 K
range, depending on the nature of the material assayed and the
heating ramp used [8,11,14,16]. Narrowing the hysteresis loop for
the manganese oxide system means that heat is stored and
released in a closer range of temperatures, as in the case of Co oxide
system, and this fact could suppose an increase of the charge–
discharge energy efﬁciency and facilitate the integration of the
system.
In a previous work [9] we demonstrated that addition of Fe
shifted reduction and oxidation reactions to higher temperatures.
Especially, Fe-doping increased the oxidation temperature, which
in turn produced a narrowing in the hysteresis loop, as for the
reduction step the variation of the temperature was more
moderate [9]. Although limited, the raise in such value forced to
carry out the reaction by heating up to 1050 C in order to reach full
conversion. What it seems that is just a slight increase in
temperature can drastically affect to the materials cyclability
[9]. This is mainly due to the exponential relation between
temperature and particle sintering, which is the main responsible
of Mn oxides aging [10]. On the other hand, addition of Cu slightly
decreased both values [22]. Therefore, in this work, the inﬂuence
that co-doping the Mn oxides with Fe and Cu has on the
temperatures of both reactions has been studied. Redox cycling
was monitored by thermogravimetric analysis. Structure and
morphology were characterized by powder X-ray diffraction and
scanning electron microscopy before and after reaction. Finally, the
thermochemical heat storage/release capacity of the material
presenting best redox behavior was analyzed by differential
scanning calorimetry.

2.1. Materials preparation
Fe–Cu-doped 
manganese

were

a

namely Mn(NO3)24 H2O (97%, Sigma–Aldrich), Cu(NO3)23 H2O

were added to an aqueous solution of citric acid (CA, 99.5%,
Scharlab) with a Me:CA molar ratio of 1:5, with constant stirring
for 3 h at 70 C. Afterwards, ethylene glycol (EG, 99.5%, Sigma–

during 2 h more under stirring at 90 C for geliﬁcation. Then, the gel
was dried at 200 C for 3 h and then calcined at 450 C for 4 h in air.
Finally, the calcined gel was ground to ﬁne powders and further
calcined at 700 C for 4 h under static air. In total, eight samples
were synthesized varying the Mn–Fe–Cu molar ratio as shown in
Table 1.
2.2. Materials characterization
X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were performed using a Philips
PW 3040/00 X'Pert MPD/MRD diffractometer with CuKa radiation
(l = 1.54178 Å) at a scanning rate of 0.2 s1. Scherrer formula was
applied to the most intense reﬂection to calculate the mean
crystallite size. The background noise from ﬂuoresced X-rays was
removed by using a diffracted-beam monochromator. Lattice
dimensions were obtained using the Bragg law and the equation
d2 = a2/(h2 + k2 + l2), where h,k and l are the Miller indexes of the
Bragg plane, a is the cell parameter and d is the interplanar spacing.
The speciﬁc surface area (SBET) was determined by nitrogen
adsorption–desorption analyses at 77 K applying the multi-point
BET method and using a Quantachrome QuadraSorb-S equipment.
Prior to these measurements, samples were degassed at 250 C
under vacuum. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were
taken with a Hitachi TM-100 microscope without any previous
treatment of the samples.
2.3. Redox cycling
Stability of redox cycles was monitored by thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA) using a SDT Q-600 from TA Instruments. In
cyclability studies, ca. 10 mg of sample were placed into 90 ml
alumina crucibles and subjected to 30 charging–discharging
cycles. Each cycle consisted of a heating step from 650 C up to
1000 C and a cooling step from 1000 C to 650 C both at a rate of
5 K min1, under a constant air ﬂow of 100 ml min1 (pO2 = 0.21).
Simultaneously to TGA, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)
was carried out in order to study the heat effects during reduction
and oxidation reactions. DSC was calibrated under Ar atmosphere
using the melting temperatures and enthalpies of Zn and Ag along
the temperature range utilized for redox cycling.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Structure and morphology of Fe–Cu-doped Mn oxides
Fe–Cu-Mn oxides were prepared by applying the Pechini
method. In total eight samples were synthesized using different
Mn:Fe:Cu molar ratios (Table 1). In the case of Cu, amounts higher
than 1% could involve the formation of a segregated phase of Mn–
Cu spinel as reported in Mn–Cu–O phase diagram [24]. The
presence of a segregated phase is usually undesirable because it is
often inert to the redox processes. Accordingly, the mixed oxide
acts as a diluting agent, implying that the whole amount of
material is not being used for thermochemical storage and,
furthermore, it could add kinetic limitations to the redox cycles.
Thus, maximum loads of 5% Cu were used in this study in order to
check the aforementioned hypotheses. In the case of Fe, increasing
its amount shifts the reduction to higher temperatures [9,25].
However, in this case, the better solubility of trivalent iron cations
in the bixbyite phase (cubic Mn2O3), due to the identical ionic
radium of Fe3+ and Mn3+ in six-fold coordination (0.645 Å [26]),
would allow its incorporation without phase segregation up to a
50%. Nevertheless in this study the maximum Fe molar percentage
was 20% since we previously demonstrated this to be the optimum
Fe/Mn ratio for thermochemical heat storage [9].
Fig. 1 
depicts the XRD diffraction patterns for the six
synthesized samples. It can be observed that all of them presented
the same reﬂections, characteristic of bixbyite, Mn2O3, crystalline
phase (ICDD-00-041-144). Thus, the formation of any segregated
phase due to addition of dopants was not observed, as it could be
expected for the samples containing 5% Cu. Crystallite sizes
(Table 1) of the as-synthesized samples were of the same order of
magnitude ranging from 37 to 50 nm. The dashed panel in Fig. 1
depicts the most intense reﬂection for each sample. It can be
observed a slight shift to higher 2u values, which is attributed to
the incorporation of Cu2+ cations in the bixbyite cubic structure.
Cubic lattice parameter determination showed a diminution for all
the doped samples, although no clear trend was observed when
increasing the Cu quantity. This decrease indicates that copper
cations are most probably occupying tetrahedral sites in the lattice
rather than octahedral ones, since Cu2+ is smaller than Mn3+ in
fourfold coordination (0.57 Å) but larger in sixfold coordination
(0.73 Å) [26]. The preference of Cu bivalent cations for tetrahedral
positions is the main reason for the formation of Cu–Mn spinels
when higher amounts of copper are used. In that case, Cu2+ cations
occupy tetrahedral positions and Mn3+ octahedral ones.
Regarding the morphology of the Fe–Cu-doped Mn oxides,
differences between the several samples prepared by Pechini
method were not huge. Fig. 2 depicts the SEM micrographs taken to
the synthesized materials before being subjected to the charging-
discharging cycles. All the samples presented similar shapes,
formed by aggregates of irregular particles. Moderate changes
were appreciated on the speciﬁc surface area. The general trend
indicates that addition of Fe enlarged the SBET, whereas increasing
amounts of Cu reduced such value (Table 1).
3.2. Inﬂuence of Fe–Cu doping on the redox thermal hysteresis
As mentioned in the introduction, one of the most remarkable
characteristics of redox cycles of transition metal oxides (e.g.,
Co3O4/CoO and Mn2O3/Mn3O4) is that oxidation and reduction
reactions take place at different temperature ranges for the same
oxygen partial pressure. With the purpose of narrowing the
thermal hysteresis that the Mn2O3/Mn3O4 pair exhibits, chemical
modiﬁcations of such materials have been chosen based on
previous ﬁndings [9,22], in which we demonstrated that addition
of Fe or Cu to the Mn oxides modiﬁed the temperatures at which
redox reactions take place. In particular, addition of Fe increased
the reduction and, in a higher extent, the oxidation temperature
[9], leading to considerable narrowing of the redox hysteresis.
Conversely, addition of 1 mol% Cu had the opposite effect. Thus, in
this work we have evaluated if the combination of both dopants
could have a synergetic effect in such modiﬁcation, by running

Through the addition of 1 mol% of both dopants such difference

increasing the molar percentage of Fe up to 10% progressively

3.3. Redox cycles stability
The capacity to withstand several charge–discharge cycles was
tested by carrying out 30-redox cycles assays to each sample.
Fig. 4a represents the results of these runs. In Fig. 4b it is separately
depicted an extract of 20 charge–discharge cycles for the 20Fe–1Cu
sample since, as explained above, it was necessary to heat up to
1050 C in order to reach full conversion in the reduction step. It
can be observed that all the Fe–Cu-doped Mn oxides presented
redox reversibility in the conditions assayed. Moreover, samples
doped with 1% Cu presented quite stable redox cycles. However,
those containing 5% Cu showed a remarkable change in their
cyclability after several charge–discharge steps. For instance,
sample 10Fe–5Cu presented a loss in its cycle stability which led to
incomplete reactions. In particular, only an 85% of the reduced
form of such sample was oxidized in the last cycle.
As well, it was observed that by adding 5% Cu the weight loss
was diminished to values among 3.1–3.2%, which is ca. 6% less than
the theoretical value (3.4% calculated based on the stoichiometry of
Eq. (3). This fact suggests that these samples might contain a minor
amount of a segregated phase in the form of CuMn2O4 spinel,

Among the three 5% Cu doped-Mn oxides, 10Fe–5Cu and 20Fe–5Cu

oxide modifying the overall reaction kinetics. Interestingly, this
does not apply for 20Fe–1Cu, which exhibited quite stable redox
cycles and high degree of coarsening.
When comparing 10Fe–1Cu and 10Fe–5Cu materials, it can be
easily observed the vast differences in grain size for the two
samples that were synthesized using the same procedure and
analyzed under the same temperature conditions. Therefore, it
may be considered that a higher concentration of Cu2+ enhanced
defect and metal cation migration improving neck formation
between particles, process that favored coarsening and densiﬁca-
tion and, hence, resulting in a larger sintering extent. Differences in
the surface energies induced by dopant incorporation surely play
an additional role in the morphological changes of sintered
aggregates from vermicular, in the case of low dopant contents, to
prismatic forms at higher concentrations of Fe and Cu.

endothermic and exothermic peaks for the 15th cycle of the
aforementioned sample. The area under the endothermic peak was
determined by tangential–sigmoidal integration. It resulted in a
reduction enthalpy value of 165 kJ kg1. Analogously, the oxidation
enthalpy obtained was 150 kJ kg1, which is 10% lower than that of
reduction. It should be noted that DSC measurements were carried
out in alumina open crucibles. This fact implicates that some heat
losses are involved due to the open system and the more moderate
heat conductivity of alumina. Although these values are very
similar to those of pure Mn oxide [9], they are 25% lower than the
corresponding to a 20%Fe doped Mn oxide measured under the
same conditions [9]. The rest Fe–Cu doped Mn oxides did not show
better energy storage densities than 20Fe–1Cu sample. This result
is attributed to the presence of Cu cations, which, even if added in
small proportions, affected negatively to the heat storage/release
values, very likely due to the dilution effect of the segregated
CuMn2O4 spinel.
3.4. Inﬂuence of Fe–Cu-doping on the redox kinetics
As mentioned above, sintering processes, related with particle
coarsening and densiﬁcation, have a direct impact on the oxidation
reaction. However, it was found that by doping with the proper
amount of Fe and Cu, the diffusional hurdles caused by sintering
can be counteracted. This can be observed in Fig. 8a, where the
cycle-to-cycle evolution of the oxidation rate for each sample is
depicted. It can be distinguished three different trends depending
on the doping content. That is, 0Fe–0Cu presented an exponential
decrease of the oxidation rate values during the initial cycles,
reaching a plateau on the last half of the assay. This exponential
decrease can be remarkably improved by the adequate addition of
Fe and Cu. For instance, by adding 1% of both dopants it was
obtained a slightly worse oxidation behavior. However, by
increasing the Fe content up to 5% (5Fe–1Cu) high and stable
oxidation rates were observed over the 30-cycles run, presenting
an average value of 4.3 mmol O2 min1 g1. 10Fe–1Cu sample
showed the second highest initial oxidation rate, but it suffered a
gradual decrease during the ten ﬁrst cycles, period after which it
reached a quasi-stable value at ca. 3.4 mmol O2 min1 g1. This
decrease is attributed to the formation of a segregated phase, as
suggested by XRD analysis of Fig. 5, as a consequence of the
prolonged thermal stresses to which the material was subjected
during the TG experiments. Accordingly, the possible formation of
segregated spinels suggested by XRD analysis could also explain
the progressive decrease of the oxidation rate values for three
samples with 5% Cu. Nevertheless, despite such samples presented
a higher sintering extent (Fig. 6) in comparison with the un-doped
oxide, they presented faster oxidation reactions along the whole
30 cycles assayed, fact that could be explained by the presence of Fe
cations in the Mn3O4 lattice. Indeed, for all the synthesized
samples, the oxidation was clearly enhanced when increasing the
Fe content. This fact was ultimately conﬁrmed when observing the
oxidation rate values for 20Fe–1Cu sample. Besides presenting
quite stable oxidation reactions over the whole test, such material
showed the fastest oxidation rate. It reached an oxidation rate peak
maximum of 6.5 mmol O2 min1 g1 (17th cycle) and an average
rate value of 6.1 mmol O2 min1 g1. These results are very similar
to those reported for 20% Fe-doped Mn oxide [9].
Conversely, all the tested materials exhibited much more stable
reduction rates along the whole 30-redox cycle runs (Fig. 8b). This
result is attributed to the different mechanisms that control
oxidation and reduction reactions [10]. In the case of reduction,
which is a thermally controlled process [9], sintering does not
affect to its rate stability over long thermal cycling in the same
extent as in the case of oxidation. Additionally, in our previous
work we found that an increase in the heating rate produced an
increase on the reduction rate for both un-doped and Fe-doped Mn
oxides. On the other hand, for the un-doped Mn oxide oxidation
rates were not improved by using higher cooling rates. Indeed,
incomplete conversions were achieved when using cooling rates
higher than 10 K min1 for a sample with equal composition to
0Fe–0Cu [9]. In general, reduction is a faster process than
oxidation. For instance for 0Fe–0Cu, in the ﬁrst cycle, reduction
is a 2-fold faster reaction than oxidation. However, it can be
observed that co-doping Mn2O3 with Fe and Cu slows down the

4. Conclusions
In this work it has been demonstrated the possibility of tuning
the reduction and oxidation temperatures of a given redox couple
by metal co-doping. This is especially important for heat storage
systems based in redox cycles, since all of them present a
characteristic thermal hysteresis. By narrowing such hysteresis,
overall process efﬁciency is improved since storage and release of
heat take place in a closer temperature range, then diminishing the
energy penalty associated with lowering the temperature for
performing the oxidation reaction. In particular, we have proven
that Fe–Cu co-doping can be used for such approach in Mn oxide-
based 
thermochemical 
heat 
storage 
system. 
The 
reduction
temperature can be diminished by Cu incorporation, whereas
oxidation temperature is increased through Fe doping. Thus, the
difference 
between 
reduction 
and 
oxidation 
temperature
(DTHysteresis) has been signiﬁcantly decreased from 225 K, for the
un-doped Mn oxide, up to 81 K for the sample containing 20 mol%
of Fe and 5 mol% Cu. However, despite working at lower reduction
temperatures and with smaller DTHysteresis is interesting from a
process point of view, adding 5% of Cu also lowered the reduction
rate and caused a gradual decrease on the oxidation one probably
due to the formation of segregated mixed Mn–Cu spinel.
