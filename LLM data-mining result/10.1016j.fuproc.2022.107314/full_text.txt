A B S T R A C T   
In order to explore the oxidation and reduction properties of the LaFe0.5M0.5O3(M = Mn, Co, Ni, Cu) perovskites, 
torrefied biomass (TB) gasification and CO2 splitting were coupled as a whole chemical looping reaction, while 
the doped LaFe0.5M0.5O3 were prepared and employed as oxygen carriers(OCs) in the chemical looping. An 
proper torrefaction temperature was screened first for producing more syngas, then the effect of doped perov­
skites on the chemical looping reaction were evaluated via thermogravimetric analyzer and fixed-bed reactor in 
terms of the syngas yield and CO formation rate. The results showed that the doped perovskites exhibited higher 
activity in the gasification process, which promoted the conversion of TB and improved the yield of H2 and CO. 
Specifically, the CO yield of LaFe0.5Co0.5O3 and the H2 yield of LaFe0.5Ni0.5O3 was improved by about 4 times 
and 1.3 times respectively compared with original LaFeO3. Moreover, LaFe0.5Ni0.5O3 exhibited better perfor­
mance in both TB gasification and the CO2 splitting reaction, and the yield of H2 and CO reached 3.71 mmol⋅g−1 
and 1.80 mmol⋅g−1, respectively. Furthermore, the composition and surface topography evolution of doped 
perovskite were analyzed via TEM, SEM and XRD, and the results of XPS, EPR and TGA were combined to reveal 
the reaction mechanism.   
1. Introduction 
The energy crisis and derived environmental problems force people 
to find novel renewable ways to obtain fuels and chemicals [1]. Biomass 
has been considered as the fourth energy source, which is also a kind of 
green and renewable energy [2]. Preparing syngas from biomass gasi­
fication is a promising way to convert biomass into fuels and value- 
added chemicals [3]. However, the ratio of syngas obtained from 
biomass gasification is difficult to reach the desired ideal value [4], 
meanwhile, the separation device is always required to separate and 
purify the gas product [5]. As the technology developed in recent years, 
chemical looping technology provides a way to obtained purity syngas 
without the costly separation process [6]. Biomass chemical looping 
gasification coupled with CO2 splitting is a method derived from tradi­
tional chemical looping technology, which not only overcomes the 
shortcomings of traditional gasification but also achieves the effective 
utilization of CO2. However, the complex composition of biomass pro­
poses more requirements for the technology, thus the concerns have 
been focused on the biomass feedstock improvement and the oxygen 
carrier development [7]. 
Previous researches have proved that the composition of biomass can 
be preliminary controlled by pretreatment, which can not only reduce 
the oxygen content of the biomass, but also achieve the enrichment of 
the lignin or cellulose [8,9]. Torrefaction is one of the most promising 
method for industrialization due to its process simplicity, it can be 
regarded as a 200–300 ◦C low-temperature biomass pyrolysis process, 
which could be used for decreasing the O content and improving the 
heat value and energy density via simple low-temperature heating 
treatment [10]. Moreover, torrefaction can reduce hemicellulose and 
cellulose content in the biomass, which is instrumental in decreasing 
CO2 formation and improving the syngas yield [11]. Several studies 
have been found on the effects of torrefaction pretreatment on the BCLG 
reaction performance. Fan et al. [12]. reported that the torrefaction 
could effectively reduce the tar content and adjusted the H2/CO ratio of 
the syngas in the BCLG process. Zhang et al. [13]. further studied the 
chemical looping gasification behavior of torrefied spruce-pine-fir (SPF)

Although torrefied biomass could replace biomass in chemical 
looping gasification for producing high quality syngas, the OCs play the 
determination role in the chemical looping gasification [14,15]. The 
route that biomass chemical looping gasification coupled with CO2 
splitting also proposes more requirements to the OCs designation. 
Except for the good oxygen releasing capacity, the competitive CO 
selectivity and CO2 cracking activity are also important for the OCs [16]. 
Transition metal oxides, such as Fe, Co, Ni, Cu and so on, in the form of 
spinel, perovskite have been extensively studied as OCs in chemical 
looping [17]. Specifically, iron based perovskite are wildly used in 
chemical looping conversion of solid fuel, attributing to their excellent 
oxygen releasing capacity, thermal stability,as well as their low cost 
[18,19]. As the typical iron based perovskite, LaFeO3 has been proved 
that its alkaline active sites on the surface prefer to adsorb CO2 rather 
than CO, which results its better CO selectivity. However, although 
LaFeO3 assembles the advantages of perovskite and iron oxide, its oxy­
gen releasing ability generally appears around 850 ◦C, while its activity 
is poor at lower temperatures [20]. B-site doping is a promising modi­
fication method for improving perovskite properties, which would 
induce other ions to substitute the iron ions [21]. Early reports have 
proved that proper substitution would increase oxygen vacancies, 
change B-site metal ion valence states, produce crystal structure defects 
and lead a directly effect on the based perovskite oxygen supply per­
formance and catalytic performance. Yan et al. [21]. have found the Ba 
doped LaFeO3 exhibited better carbon conversion and gasification effi­
ciency in the chemical looping steam gasification of algae, while Li et al. 
[22]. proved the Cu doped LaFeO3 showed the best gasification effi­
ciency in the chemical looping gasification of fermented sludge. How­
ever, most of the researcher concern about oxidation property of the 
doped perovskites, while its reduction property after chemical looping 
gasification are rare studied, let alone its applying in CO2 reduction. 
Inspired by this, herein, the torrefied biomass gasification and CO2- 
splitting were assembled as a whole chemical looping (See Fig. 1), while 
the M-doped(M = Mn, Co, Ni, and Cu) LaFeO3 perovskites were 
employed as oxygen carriers(OCs) in the chemical looping. The oxida­
tion property of the doped perovskites and the reduction property of the 
reduced doped perovskites were investigated via gasification and CO2- 
splitting reaction respectively. Moreover, the composition and surface 
topography evolution of doped perovskite were analyzed via SEM, TEM 
and XRD, while TGA, EPR and XPS were applied to reveal the reaction 
mechanism. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
2.1.1. The torrefaction of biomass 
The shell of camellia oleifera was obtained from Huanggang city, 
Hubei Province (China). Before torrefaction, the samples were dried at 
100 ◦C for 24 h, then crushed into powder with pulverizer and sieved 
through a 60-mesh sieve. In a typical torrefaction, 25 g of biomass (UB) 
powder was torrefied in a fixed bed quartz tuber reactor at aim tem­
perature (200, 240, 280, 320 ◦C) and held for 90 min with 100 mL/min 
N2 as protection gas. 
2.1.2. The preparation of oxygen carriers 
LaFeO3(LaFe) and the half doped LaFe0.5M0.5O3(LaFeM) perovskites 
(M = Mn, Ni, Cu, Co) were prepared by sol-gel method and the details of 
all chemical reagents were listed in Table S1. The La (NO3)3⋅6H2O 
(99%), Fe (NO3)3⋅9H2O (99%), 50%wt Mn (NO3)2 (99%), Co 
(NO3)2⋅6H2O (99%), Ni (NO3)2⋅6H2O (99%), Cu (NO3)2 (99%) and citric 
acid monohydrate were bought from Macklin. In typically, all metal 
nitrates were dissolved into 200 mL deionized water. Then citric acid 
was added into the above solutions in an amount of 1 M ratio to the total 
metal ions. The above mixture solution was stirred with 400 rpm for 10 
min, then the solution was transferred to an oil bath for evaporating 
(80 ◦C, 8 h) until the solution was transformed to gel. The obtained gel 
was dried at 120 ◦C for 100 min, then a sponge-like solid was obtained. 
After grinding, the powders were moved into a crucible, in which these 
powders were thermally decomposed at 400 ◦C for 2 h and calcined at 
900 ◦C for 4 h. The calcined sample was ground and sieved (60 mesh) to 
obtain the required samples. 
2.2. Characterization of biomass samples 
The structural changes of biomass were analyzed by a Fourier 
transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany) 
between 4000 and 400 cm−1. The ultimate analysis of fresh and torrefied 
biomass samples were analyzed via the CHNS and O analyzer (Ele­
mentar, Vario EL cube). The proximate analysis of samples (moisture 
content, volatile matter, and ash content) was measured by ASTM 
D5142-09. 
2.3. Oxygen carrier characterization 
The phase composition was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) on 
an Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation, an operating 
voltage of 40 kV, and a current of 40 mA in the angle interval from 20◦to 
80◦(2θ), with a step size of 12◦(2θ). Meanwhile, the crystallite size also 
derived from XRD via Scherrer equation, while the patterns were drawn 
after background subtraction. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy 
(XPS) experiments were obtained using Thermo-Scientific spectrometer 
with an atmosphere of 5 × 10−9 mbar vacuum and monochromatized Al 
Kα X-rays (1486.6 eV). The morphology of the perovskite-type oxides 
was determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on a Hitachi- 
S4800 FESEM instrument. The initial oxygen vacancy concentration of 
perovskites was tested via Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR, 
Bruker A300).

2.4. Reactivity test 
2.4.1. Thermogravimetric analysis 
The oxygen releasing capacity of the fresh OCs was tested by a 
thermogravimetric analyzer (NETZSCH STA-449c). The oxygen release 
test: 8-12 mg LaFeM was put into an alumina crucible, after crucible was 
settled in the TGA, 0.8%–1.6% H2 (balanced with Ar) at 100 mL/min 
were sent to the instrument. Then, the temperature was raised from 
room temperature to 800 ◦C with 20 ◦C/min and held for 40 min. The 
gasification test of TB (320) with LaFeM: the mixture of LaFeM and TB 
(320) with a mass ratio(m(OCs)/m(TB)) of 2 were put into an alumina 
crucible and settled in TGA, N2(100 mL/min) was injected to keep the 
inter atmosphere,while the temperature was raised from 30 ◦C to 800 ◦C 
at a heating rate of 20 ◦C/min and then maintained at 800 ◦C for 30 min. 
2.4.2. The tests of TB gasification and CO2 splitting on fixed bed 
TB gasification and CO2 splitting reaction with B-doping LaFe pe­
rovskites were carried on a fixed-bed quartz reactor. Typically, The 
mixed samples with 1.5 g TB and 3 g perovskite were packed into quartz 
tube with length of 310 mm and inner diameter of 21.62 mm. Quartz 
wool was placed on both sides of the sample to ensure uniformity of the 
inlet mixture gas and avoid the outflow of the OCs and TB. After the 
reactor temperature arrived at 800 ◦C,the quartz tube with samples was 
placed into the furnace and kept in N2 atomsphere with N2 flow at 30 
mL/min. During the reaction(60 min), the yield gas was collected by gas 
bag in each 5 min. After gasification stage, the N2 flow was kept for 10 
min, then the gas was switched to CO2 with flow rate at 20 mL/min. The 
yield gas was collected and detected in each 2 min until no CO being 
detected in the yield gas, then end the reaction. The generated gas was 
analyzed by the gas chromatograph (Agilent 6820) as the previous 
research [23]. Carbon conversion (ƞ) and carbon conversion efficiency

3. Results and discussions 
3.1. The effect of torrefaction temperature on the biomass chemical- 
looping gasification coupled with CO2-splitting 
Since torrefaction temperature played a key role in the composition 
of torrefied biomass, an proper torrefaction temperature was screened 
through evaluating the production distribution during chemical looping 
gasification. FT-IR, proximate analysis and ultimate analysis were 
applied to observe the influence of torrefaction temperature on the 
composition of biomass, and the results were showed in Fig. 2(a–b) and 
Table.1. It can be found from Fig. 2(a) that the weight loss of biomass 
gradually decreased with the increasing of torrefaction temperature, 
which indicated that the components of biomass were converted during 
the torrefaction. Moreover, the DTG curve(Fig. 2(b)) showed that the 
weight losing peak, which represented hemicellulose (224 ◦C) and cel­
lulose (283 ◦C and 347 ◦C), disappeared gradually with the increasing of 
torrefaction temperature, while the weight loss peak area of lignin 
(>400 ◦C) increased. Meanwhile, part of H and O were removed from 
biomass via the dehydration, decarboxylation, and dehydrogenation 
during torrefaction. Therefore, the C content of torrefied biomass was 
increased with the torrefaction temperature, while its value of O/C and 
H/C gradually decreased. Furthermore, the increasing of fixed carbon 
was accompanied by the decreasing of volatile matter, which may weak 
the connection between biomass and oxygen carriers. 
The effect of torrefaction temperature on LaFeNi performance during 
the gasification and CO2 splitting reaction were explored and the results 
were showed in Fig. 2(c–d) and Fig. S5. The changing trend of CO (Fig. 2 
(c)) showed that its yield decreased gradually with torrefaction tem­
perature increasing. 240 ◦C could be considered as a critical value of 
torrefaction temperature, the CO yield was more than 240 mmol at 
torrefaction temperature lower than 240 ◦C, while the CO yield 
decreased lower than 160 mmol after the temperature increasing over 
240 ◦C. Combined with the composition analysis(Fig. 2(b)), cellulose 
and hemicellulose content showed the same variety trend as CO with the 
torrefaction temperature increasing, which proved that CO may be the 
mainly gasure production from cellulose and hemicellulose gasification. 
With the increasing of torrefaction temperature (≥240 ◦C), the product 
distribution showed that hydrogen gradually increased, while CO

In the CO2 splitting stage, Fig. 3 (c) showed the variation of CO 
generation rate with time via different perovskites as oxygen carriers. 
The results proved that doping different metal in perovskites changed 
the CO generation time and generation rate during the CO2 splitting 
stage. For all doped perovskites, the rate of CO generation maintained 
above 15 mL⋅min−1 for 120 min. Although LaFeMn and LaFe only 
released a slight amount of oxygen during torrefied biomass gasification, 
they showed a promoting effect in CO2 reduction. For these two pe­
rovskites, oxygen vacancies may play a role in promoting the reaction of 
C + CO2 = 2CO. Besides, LaFeCu, LaFeNi, and LaFeCo promoted the 
splitting of CO2, especially, the rate of CO generation via LaFeNi was 
much higher the other half doped perovskites in the first 120 min. 
Furthermore, the effect of different doping metal on the CO yield and 
the average rate of CO generation during CO2 splitting stage were shown 
in Fig. 3(d). The CO yield was in the following order: LaFe > LaFeNi >
LaFeMn > LaFeCu > LaFeCo > SiO2. However, the production of CO 
mainly depended on the C content of biomass residue during gasification 
stage and the amount of oxygen uptake of the perovskite from CO2 [25]. 
Due to the OCs exhibited different activity in the stages of gasification, 
the high active OCs had less carbon residue and showed more oxygen 
consumption, while the low active OCs obtained more carbon residue 
and less oxygen consumption, which resulted the difference of CO pro­
duction was slight. In these two stages, the Ni-doped perovskite obtained 
a high yield of syngas(5.4 mmol g−1) and CO (45.31 mmol g−1), 
respectively. Compared with LaFe perovskite, Ni doping increased the 
average rate of CO generation from 189.89 × 103 mmol g−1⋅min−1 to 
221.00 × 103 mmol g−1⋅min−1 with a lower carbon residue content from 
gasification stage, which prove the reduced metal not only acted as an 
active centers for the activation of the CO2 [26],but also participated the 
reaction of CO2 reduction. Meanwhile, the change of surface basicity of 
perovskite caused by the enhancement of the reduction degree of 
perovskite may be the other main reason for the activation of CO2 [27].

perovskite, appropriate metal doping treatment can effectively control 
the oxygen release of perovskite. Due to the slight C, H, N content of 
spent samples after CO2 splitting reaction, the TG results showed in the

recovery oxygen amount appeared in the TG (air), the less recovery

of weight gain in TG(air) was: LaFeCu > LaFeNi > LaFeMn > LaFeCo >

of these perovskite was LaFeCo > LaFeNi > LaFeCu ~ LaFeMn ~ LaFe. It 
was normal to have little weight loss in an air atmosphere according to 
the poor performance of LaFe and LaFeMn in the gasification stage for 
the combustion of residual carbon. Since the structure of perovskite will 
be changed in the redox process, thus the initial structure of perovskite 
will not be the main factor in influencing the CO2 splitting reaction, the 
activation of C–O bond via active metal played a key role on the oxygen 
recovery. Consistent with previous studies, Ni and Co metal showed 
better activity during CO2 splitting reaction. 
3.4. Physical and chemical properties of OCs 
3.4.1. Composition evolution during coupled gasification 
XRD patterns of the fresh, reduced, and regenerated samples were 
presented in Fig. 5. The phase changes and grain size were shown in 
Table S3. The curves of fresh samples were compared to the Joint 
Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card of 
LaFe1−xMxO3−δ. There was no other crystalline phase that can be 
detected in the patterns of LaFe (Fig. 5.a), LaFeMn (Fig. 5.b), LaFeCo 
(Fig. 5.c) and LaFeNi (Fig. 5.d), indicating that the pure phase was ob­
tained. However, CuO was found in the fresh sample of LaFeCu (Fig. 5. 
e), which may be due to a consequence of a B-site deficiency in the 
perovskite [31]. Zhang et al. [32] proved that CuO began to be reduced 
by H2 at about 240 ◦C, so the first weight loss of LaFeCu (in Fig. 4) was 
much larger than other perovskites for the reduction of CuO coinciding 
in the LaFeCu. After gasification, all the doped perovskites showed 
relative reduction products, even these perovskites were not reduced 
completely. It should be noted that LaFeNi (Fig. 5.d) and LaFeCu (Fig. 5. 
e) samples showed some peaks of C after gasification and it indicated 
that crystalline carbon was generated through volatiles cracking after 
gasification. 
Due to the variation in the XRD patterns of LaFe (Fig. 5.a) and 
LaFeMn (Fig. 5.b) were slight, which proved both of LaFe and LaFeMn 
showed less effect as OCs on the gasification or CO2 splitting. Thus, the 
analysis were focused on LaFeNi, LaFeCo and LaFeCu. After CO2 
reduction, the XRD results showed that LaFeCo, LaFeNi and LaFeCu 
produced a large proportion of La2O2CO3 in CO2 reduction stage and the 
value of La2O2CO3/La was 20–30%. The LaFeNi (Fig. 5.d) and LaFeCo 
(Fig. 5.c) samples failed to detect the corresponding alloy and the peak 
of C that existed in the samples after gasification, indicating that the 
regeneration of LaFeNi and LaFeCo. However, the peak of C did not 
disappear in the pattern of LaFeCu (Fig. 5.e) after reacting with CO2. 
These results proved that the alloy in the reduced LaFeNi (Fig. 5.d) and 
LaFeCo (Fig. 5.c) promoted the reaction of C and CO2 as the function of 
catalyst, while Cu showed less catalytic activity on the same reaction. 
Qiu et al. [33] also found that increasing Cu doping amount was not 
conducive to the oxidation reaction of the reduced sample in a CO2 at­
mosphere. In summary, the reduced LaFeNi and LaFeCo absorbed parts 
of the oxygen from CO2 for their own oxidation and LaFeNi should be the 
best oxygen carrier for TB (320) chemical looping gasification in the four 
doped perovskites. The grain size changes of various components in the 
phase change of perovskites were calculated in Table S3. For the pe­
rovskites doped with Co, Ni and Cu after gasification, the grain size of 
ABO3-δ was smaller than the fresh and regenerated one. Moreover, the 
intensity of the ABO3-δ diffraction peak was reduced after reduction, 
which may due to the reduction of perovskite, but the intensity was 
restored after the CO2 regeneration.

M-doping modified perovskites showed weight loss at the zone of below 
500 ◦C, indicating their redox capacity were increased. It was possible 
that the Fe–O bond strength was weakened by the doping. The first 
reduction peak was positioned at 250–600 ◦C, corresponding to the 
surface oxygen transformation. It is generally accepted that the higher 
oxidation state of B-site metal ion would form more oxygen on the 
surface, thus the initial reduction would also bring these metal to normal 
oxidation state, such as Fe5+/Fe4+ → Fe3+ [34], Mn4+ → Mn3+ [35], 
Co4+ → Co3+, Co3+ → Co2+, Ni3+ → Ni2+, Cu2+ → Cu0 [36,37]. Notably, 
due to Cu2+ was easily reduced to Cu at low temperature, a much larger 
weight loss peak appeared at LaFeCu pattern. Based on the first weight 
losing ratio of doping OCs (Fig. 7(a):300–600 ◦C), the sequence of ox­
ygen releasing capacity at 250–600 ◦C can be concluded as follows: 
LaFeCu >LaFeNi > LaFeCo > LaFeMn > LaFe. An extremely slow 
weightlessness peak began at 675 ◦C corresponding to the deep reduc­
tion of metal cations, such as Fe3+ → Fe2+, Mn3+ → Mn2+ (bulk) [38], 
Co2+ → Co0, Ni2+ → Ni0. According to the second weight losing ratio 
(Fig. 7(a):>600 ◦C), the deep reduction taken most of the lattice oxygen 
from B-site metal, the sequence of lattice oxygen releasing capacity as 
follow: LaFeNi > LaFeCo > LaFeCu > LaFeMn > LaFe. 
The EPR results were shown in the Fig. 7(b). It showed that metal 
doping increased the initial oxygen vacancy concentration of perovskite 
(initial oxygen vacancy concentration: LaFeCu> LaFeCo> LaFeMn >
LaFeNi > LaFe). According to the performance of releasing oxygen of 
different perovskites under the atmosphere of H2, the amount of oxygen 
releasing during the reduction process was in the order: LaFeCu >
LaFeCo > LaFeNi > LaFeMn > LaFe. Combined with TG (H2) results, it 
can be found that the increasing of oxygen vacancy can improve the 
oxygen release capacity of perovskite. On one hand, the increasing of 
vacancy increased the adsorption oxygen concentration on perovskite 
surface [39], on the other hand, it enhanced the difference of oxygen 
concentration between the surface and lattice, which can promote the 
release of lattice oxygen [40]. However, the different doping metals may 
induce different influence on the Metal-O bond, which also affected the 
oxygen release property. Thus, the LaFeCu with the highest oxygen 
vacancy concentration showed the highest oxygen release ability, while 
LaFeNi with the weakest improvement of oxygen vacancy showed the 
third oxygen release ability, which indicated that the enhancement of 
oxygen releasing ability of perovskite doped with different metals was 
not limited to the single effect of oxygen vacancy or metal-O bond, but 
the synergistic effect of the two sides. 
XPS measurements were conducted to study the surface chemical 
compositions of fresh samples and reduced samples. The XPS analysis 
results of fresh samples and reduced samples were displayed in Table.2 
and the deconvolution spectra of Mn 2p, Co 2p, Ni 2p and Cu 2p of fresh 
samples were shown in Fig. S4, while the Fe 2p patterns were showed in 
Fig. 8. For the spectrum of Fe 2p, the presence of satellite peak at ~719 
eV and the asymmetry of the peak shape indicated that the samples 
contained ions with different valences. Hence, both Fe 2p peaks could be 
fitted to two peaks, as high valence Fe3+/Fe4+ located at 710.5 eV and 
low valence Fe2+ located at 709.6 eV. As shown in the Fig. S4, for pe­
rovskites containing Co and Ni, the effect of the Auger peak between 710 
and 720 eV [41] was removed through deconvolution. The Mn states 
were characterized by an asymmetric 2p3/2 main peak and an 
asymmetric 2p1/2 main peak. The peaks centered at 641.3 eV and 
652.9 eV were ascribed to Mn3+; the peaks centered at 642.9 eV and 
654.2 eV were ascribed to Mn4+ [42]. The peak of Co 2p3/2 was 
assigned to two parts with the relevant area at 779.3 eV (Co2+) and 
780.3 eV (Co3+) [41]. The spectrum of Ni and La partially overlap at 
840–860 eV. Concretely, the two peaks around 850.2 eV and 854 eV 
could be assigned to Ni2+ and Ni3+ [43]. Since the photoelectron peaks 
of Ni and La cannot be separated, the valence distribution of Ni was only 
used as a reference. The peaks correspond to Cu 2p3/2 could be fitted 
into two peaks, respectively. Generally, the band at 932.6 eV was rooted 
in Cu+, while the peak at 933.2 eV belonged to Cu2+. 
As previous researches, metal doping showed an important influence 
on the formation of oxygen vacancy [44]. On one hand, the charge 
compensation caused by doping would lead to the increase of oxygen 
defect sites in perovskite system [45], on the other hand, metal doping 
reduced the oxygen vacancy formation energy on perovskite surface 
[46]. However, based on the results of O 1s, the distribution of lattice 
oxygen and absorbed oxygen were inconsistent with EPR results, due to 
the absorbed oxygen also including the surface air pollutants. Zhang 
[47] proved that the number of Fe2+ ions in the system reflected the 
concentration of oxygen vacancies on the premise of maintaining the 
perovskite structure. Therefore, the distribution of Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions in 
doped perovskite was analyzed, and the results were shown in Table 2. 
Besides of the Mn doped perovskite, the Fe2+ ratio of other doped pe­
rovskites followed the sequence of the EPR results, which proved the 
doping accelerated the reduced of Fe3+ via the charge compensation 
effect and further promoted the formation of oxygen vacancy. Moreover, 
the increasing of oxygen vacancies in Mn doping perovskite may be 
induced by the extra existing Mn oxide, which provided more oxygen 
vacancies. Combined the results of H2-TG, EPR and XPS results, it can be 
proved that oxygen vacancy showed significant influence on the 
releasing of oxygen of perovskite, thus the choose of suitable doping 
metal would be an important strategy for perovskite utilization. After 
the gasification reaction, the biomass residue inducing other kind of 
oxygen to the sample, causing the increase of adsorbed oxygen ratio as 
showed in Table 2 (7, 8 columns). Moreover, the oxygen vacancy dis­
appeared for the perovskite structural destruction, indicating the metal 
activity played an important role during the CO2 splitting reaction.

4. Conclusions 
Half doped LaFe0.5M0.5O3 perovskites (M = Mn, Ni, Cu, Co) prepared 
by sol-gel method were used as the oxygen carrier and catalyst in 
chemical looping reaction coupled with TB gasification and CO2 split­
ting. Compared untreated biomass, torrefaction pretreatment could 
adjust the composition of biomass and the product distribution of 
chemical looping cycle process. Specifically, the CO yield increased from 
46.76 mmol g−1(untreated biomass) to 149.76 mmol g−1 (torrefied at 
240 ◦C), while the H2/CO ratio reached 2.0 during the gasification of 
torrefied biomass(320 ◦C). The doping of the B-site metal enhanced the 
oxygen release performance of LaFe perovskites. Moreover, the doped 
perovskite showed better CO selectivity and improved the syngas yield. 
Concretely, the H2 yield of LaFeNi was the highest (3.71 mmol g−1) and 
LaFeCo had the highest CO yield (1.98 mmol g−1), while the H2/CO ratio 
reached 2.07 with LaFeNi. During the CO2 reduction stage, the average 
rate of CO generation also increased from LaFe (189.89 × 103 mmol g−1 
min−1) to LaFeNi (221.00 × 103 mmol g−1 min−1). 
The measurement of XRD, SEM, XPS and EPR were used to analyze 
the mechanism. The results showed that the doping of Ni weakened the 
bond strength between metal and O and promoted the lattice oxygen 
transferring at gasification step, and the reduced metallic Ni further 
promoted the activation of CO2 and accelerated CO generation during 
the CO2 splitting stage. The study provided a new way for obtained H2/ 
CO = 2 syngas via the chemical looping coupled with gasification of 
torrefied biomass (TB) and CO2 reduction/splitting.
