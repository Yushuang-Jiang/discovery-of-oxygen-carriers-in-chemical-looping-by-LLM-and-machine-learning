A B S T R A C T   
In this paper, the effect of alkaline-earth metals (AEMs, Ca, Ba, and Sr) on the performance of CuO/ZrO2 oxygen 
carriers (OCs) and the hemical looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) process of wheat-derived hydrochars 
with these Cu-based OCs were investigated. The results showed that after multiple cycles, the crystal phases of 
modified Cu-based OCs were the same as that of pure CuO/ZrO2 OC without any interaction between them. Due 
to the catalytic effects of AEMs, an increase in the decomposition of wheat and hydrochar-235 in modified Cu- 
based was observed at 500 ◦C. The combination of Ca and HTC promotes reoxidation, while Ba and Sr inhibit 
reoxidation. Notably, the Cu-based OCs exhibited moderate agglomeration after 20 redox cycles at 950 ◦C. The 
results of the kinetic analysis indicate that the chemical order models and diffusion models can suitably describe 
the first combustion stage for wheat and hydrochars with Cu-based OCs, respectively. The geometrical 
contraction model was also found to most suitably describe the second combustion stage of all samples. These 
results suggest that a combination of hydrothermal carbonization pretreatment with AEMs modification is an 
efficient approach for CLOU using CuO-based OCs.   
1. Introduction 
Due to the increasing energy demand, a large number of non- 
renewable fossil fuels is being consumed and a huge amount of CO2 is 
released [1,2]. CO2 is a primary greenhouse gas with the highest 
contribution (65%) to global emissions [3]. Global warming is the most 
serious environmental challenge. Countries warming is currently 
considered the most serious threat, such that governments worldwide 
have acknowledged the need to achieve carbon neutrality [4]. In 
September 2020, China pledged to enable carbon peaking by 2030 and 
achieve carbon neutrality by 2060 [5]. Driven by this goal, researchers 
are investigating efficient CO2 capture technologies and renewable en­
ergy sources. Chemical looping combustion, a thermochemical conver­
sion technology, provides active oxygen by employing the circulation of 
metallic oxygen-carrying materials and avoids direct contact between 
fuel and air [6]. This technology not only can achieve high concentra­
tion of CO2 enrichment, and emits a significantly lower amount of NOx, 
but also has many advantages such as a high system exergy efficiency 
and the in situ reduction of SOx, HCl, and polychlorinated 
dibenzo-p-dioxins and -furans (PCDD/Fs) secondary pollutants [6,7]. To 
increase the rate of solid (fuel)-solid (oxygen carrier, OC) reactions, in 
2009, Mattisson et al. [8] proposed chemical looping with oxygen 
uncoupling (CLOU) for the combustion of solid fuels. The OCs used in 
CLOU, such as CuO/Cu2O, Mn2O3/Mn3O4, and Co3O4/CoO, release 
oxygen in the fuel reactor (FR), which is then regenerated in the air 
reactor (AR) [2]. As shown in Fig. 1, the OCs release gaseous reactive 
oxygen in the FR in an oxygen-deficient environment and at tempera­
tures between 850 and 1000
○C (Eq. (1)). The char and volatiles from the 
pyrolysis reaction are then react with gaseous oxygen (Eq. (2)) [6]. The 
combustion flue gas stream from the FR only comprise CO2 and water 
vapor. The high-concentration CO2 can easily be separated by carbon 
capture and storage technology via water vapor condensation; a small 
amount of CO2 is also used as the fluidizing agent in the FR. The reduced 
OCs are then transported to the AR and re-oxidized by reacting with air 
(Eq. (3)) [8]. The re-oxidized OCs are then reused in the FR.

Recently, biomass has gained significant attention due to its renew­
ability, abundance, and near-zero CO2 emissions. The utilization of 
biomass energy via CLOU can help to reduce reliance on fossil fuels and 
achieve negative CO2 emissions [9]. Adanez-Rubio et al. [10] reported 
that the use of biomass with CLOU resulted in the successful operation of 
a 1.5 kW continuous unit using a 60% CuO/MgAl2O4 OC. The results 
showed that the biomass was combusted completely and that a CO2 
capture efficiency of 100% was reached at 935 ◦C. However, the char­
acteristics of raw biomass (low energy density and high moisture con­
tent) significantly affect the energy efficiency of CLOU, such as increase 
transportation costs and smoke heat loss. Therefore, pretreatment 
methods are necessary to combat these issues. 
Recently, hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) has been used as an 
energy-efficient and environmentally friendly pretreatment technology 
for wet biomass waste [11,12]. During HTC, water acts as a solvent and 
participates in the reaction by providing H3O+ and OH−ions. After that, 
the wet biomass waste is converted into value-added hydrochars with 
high energy densities and low moisture content under autogenous 
saturation pressure. During HTC, the moisture contained in the biomass 
is removed in the liquid phase, thereby avoiding energy-intensive 
pre-drying processes [12]. Previously published works had confirmed 
that HTC can improve combustion performance and reduce pollutant 
emissions [11,13,14]. However, the chemical looping process of 
hydrochars has not yet been sufficiently researched. This study is the 
first to investigate the CLOU process of biomass-derived hydrochars 
with Cu-based OCs. 
Cu-based OCs are widely used in CLOU, due to the primary advan­
tages of fast reaction rate, high oxygen transport capacity and favorable 
combustion thermodynamics [2]. However, CuO is relatively expensive, 
has low mechanical strength and a high agglomeration tendency at high 
temperatures [8,10]. With the exception of the inter supports, such as 
MgAl2O4 and ZrO2, the use of low-cost exogenous heterogeneous ions 
for CuO modification improves the reactivity and anti-agglomeration 
ability of the OC. Arjmand et al. [15] reported that CaZrO3- and 
SrZrO3-modified CuO-based OCs exhibited rapid oxygen release, with 
the latter showing no agglomeration or deactivation. Abuelgasim et al. 
[16] also found that the addition of alkaline earth metal (e.g., CaO and 
MgO) increased the CuO uncoupling rate. However, the alkali species 
(Na and K) decreased the reactivity of CuO and led to sintering and 
agglomeration due to their lower melting point [16]. Additionally, 
alkaline earth metals have been reported to improve the solid fuel 
gasification rate and promote the combustion of tar and coke [17]. 
Therefore, alkaline earth metals are undoubtedly a better choice for CuO 
OC modification compared to alkali species. However, research on the 
use of alkaline-earth-metal-modified Cu-based composite OCs in the 
CLOU process of biomass is limited. 
Because of the crucial role of CLOU technology in the large-scale 
utilization of biomass in future carbon-neutral processes, it is impor­
tant to study the characteristics of combustion processes and the per­
formance of Cu-based OCs during the CLOU process of biomass and its 
hydrochars. Therefore, the primary objectives of this study are: i) to 
study the effect of HTC on the CLOU process of biomass waste, ii) to 
evaluate the effect of AEMs on the performance of Cu-based OCs, and iii) 
to perform a kinetic analysis of the thermodynamic combustion process 
using the Coats–Redfern method.

2.2. Preparation and characterization of Cu-based OCs 
The Cu-based OCs were prepared using a mechanical mixing method. 
CuO (AR, ≥99%), ZrO2 (≥99%, act as support), CaO (≥98%), BaCO3 
(AR, ≥99%), SrCO3 (AR, ≥99%), and SiO2 (≥99.99%, 10 μm) were 
purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. For 
the CuO OC, the mass ratio of CuO:ZrO2 was 60:40. For Cu-based OCs 
modified by AEMs, the mass ratio of CuO: AEM precursors: ZrO2 was 
50:10:40. Briefly, 60 g or 50 g of CuO, 40 g of ZrO2, and without or 10 g 
AEMs precursors were mixed with 300 mL deionized water. For a 
comparison, the same ratio of SiO2 was also added. The mixtures were 
continuously stirred (500 rpm) and heated to approximately 75 ◦C until

2.3. Combustion experiments using a fixed furnace and 
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) 
In this study, the stoichiometric amount of OC required for the 
complete combustion of the biomass or hydrochar was determined by 
the elemental analysis and oxygen transport capacity of Cu-based OCs. 
The ratio of the biomass or hydrochar to the Cu-based OC was set to be 
1:25 for all samples. The decomposition of wheat with the same amount 
of SiO2 was also carried out for comparison. The redox cycle reactions of 
OCs with the biomass or hydrochar was performed in a fixed furnace. 
For the purpose, 40 mg of the biomass or hydrochar and 1 g of OCs were 
mixed thoroughly and loaded in the crucible. The order of the reaction 
stages in one cycle was and follows: the fixed furnace was heated to 
950 ◦C (pure N2, 200 mL/min) → send the sample to the furnace center 
→reduction (pure N2, 200 mL/min, 20min) → reoxidation (air, 200 mL/ 
min, 20min) →cool to room temperature (air, 200 mL/min) → adding 
40 mg sample to the re-oxidized OCs. PerkinElmer-STA6000 was 
employed to perform the TGA experiments of the biomass, Cu-based 
OCs, and biomass-OCs mixtures. For this analysis, 20 ± 0.1 mg of the 
premixed biomass-CuO-based OCs (1:25) was loaded in the alumina 
crucible. The TGA was heated from 30 ◦C to 950 ◦C at a rate of 20 ◦C/ 
min under N2 atmosphere (80 mL/min) and held for 40 min at 950 ◦C. 
Subsequently, the gas was switched to air at 80 mL/min and then held 
for 40 min at 950 ◦C. In addition, the surface morphology of reoxidation 
OC particles was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, 
FESEM, Regulus 8100, HITACHI, Japan).

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characterization of wheat, hydrochars, and Cu-based OCs 
The physicochemical properties of wheat and hydrochars are plotted 
in Fig. 2. As expected, the C content of biomass increased from 41.64% 
to 55.83% and 66.33% after HTC, which can be attributed to the 
aromatization and polymerization reactions, resulting in a significant 
number of C––C bonds (Fig. S1) [12,14]. On the other hand, the O 
content decreased significantly from 44.93% to 28.94% and 17.76%, 
which can be attributed to the dehydroxylation and decarboxylation 
reactions, causing many oxygen-containing functional groups, such as 
C–O, C––O, and O–C–C, to break (Fig. S1). Correspondingly, from Fig. 2 
(b), an increase in fixed carbon was also observed, and the volatile 
matter was found to decrease from 79.15% to 58.24% and 54.64%. 
Although the ignition was more difficult due to the increase in fixed 
carbon and decrease in volatile matter, the hydrochars will exhibit more 
stable combusting profiles and higher combustion efficiency [22]. 
Meanwhile, the hydrochar with a low H/C and O/C ratio will reduce the 
exhaust smoke heat loss during combustion. 
ZrO2 is an excellent support material for OCs, such that CuO/ZrO2 
OC exhibit excellent oxygen uncoupling even after a 50-cycle operation 
[23]. The crystal phases of the fresh, reduced, 1-redox-cycle, and 
10-redox-cycle Cu-based OCs are shown in Fig. 3. As Fig. 3(a) indicates, 
the fresh CuO/ZrO2 OC exhibited ZrO2 characteristic peaks that can be 
attributed to the baddeleyite monoclinic phase (PDF# 83–0942), while 
the active CuO exhibited characteristic peaks that can be attributed to 
the tenorite monoclinic phase (PDF# 80–1916) [24]. As can be observed 
from Fig. 3(b), the reduced OCs reflect the Cu2O cubic phase (PDF# 
78–2076), with major characteristic peaks observed at approximately 
36.44o, 42.32o, 61.38o, and 73.52o. No CuO peaks are detected in the 
reduced OCs, indicating the complete conversion of CuO into Cu2O. 
After multiple redox cycles, the crystal phases of the 1st CuO/ZrO2 
(Fig. 3(c)) and the 10th CuO/ZrO2 (Fig. 3(d)) are found to be the same as 
that of the fresh OC. Notably, there was no interaction between the 
active component and the support during the calcination and multiple 
redox processes, which is consistent with the previous reports [23,24]. 
In particular, there was no evidence to suggest that the CuO and Cu2O 
phases in the modified OCs were affected by the AEMs at this mixing 
ratio. As can be observed from Fig. 3(a)–(d), the characteristic reflection 
angles and intensities of the CuO and Cu2O peaks in CuO/ZrO2–Ca, 
CuO/ZrO2–Ba, and CuO/ZrO2–Sr are the same as those of the CuO/ZrO2 
after multiple redox cycles. This indicates that the redox ability and 
oxygen capacity of the modified Cu-based OCs were effectively main­
tained. This result is considerably different from Cao’s work which re­
ports the production of CaCu2O3 and Ca2CuO3 when modifying CuO 
with limestone (mainly containing CaO) [25]. This can be attributed to 
the excessive loading of limestone (40%) during OC synthesis. However, 
AEMs can easily combined with the support material to form of CaZrO3

accelerate the transfer of oxygen from the bubble to the particulate

particles was reduced [38]; as a result, the combustion rate of biomass

the DTG values of the second mass loss peak were larger than those of

than that of CuO/ZrO2 under unit oxygen supply capacity; for compar­
ison, that of CuO/ZrO2–Sr was higher. This indicates that the catalytic 
effects of Ca and Ba are more sensitive to temperature than that of Sr. At 
the reoxidation stage, CuO/ZrO2–Ca still exhibited good regeneration 
performance, while that of CuO/ZrO2–Ba and CuO/ZrO2–Sr was found 
to decrease; this is consistent with the results shown in Fig. 5. 
Although the fuel grade of the biomass-derived hydrochar was 
upgraded, the increase in ash content may also negatively impact CLOU 
performance. Fig. 7 presents the TG and DTG curves of CuO/ZrO2 with 
wheat and hydrochars in one redox cycle. A significant change in the 
first mass loss peak for the CuO/ZrO2 OC with hydrochar was observed. 
First, the CLOU DTG curves shifted toward the high-temperature region, 
which is due to the decrease in the volatile content. The DTG1 center 
temperatures of hydrochar-235 and hydrochar-270 were determined to 
be 376 ◦C and 391 ◦C, respectively. However, the DTG1 value of 
hydrochar-235 was −1.01%/min, which is bigger than that of wheat, 
while the DTG1 value of hydrochar-270 was −0.25%/min. This could be 
due to the decay of biomass cell walls in the hydrothermal environment, 
causing severe damage to the hemicellulose structure and promoting the 
decomposition of lignin. In addition, compared to the parent biomass, 
the highly homogeneous amorphous carbonaceous and porous structure 
of hydrochar which is conducive to the transfer of lattice oxygen [39], 
which enhances the oxidation reaction between CuO and hydrochar. 
With a further increase in temperature, many C–O functional groups 
were shed due to the hydrolysis of cellulose and hemicellulose. Xiao 
reported that the hemicellulose and cellulose was nearly complete 
decompose when the HTC process temperature exceeded 250 ◦C [40].

3.4. Kinetic analysis 
The CLOU process of biomass with Cu-based OCs involves devolati­
lization, heterogeneous and homogeneous combustion of the volatile 
matter and char, migration of lattice oxygen, and release of gaseous 
oxygen [2]. The fuel conversion rate is also dependent on the reaction 
temperature and the extent of oxygen diffused from the OC particles to 
the combustible product layer. In addition, the physical structure of the 
fuel and the OCs are also affected by the above process.

CLOU process. The kinetic plots of ln(g(x)/T2) vs 1/T are shown in

highest activation energy in the first combustion zone was observed in 
hydrochar-270, followed by hydrochar-235 and wheat. This implies that 
the hydrochars had to overcome a larger energy barrier than raw wheat 
to induce combustion during the CLOU process. For example, the E 
values of CuO/ZrO2 for the three samples were 94.95, 147.52, and 
231.55 kJ/mol, respectively. 
In the second combustion zone, the geometrical contraction model 
(R2) most suitably described the reaction mechanism for all samples, 
indicating that the combustion rate was mainly affected by the forma­
tion of the Cu2O layer [23]. In this combustion zone (approximately 
750–950 ◦C), the nucleation of Cu2O occurred rapidly on the surface of 
particles and released gaseous oxygen for char combustion. Except for 
hydrochar-270 with the CuO/ZrO2–Ca OC, the E values of all samples in 
this zone were larger than that of CuO/ZrO2 OC reduction (250.6 
kJ/mol) reported by Ku et al. [23]. The increased activation energy can 
be attributed to the complex chemical reaction between the CuO-based 
OCs and the fuel, the composition of CuO-based OCs, and the synthetic 
process of these CuO-based OCs. Although the DTG peaks in this zone 
were larger than the former one, the activation energies of all samples 
were higher than those in the former zone. This further implies that 
there was a disproportion between activation energy and combustion 
reactivity and that the combustion reactivity of biomass or hydrochars 
with Cu-based OCs cannot be determined from only the activation 
energy. 
Guo et al. [19] investigated the CLC of cellulose with iron-based OCs 
and the E values were 219–230 kJ/mol in the first stage (236–373 ◦C) 
and 389 kJ/mol in the third stage (461–568 ◦C). These values were 
higher than those in this study. In our previous work, the E values of 
waste wood hydrochar obtained at 240 ◦C are 63.8 kJ/mol in the first 
stage (171–349 ◦C) and 270.8 kJ/mol in the third stage (458–633 ◦C) in 
air combustion [47]. While these values were lower than those in the 
present study. The E values of CLC process was the highest and followed 
by CLOU and air combustion, implying the combustion under gaseous 
oxygen is easier than lattice oxygen. Besides, different models used, 
sample properties, and different OCs also affected the E values. 
In summary, the kinetic parameters for the CLOU process of biomass 
and hydrochars with Cu-based OCs modified by AEMs have rarely been 
reported on thus far. Hence, the findings of this study are meaningful for 
the design of CLOU reactors utilizing biomass or providing theoretical 
guidance for the initial design of highly active OCs. Notably, deficiencies 
inherent in this method should be noted for its combining the kinetic 
process of solid fuel and CuO-based OCs. 
4. Conclusions 
The effect of AEMs (Ca, Ba, and Sr) on the performance of CuO/ZrO2 
OCs and the CLOU process of wheat-derived hydrochars with these Cu- 
based OCs were investigated. The main conclusions can be summarized 
as follows:  
1) The XRD and TG results showed that Ca, Ba, and Sr had no negative 
effects on the crystal phases and oxygen-carrying capacity of the CuO 
active component. Furthermore, the uncoupling rate was slightly 
improved by the modification of the OCs with Ca and Ba.  
2) Compared to that of raw wheat and hydrochar-720, hydrochar-235 
with the CuO OC exhibited the best CLOU redox cycle performance. 
The first mass loss peaks of wheat and hydrochar-235 at the medium- 
low temperature range (<500 ◦C) were improved for the AEM- 
modified Cu-based OCs, implying the improvement of lattice oxy­
gen activity.  
3) The HTC process and the doping of Ca resulted in significant 
improvement of the reoxidation performance of CuO, while inhibited 
effect were in the case of Ba and Sr. 
4) Although the active CuO components could be completely trans­
formed with no interaction with the accumulated ash, CuO still 
exhibited moderate agglomeration after 20 redox cycles at 950 ◦C.  
5) The kinetics analysis revealed that the chemical order models and 
diffusion models were best suited to describe the reaction mecha­
nisms in the raw wheat and hydrochars with Cu-based OCs in the first 
combustion stage. In the second combustion stage, the geometrical 
contraction model (R2) was suitable choice to describe the reaction 
mechanisms for all samples.
