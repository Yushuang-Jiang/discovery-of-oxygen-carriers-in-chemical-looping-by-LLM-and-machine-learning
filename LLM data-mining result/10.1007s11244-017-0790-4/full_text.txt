1  Introduction
The transportation sector consumes huge quantities of 
energy (ca. 30% of the total demand) in the form of dif-
ferent types of fuels, which consumption is continuously 
increasing. Consequently, producing fuels by more sus-
tainable and efficient processes is one of the most signifi-
cant challenges the society is facing nowadays in order to 
develop a cleaner energy system. Therefore, any alternative 
technology must contribute to solve this problem by envi-
ronmentally acceptable means and, in particular, reduc-
ing substantially ­CO2 emissions. Within this context, solar 
thermochemical splitting of both ­CO2 and ­H2O constitute 
an interesting route that potentially can fulfill these con-
ditions due to the use of a renewable energy source and 
feedstocks based on waste and abundant molecules. In this 
way, initially generated syngas (CO + H2) can be further 
processed to obtain other solar fuels, which in the future 
can contribute to reduce the strong dependence on fossil 
resources [1].
Abstract  Mixed oxides with perovskite structure have 
been proposed as promising alternative for the solar fuel 
production via thermochemical redox cycles. For this 
work, the system ­La0.6Sr0.4Mn1−xAlxO3 (x = 0 to 0.8) was 
selected according to its high thermal stability and rapid 
oxidation kinetics, and the influence of the Al/Mn ratio on 
the redox properties was investigated. The characterization 
of the five oxides samples with different Al content con-
firmed the high redox capacity and the favorable behavior 
of these materials in consecutive cycles, as analyzed ther-
mogravimetrically. The results show that following reduc-
tion at 1300 °C in inert atmosphere up to 0.32 mmol g−1 of 
­O2 are released, while a 10-cycle reaction test confirms the 
feasibility of long term operation with these perovskites. 
It was observed that the reduction extent was enhanced 
with increasing the Al-content, but the oxidation degree 
is maximum for compositions near x = 0.5, correspond-
ing to an ­O2 release of 0.318 mmol g−1 (δ = 0.132). After

Solar thermochemical cycles imply the use of a mate-
rial that works as oxygen exchanger in long period 
cycling reactions. Although many types of thermochemi-
cal redox cycles have been studied [2], those with two 
steps are the most attractive due to the simplicity of the 
process, which entails lower operation costs. The general 
thermochemical redox cycle in two steps with a metal

In the first step, the endothermic reaction (1), the 
material is reduced, partially or totally, at high tempera-
tures, liberating oxygen from the structure. This reaction 
must occur in a very low ­O2 pressure, using vacuum or 
an inert atmosphere to avoid the material re-oxidation. In 
the second step, the exothermic oxidation, reactions (2a) 
and/or (2b) proceed at lower temperatures after the injec-
tion of ­CO2 and/or ­H2O. In this way the desirable solar 
fuels (CO and ­H2, respectively) are obtained by splitting 
of the oxidants. Ideally, the metal oxide is simultaneously 
re-oxidized to recover the initial state, so the cycle can 
start again.
Several compounds have been proposed as oxygen car-
riers in the reduction–oxidation reactions and they can 
be differentiated according to the extent of the structural 
changes induced by the redox reaction [3–16]. The most 
adequate materials for these processes are the non-stoi-
chiometric metal oxides because they present very high 
thermal stability and do not require additional steps to 
recover the material or to separate the products. Among 
the non-stoichiometric metal oxides, ­CeO2 is the state-of-
the-art material for this process because it presents attrac-
tive redox capacity and durability, while no phase changes 
were observed after reactions. However, the excessively 
high temperature (1500 °C) required for achieving ade-
quate production rates [17, 18] supposes an important 
design drawback for production plants. On the other, dop-
ing ­CeO2 with other cations, such as ­Zr4+ or ­Hf4+, have 
showed to enhance the fuel production [18–21]. Recently, 
Marxer et al. [22] have proven for the first time the pro-
duction of solar-kerosene, utilizing ceria-based thermo-
chemical ­H2O and ­CO2 splitting. These promising results 
in term of fuel productivity and cycling stability demon-
strate the viability of the technology.
In the last years, perovskites have been proposed as 
interesting alternative non-stoichiometric oxides because 
they can decrease the reduction temperature about

enhancing the redox properties, such as oxygen non-stoi-

thermodynamic properties.
However, few perovskites have shown to achieve better 
or even similar performance than the reference ­CeO2 mate-
rial. Thermodynamic and exhaustive theoretical assessment 
has been proposed to evaluate the efficiency and ­H2 pro-
duction of the perovskites systems [23–25]. The most stud-
ied compositions are derived from (La, Sr)MnO3 perovs-
kites [26, 27] that show promising results. These oxides has 
been doped in the A-site with different cations including: 
(a) Ca, obtaining five times more yield than the ceria sys-
tem, [28]; (b) Y showing substantial CO production due to 
the high oxygen release capacity [29]; or (c) Ba, which has 
shown lower redox capacity than the LSM perovskites [30]. 
On the other hand, the B-site cation has been also modified 
with Mn, Co, Fe, Cr or Al [29–36], rendering encouraging 
fuel yields. It is also remarkable the good redox capacity 
of the perovskites involving ­Ga3+ and ­Sc3+ for the partial 
substitution of the B-cation [32]. Among these works, it 
is worth highlighting the notable improvement in the fuel 
yield of the system doped with Al, which has been stud-
ied for only three compositions and compared with ­CeO2 
by McDaniel et al. [34]. Furthermore, this perovskite fam-
ily has been investigated using a DFT calculation for the 
material design by Deml et al. [30] and experimentally in 
­CO2-splitting comparing the La–Sr–Mn–Al perovskite with 
the La–Sr–Mn and La–Ca–Mn systems [36]. In contrast 
with other cations, doping with Al has displayed a stabi-
lizing role in the perovskite structure by reducing possible 
sintering problems.
In this work, the influence of Al doping in the ­CO2 split-
ting performance of ­La0.6Sr0.4Mn1−xAlxO3 perovskites 
has been systematically studied, varying x from 0 to 0.8, 
in order to get further insight on the effect of the B-site 
composition, which incorporates two elements that pre-
sent good redox capacity and high thermal and structural 
stability [37]. These materials were synthetized by a modi-
fied Pechini method [38, 39], fixing the composition of the 
A-site, since previous literature reports have revealed that 
such Sr/La ratio presents optimal results for both reduction 
and oxidation steps and does not induce phase changes after 
redox cycles [40]. As to confirm these potentially favora-
ble characteristics, the redox activity has been studied in 
two ways: (a) thermogravimetric analysis, to determine its

2  Experimental Section
2.1  Perovskites Synthesis
La0.6Sr0.4Mn1−xAlxO3 (x = 0; 0,2; 0,4; 0,5; 0,6; 0,8) were 
synthesized following a modified Pechini method [38, 
39]. The different metal nitrates, La(NO3)3·6H2O (Sigma-
Aldrich, 99% purity), Sr(NO3)2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99% 
purity), Mn(NO3)2·4H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, 97% purity), 
Al(NO3)3·9H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, 98% purity) in the 
selected proportions were added to a citric acid (CA) solu-
tion in distilled water (in a 2:1 molar ratio of CA:Metal) 
and the mixture was stirred at 70 °C for 3 h. Then, ethyl-
ene glycol (EG) was incorporated in a 3:2 ratio (CA:EG) 
and stirred again at 90 °C for 2 h. After this step, the result-
ing gel was dried at 200 °C for 3 h, to remove the water 
and form a black resin, which afterward was ground in an 
agate mortar. Finally, the powdered sample was calcined at 
1100 °C for 10 h under static air using a heating ramp of 
5 °C min−1. The so-obtained samples are labelled according 
to the Al content as LSM(1-X)AX.
2.2  Characterization
Elemental analysis was performed by Inductively Coupled 
Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry, (ICP-OES) using a 
Perkin Elmer Optima 3300 DV equipment. Samples were 
previously digested in acid media in an Anton Paar Multi-
wave 3000 microwave oven. The textural properties were 
measured by ­N2 adsorption–desorption at 77 K (−196 °C) 
using a Quantachrome QuadraSorb-S equipment, previ-
ously degassed at 250 °C for 12  h. The specific surface 
area, ­SBET, was calculated using the B.E.T. equation. 
The crystalline structure was analyzed with a Philips PW 
3040/00 X’Pert MPD/MRD X-ray diffractometer using Cu 
Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å) at a scanning rate of 0.2°s−1 
in the range of 2θ = 20°–80°. SEM images were obtained 
by using a XL 30 ESEM Philips microscope.
Redox capacity of the samples was studied by ther-
mogravimetric analysis using an SDT Q-600 from TA 
Instruments, placing ~20  mg of powdered sample into 
a 90  μl alumina crucibles. The samples were heated to 
1300 °C at 20 °C  min−1 in an Ar atmosphere (reduction 
step) and then cooled down to 1000 °C, where the syn-
thetic air was injected (oxidation step).

2.3  CO2‑Splitting
Thermochemical redox cycles with ­CO2 splitting were 
performed in a setup schematically represented in Fig. 1. 
About 250  mg of sample were deposited in an alumina 
fiber bed in the middle of the reactor. The temperature 
of the sample was measured by an S-type thermocouple 
placed at the top part of redox material bed. The reduction 
step was carried out heating up to 1250 °C at 20 °C min−1 
in inert atmosphere (Ar) for 30 min, whereas the oxidation 
step at 900 °C for 30 min in a 5% ­CO2 in He stream from a 
pre-mixed cylinder. The total flow rate used for both steps 
was 50  mL  min−1. The output gases were analyzed by a 
Cirrus 2 MKS instruments quadrupole mass spectrometer 
periodically calibrated with standard mixtures. Gas com-
position was also checked off-line in an ­Agilent® CP-4900 
μGC using gas sampling bags and, in order to avoid peaks 
overlapping, He was chosen as carrier of ­CO2.

3.2  Redox Capacity
The capacity to release and uptake oxygen of the as-
synthesized perovskites was first investigated by thermo-
gravimetric analysis, performing the following tempera-
ture program: (a) reduction at 1300 °C in Ar atmosphere 
and (b) subsequent oxidation at 1000 °C in air. The 
results of the tests performed with the different materials 
are shown in Fig. 4.
Reduction capacity is determined by the loss of mass as 
a function of temperature in Ar flow, which for all the sam-
ples takes place in three successive events. Desorption of 
humidity from the solids at around 130 °C causes the first 
weight loss, while a second mass loss in the 700–900 °C 
range, depending on the sample, can be associated to the 
decompositions of surface carbonates (not detectable by 
XRD) on the basis on mass spectrometry analyses per-
formed in the fixed bed reactor tests (see below). Those 
experiments revealed different peaks of m/z = 44 trace (cor-
responding to the ­CO2 signal), which are attributed to the 
adsorption of atmospheric ­CO2. This fact could explain 
the uncomplete reversibility of the reduction process. The 
most significant mass loss starts at about 1100 °C and cor-
responds to the reduction of the perovskites by release of 
oxygen, which comes from the crystal structure by dif-
fusion of the ­O2− anions across the lattice to reach the 
material surface. These results show that reduction extent 
increases with the Al content, although difference for sam-
ples with high Al content (x > 0.5) are limited. This fact 
could be explained since, as mentioned before, the intro-
duction of ­Al3+ in the structure reduces the unit cell vol-
ume. This leads to stronger atomic interactions and, accord-
ingly, the stability of the perovskite is enhanced, but at the 
same time the redox capacity is diminished by reducing the 
concentration of ­Mn3+ cations.
Subsequently, the introduction of air at 1000 °C induces 
a very rapid oxidation of the perovskites that gain weight 
until values appreciably lower than those previous to the 
reduction. A slight mass increment is also observed dur-
ing the cooling of the samples, which is most likely due 
to minor partially reoxidation caused by ­O2 impurities 
­(pO2 < 10−3 bars) in the argon used as a carrier. Thermo-
dynamic studies for non-stoichiometric materials have 
reported that when the reduction capacity is enhanced, 
the re-oxidation capacity decreased [24, 25, 29, 44].

In Table  3 and Fig.  5 are summarized the amount of 
­O2 released during the reduction reaction, the oxygen 
reincorporated during the oxidation and the non-stoichi-

as measured by mass loss with respect to oxidized state, 
is 0.97 wt% for LSM5A5, which corresponds to approxi-
mately 0.318  mmol  g−1 of ­O2 released (δred = 0.132), 
assuming the process contributing to reducing the sample 
weight. This value agrees well with those reported for per-
ovskites of similar composition in the study of Dey et al. 
[29]. In the redox cycle, both reduction and re-oxidation 
extents increase with the Al-content until x ≥ 0.6 when 
the redox capacity starts to be hindered due to the larger 
content in Al. It has been reported than the role of ­Al3+ is 
to contribute to the stabilization of the perovskite, but it 
may also contribute to facilitate the reduction of ­Mn4+ to 
­Mn3+ and ­Mn2+ [23]. Nevertheless, as the amount of ­Al3+ 
increases the redox capability is progressively reduced, as 
observed in Fig. 5, because this element cannot change its 
oxidation state.
Therefore, for a more detailed study LSM6A4, LSM5A5 
and LSM4A6 perovskites were selected considering their 
better balance between oxidation and reduction processes. 
These samples were submitted to a 3 redox cycles vary-
ing the reduction temperature from 1300 °C to 1200 and 
1100 °C in order to determine the effect of this param-
eter on the redox behavior of the perovskites. The results 
of these thermogravimetric assays are presented in Fig. 6. 
As expected, the reduction extent rises with increasing the 
temperature because the ionic diffusion is facilitated and, 
accordingly, the degree of non-stoichiometry, δ, for a fixed 
oxygen partial pressure increases. In all the three cycles, 
once corrected by the baseline shift, the re-oxidation was in 
the range of 95–100% for all the materials and at the three 
assayed temperatures. A difference in the reduction rate 
can also be observed as a change in the slope in the TG 
plot of Fig. 5 after reaching the isotherm at 1300 °C. This 
indicates that perovskite’s reduction is not completed dur-
ing the heating stage and continues at the high temperature 
dwell time, but more slowly as expected for final stage of 
the process of oxygen release.

4  Conclusions
La0.6Sr0.4Mn1−xAlxO3 perovskites were successfully syn-
thesized by a modified Pechini method to study the CO 
production capacity by thermochemical redox cycles of 
­CO2-splitting. The redox capacity of these materials was 
tested using thermogravimetric analysis and a lab-scale 
fixed bed reactor coupled to a mass spectrometer. The 
morphological and elemental analyses reveal the forma-
tion of homogeneous and pure phases, with hexagonal or 
tetragonal structures, for all the compositions. Even after 
the high temperatures redox reactions the oxides main-
tain their structures and no phase segregation is observed. 
These results confirm the attractive characteristics of 
these materials for long-term cycling.
It has been proved that the reduction extent for these 
oxides increases with the content in Al, but an optimal 
re-oxidation range is observed for the perovskites with 
x = 0.4–0.6. However, it was also demonstrated that the 
oxidation kinetics for these materials is very fast. The 
results obtained performing successive assays indicate 
that ­O2 release increases with the reduction temperature 
and complete re-oxidation is achieved. That behavior was 
also confirmed during the 10-cycles reaction performed 
using LSM5A5, in which after a slight decrease in the 
second cycle, the redox capacity remained stable.
In the fixed bed reactor, similar reactions were real-
ized using a stream containing 5% of ­CO2 in He as oxi-
dant. The amount ­O2 released is in accordance with the 
expected values, but the CO production is appreciably 
lower, possibly due to the low concentration of ­CO2 in 
the feeding stream and/or other differences in the opera-
tion conditions. The best results were achieved with the 
LSM6A4 perovskite, which best balance between reduc-
tion and oxidation capacity. Again the XRD and SEM 
analysis confirm the stability of the perovskites after 
reaction at high temperature with ­CO2.
