A B S T R A C T
An experimental investigation on chemical activity and stability of innovative CuO-based oxygen carriers (OC)
supported on coal ﬂy ash, towards Chemical Looping Combustion (CLC) and Chemical Looping Oxygen
Uncoupling (CLOU) is presented. The materials were prepared by granulation of ﬁne powders of CuO and ﬂy ash
(FA), with 40–60–80 wt% CuO loading (Cu40FA, Cu60FA and Cu80FA), using a high shear granulator. CLC and
CLOU activities were investigated in a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) in the presence of a reactive fuel or an
inert atmosphere, respectively. A rather stable CLOU capacity (Ro-CLOU) in the range of 4–8 g O2/100 g OC was
found over 42 redox cycles, in agreement with the stoichiometry of the prepared samples. CLC oxygen carrying
capacity (Ro-CLC) with gaseous fuel, on the other hand, has evidenced a quick decline for the material with higher
copper loading (80 wt% CuO). Furthermore, both oxygen carriers with 40–60 wt% CuO were tested for com-
bustion of coal in a macro-TGA rig using CO2 as carrier gas and diﬀerent OC:fuel ratios. First, a blank test in the
absence of the OC was carried out to characterize the fast devolatilization and slow gasiﬁcation reactions taking
place due to the exposure of the fuel to high temperature and CO2-rich atmosphere. The comparison of the
weight loss curve evolution with and without oxygen carrier, and the measured residual carbon after test, en-
abled to extract the CLOU eﬀect for each combination of material/solid fuel.
1. Introduction
In a view of the current climate change and rapid increase of
greenhouse gases (GHG) concentrations, carbon capture, utilization and
storage (CCUS) has been widely accepted as an eﬃcient way for miti-
gating the contribution of GHG emission from fossil fuels combustion to
global warming. Among the proposed technologies for CO2 capture,
three main groups can be distinguished: pre-combustion, oxy-fuel
combustion and post combustion [1,2]. Chemical Looping Combustion
(CLC) is a zero-emission technology, with a very low eﬃciency penalty
and low CO2 capture cost, producing a concentrated CO2 stream ready
for utilization and/or storage [3,4]. The CLC process does not use air for
combustion, so avoids the costly oxygen separation from air needed in
alternative CO2 capture technologies. Moreover, the nitrogen is elimi-
nated from the process, thus strongly reducing the NOx emissions and
producing a ﬂue gas stream consisting only of CO2 and H2O.

The oxygen needed for the combustion reaction is provided by a
solid, the so-called oxygen carrier (OC). The OC is transported between
two reactors: the combustion or fuel reactor and the air reactor. In the

a result, the carbonaceous fuel is burnt in an oxygen-rich atmosphere
without nitrogen.

of units in the size range of 0.3 to 140 kW showing successful results
[5]. Worldwide data from 12 chemical looping combustors have been
reported in the last years and > 700 OC materials have been tested for

Whilst CLC technology is well suited for natural gas or syngas
combustion, its deployment with solid fuels such as coal and biomass is
limited by the slow combustion rate achievable in the solid-solid re-
action with the OC lattice oxygen. To face this challenge, in 1951 Lewis
et al. [11] presented for the ﬁrst time the application of a decomposi-
tion reaction of a solid OC releasing molecular oxygen at high tem-
perature for solid fuels gasiﬁcation. > 50 years later, Mattisson et al.
[8] adapted Lewis concept to solid fuels combustion within Chemical
Looping technology, and introduced the acronym CLOU (Chemical
Looping with Oxygen Uncoupling). In CLOU, the key step is the thermal
decomposition of OC at high temperature, providing molecular oxygen:

Since both devolatilization and gasiﬁcation of the fuel also occur in
parallel, tars and other light hydrocarbons, together with the produced
syngas, are combusted using both molecular (gaseous) and/or lattice
(solid) oxygen of the OC.
OCs activity for eﬀective decomposition of oxygen relies on both
reaction thermodynamics and physico-chemical properties of the ma-
terials [10]. The metal oxide redox pairs identiﬁed as the most suitable
are CuO/Cu2O, Mn2O3/Mn3O4, and Co3O4/CoO [7,8]. The system
CuO–Cu2O displays the maximum theoretical oxygen transfer capacity
of 0.1 g O2/g CuO, whereas Co3O4–CoO and Mn2O3-Mn3O4 account for
0.066 and 0.034 g O2/g OC, respectively. This makes copper oxide an
attractive material for CLOU technology, although the relatively low
melting point of metallic copper (1085 °C) limits high temperature
operation. As a matter of fact, copper decomposition begins at 800 °C
(0.1% equilibrium concentration of O2) and increases exponentially
with temperature (e.g., reaching 11.4 vol% at 1000 °C) [12].
Pure metal oxides are not suitable OC candidates [13–15], due to
their sintering tendency and mechanical weakness. Therefore, synthetic
supports such as Al2O3, MgAl2O4, SiO2, MgO or ZnO are typically
proposed in literature [7,10,16–18]. Recently, to promote a resource-
eﬃcient recirculation of materials with beneﬁcial environmental im-
pact, Aisyah et al. [19] proposed low-value ﬂy ashes as support for Cu,
Ni and Fe based OCs, as they inherently contain SiO2, Al2O3 and MgO.
The present study provides a new investigation on the use of ﬂy ash
as support for CuO-based OCs, with 40–60–80 wt% loading of CuO.
Materials have been prepared via a simple and cost-eﬀective agglom-
eration step, using a high shear granulator, and their CLOU and CLC
activities have been investigated in a thermogravimetric analyzer.
Cyclic CLOU properties of the materials have been ﬁrstly measured
under inert atmosphere at high temperature. Then, the OCs have been
physically mixed with a solid fuel (coal) and rapidly heated up in a
macro-TGA device; after decomposition of the volatile fraction of the
fuel at intermediate temperature, the combustion of char residues via
CLOU eﬀect has been extracted showing the potential of the prepared
materials towards solid fuels combustion.
2. Material and methods
2.1. Synthesis of oxygen carrier particles
Oxygen carrier granules with desired particle size were prepared by
agglomeration of copper oxide (purity: 97%, Alfa Aesar) and ﬂy ash
from Sobieski coal combustion, using a high shear granulator Lab-scale
GMX-LAB Micro (Vector Corporation). The adequate weight of solids
(100–200 g of ﬂy ash or blend of CuO with ﬂy ash) in powder form
(0.5–100 μm) was dried at 105 °C overnight, then introduced in the
1 dm3 vessel of the agglomerator and dry-mixed for 1 min. An aqueous

The ﬁnal materials were named according to their composition, i.e. FA

weight percentage of copper oxide in the OC.
2.2. Materials characterization
Particles crushing strength was determined using a Digital Force
Gauge SHIMPO FGV-10× apparatus. The crushing strength was taken
as the average value of a minimum of 75 consecutive measurements
undertaken on diﬀerent particles (particle size range of 500–800 μm) of
each sample, randomly chosen.
The identiﬁcation of crystalline chemical species was carried out by
powder X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) patterns acquired in an X-ray dif-
fractometer, Bruker D8 Advance, using a CuKα radiation source
(λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA. The diﬀractograms were collected
in the 2-theta range of 10°–90° with a step of 0.02°.
The initial speciﬁc surface of the oxygen carrier materials was de-
termined according to Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) theory of gas
physical adsorption on material surface, using a Thermo Fisher
Scientiﬁc Surfer analyzer. Degassed samples (under vacuum, at 200 °C,
for 12 h) were subsequently analyzed at the temperature of liquid ni-
trogen with the use of gaseous nitrogen as adsorbate.
The total and residual moisture, ash and volatile matter of solid
fuels were measured by gravimetric method. Caloriﬁc value was ana-
lyzed according to calorimetric method using a AC 500 LECO Isoperibol
Calorimeter. Elemental composition was analyzed using a TruSpec
CHNS Micro LECO Analyzer. Carbon, hydrogen and sulfur were ana-
lyzed according to high temperature combustion method. Nitrogen
content was analyzed according to catharometric method.
Mineral composition of ﬂy ash derived from combustion of Sobieski
coal was determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission
spectrometry (ICP-OES), using Thermo iCAP 6500 Duo ICP equipment.
Total carbon contained in fuel-OC mixtures after macro-TGA tests
was analyzed as follows: 20 mg of each sample was weighed into a tin
capsule (× 4 replicates per sample) and combusted in an Elemental
Analyzer (EA) connected to an Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometer (IRMS),
where all C was converted to CO2 at 1700° Celsius and subsequently
analyzed in the IRMS. The obtained peak amplitudes were used to
calculate the %C in the samples based on a reference standard (1.56%C,
n = 6) that was analyzed together with the samples. 2 empty capsules
were analyzed at the end of the run and the average carbon contribu-
tion from the empty capsules was used to correct for the capsule and
instrument background.

2.3.2. Macro-TGA tests in the presence of solid fuel
In addition to multicycling CLC and CLOU tests, OCs have been
physically mixed with coal and tested in a macro-TGA to evaluate the
CLOU eﬀect in the presence of a solid fuel. About 1 g of Sobieski coal
was mixed with variable amounts of CLOU active oxygen carriers (de-
pending on the oxygen capacity and on the desired oxygen excess in the
bed) and analyzed in a customized macro-thermobalance (macro-TGA)
designed and manufactured at Institute of Power Engineering in
Warsaw, Poland. The TGA device consists of an insulated and elec-
trically heated steel cylindrical reactor (0.06 m internal diameter and
0.5 m height) equipped with a set of thermocouples which ensure a
constant temperature inside the chamber. The sample (consisting of
physically mixed fuel and OC) is placed in a Al2O3 crucible (ID: 34 mm)
hanging on a scale located at the top of the reactor [22]. The crucible
containing the sample is introduced from the top of the pre-heated
reactor while preheated CO2 with controlled ﬂow (40Nl/h) is fed from
the bottom, preventing air to enter during the fuel/OC introduction
phase.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Physical and chemical properties of fuels and oxygen carriers
Sobieski coal and wood chips biomass were initially screened for
macro-TGA tests. Coal is reported to have a higher caloriﬁc value,
higher content of sulfur and ash, and lower volatile matter than biomass
(Table 1). Due to its low residual char content, biomass has not been
employed as fuel for CLOU tests; however, it has been used together
with coal to investigate the evolution of volatiles decomposition at high
heating rate in the macro-TGA (see blank test in paragraph 3.3).
Fly ashes derived from combustion of Sobieski coal – which are used
as oxygen carrier support in this work – have been analyzed by ICP-OES
and results are shown in Table 2. A signiﬁcant amount of Si, Al and Fe
has been detected. The main ﬂy ash phase detected by X-Ray Diﬀraction
analysis was SiO2 (Fig. 1, diﬀractogram a), while additional tenorite
peaks (CuO) have been identiﬁed on the synthesized oxygen carriers
(Fig. 1, diﬀractograms b–d). No relevant copper-support interactions
(e.g., CuO-Al2O3 or CuO-SiO2) have been detected.
Porosity, density and BET surface area of both support and OCs
(Cu40- and Cu60FA) are shown in Table 3. As expected, materials
density increases when copper is added to ﬂy ash supports, and is
proportional to copper loading. Moreover, a slight progressive decrease
in both porosity and BET surface area is observed when copper oxide is
added to the ﬂy ash support in the range 40–60 wt%.
Results from crushing strength tests of copper-enriched ﬂy ash
particles are shown in Table 4 in comparison with both raw ﬂy ash and
ilmenite particles – the latter was used as benchmark oxygen carrier
material. All the agglomerated Cu-enriched samples have shown ade-
quate mechanical strength and compare well with previously reported
CuO/synthetic supports OCs [16]. Fly ash and ilmenite displayed a

3.2. Multicycling tests in TGA
The evolution of molecular oxygen uptake/release capacity (Ro-
CLOU) and the CLC oxygen carrying capacity (Ro-CLC) over 42 and 90
TGA redox cycles, respectively, are shown in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 (a,b) for
the diﬀerent OCs, and are discussed in Sections 3.2.1 and 3.2.2.
3.2.1. CLOU tests
As shown in Fig. 2a, CLOU carrying capacity (Ro-CLOU) has been
found to be rather stable along 42 cycles performed for the three ma-
terials Cu40-, Cu60- and Cu80FA, and close to the expected maximum
value of 4, 6 and 8 g O2/100 g OC, respectively. As previously reported,
pure ﬂy ash support was found to be inactive in CLOU tests [19]. The
weight change as function of time for the three diﬀerent samples during
the 2nd and 42nd cycle is shown in Fig. 2b. Deactivation phenomena of
CuO-based oxygen carriers during CuO-Cu2O cyclic phase change of
CLOU tests have been previously reported due to copper sintering or
interactions with Al2O3 contained in the support to form copper(II)
aluminate (CuAl2O4) and copper(I) aluminate (CuAlO2) [24]. No sig-
niﬁcant variations of oxygen carrying capacity, however, were ob-
served between cycle 2 and 42, pointing out ﬂy ash as good candidate
for supporting copper-based materials in this respect. It is worth men-
tioning that the rate of O2 release during the ﬁrst cycle for both Cu60-
and Cu80FA materials was slightly slower (1.4–1.5 times) than for the
subsequent cycles.
3.2.2. CLC tests
As previously reported, even under low concentration of reductive
gas (> 1 vol% H2) gas-solid reaction (Eq. (7)) seems to dominate the
oxygen transfer mechanism of CLOU active materials (Eq. (5)) [21]. For
this reason, under the adopted experimental conditions (5 vol% H2 and
15 vol% CH4 in the ﬂow) common CLC gas solid reactions will likely
prevail over the oxygen uncoupling mechanism. The CLC oxygen car-
rying capacity (Ro-CLC) of Cu40FA, Cu60FA and Cu80FA over 90 TGA
redox cycles under reductive gas mixture is shown in Fig. 3a.
As blank test, activity of ﬂy ash support alone has also been mea-
sured (for 3 redox cycles) showing a minor capacity of 0.7 g O2/100 g
(not reported in Fig. 3a). When in contact with a reducing atmosphere,
Cu80FA sample activity drops by about 20% after 4 reduction/oxida-
tion cycles, close to the level of Cu60FA sample, and for this reason the
test has been stopped before the scheduled 90 cycles. The inspected
sample after test appeared to have a hard sintered cake shape; as a
matter of fact, deactivation phenomena are associated to metallic
copper formation as result of chemical reaction with the reducing gases:
increased Cu mobility and migration [25], cracking [26] and segrega-
tion of copper-rich nodules [24].
The measured Ro-CLC of Cu40- and Cu60FA (9 and 11.2 g O2/100 g
OC, respectively, as measured from cycle 2), on the other hand, was
stable along 90 redox cycles and compared well with the expected value
from the nominal loading of copper oxide plus the measured capacity of
the support, 8.5 gO2/100 gOC and 12.3 gO2/100 gOC, respectively, in-
dicating that copper oxide is well dispersed on the ﬂy ash support and
exposed to the gas mixture. This conﬁrms that the presence of ﬂy ash
may enhance the thermal stability of the oxygen carrier [19].
The dynamic response of the weight loss curves as function of time
for cycle 2 and cycle 90, for Cu40FA and Cu60FA OCs, is reported in
Fig. 3b. Both samples have shown a good performance with similar O2
uptake/release slopes. As previously reported [21] the oxygen carrier
underwent a slightly slower conversion rate in the ﬁrst cycle.
3.2.3. Kinetic data
The rates of reaction of both CLOU and CLC tests, measured in the
middle of each reduction/oxidation curve to account for the maximum
slope of weight change, are shown in Table 5 for cycle 2 and cycle 42
(CLOU) or 90 (CLC). Although the reported values might not express
the intrinsic kinetic rate of the respective decomposition/reduction
reactions due to heat and mass transfer limitation speciﬁc of the TGA
used (for instance, as a result of the unknown O2 partial pressure inside
the crucible, limiting the CLOU driving force), they clearly indicate that
CLOU reaction proceeds at appreciable and satisfactory rates. A fast
oxygen release is an advantage for future use in solid fuels oxidation,
e.g. lowering the solid inventory of the process [27]. Observed CLOU
rates are in line with previous observations (0.36–0.46 ·10−3 kgO2/
(s·kg OC) [21]).

ried out using either coal or biomass (1 g) in the absence of the oxygen
carrier. This test will enable to extract the eﬀect of the OC from fuel
thermal decomposition, knowing that mass loss curve of blank and
CLOU tests are expected to become substantially diﬀerent when the
material will release molecular oxygen, initiating char combustion re-
actions.
It can be observed from the blank test (Fig. 4) that the release of
volatiles takes place during the initial 150 s, while further weight losses
(e.g., residual char reactions with CO2) are negligible at the time scale
of interest for CLOU (1000 s). For biomass and coal the residual char
weight after devolatilization corresponds to about 7 and 59 wt% of the
original mass, respectively. As previously evidenced (Table 1) biomass
has a signiﬁcant higher content of residual moisture and volatile matter
as compared to coal, resulting in a lower solid residue after thermal
treatment.
A comparison between the blank (coal only) and combustion in the
presence of the oxygen carrier (coal + OC) tests, using Cu60FA mate-
rial at λ = 1.1 and 925 °C, is shown in Fig. 5. In the ﬁrst part of the
curve (t < 200 s) the devolatilization in presence of the oxygen carrier
is slower due to hindered heat and mass transfer. However, at t ≈280 s
the weight loss drops well below the devolatilization plateau of the
blank test; at this stage, the observed weight change is ascribed to
oxygen release and solid char combustion.

3.4. Characterization after test
The crystallite size of copper and copper oxide for samples before
and after CLC/CLOU tests have been analyzed by XRD using the
Scherrer equation. Since TGA-CLOU tests were ended after an oxidation
step, CuO was the main phase detected by XRD after cycling and its
crystallite size was measured and compared with the raw samples be-
fore test. On the other hand, CLC tests were ended after a reduction step
with gaseous fuel and metallic copper reﬂections were found to be
dominant. It was previously reported that crystallite size of CuO (111)
and Cu (1 1 1) (after CuO reduction) are comparable if sintering does
not occur during copper reduction; sintering would produce an increase
in Cu crystallite sizes compared to crystallite sizes of the corresponding
oxides [30,31]. Therefore both crystallite size of Cu (after CLC tests)
and CuO (as-prepared sample and after CLOU tests) have been mea-
sured to get an insight of possible sintering occurring after both CLC
and CLOU multicycling operation. 2-theta angles of about 43.1–43.6°
and 38.5–39.5°, corresponding to the most prominent CuO (1 1 1) and
Cu (1 1 1) reﬂections, were used for such a measurements, and results
are reported in Table 6.
Increase in the CuO loading from 40 to 60 wt% results in an en-
largement of the initial CuO crystallites. As compared with the sample
before test, Cu40FA shows no substantial diﬀerence in crystallite size
after both CLOU (CuO) and CLC (Cu) cycles (83 and 77 nm, respec-
tively). For the Cu60FA, on the other hand, the ﬁnal crystallite size of
Cu after CLC (112 nm) is sensibly higher than the size of the initial
oxide (67 nm) and the one after CLOU cycles (52 nm). This suggests
that sintering phenomena might begin at higher copper loading in
presence of reducing gases and deep reduction to metallic copper, in
agreement with Table 5 that has shown a slightly slower CLC rate for
Cu60FA compared to Cu40FA. The obtained crystallite size of metallic
copper were previously reported to be in the range 68–98 nm after 1–20
CLC redox cycles with methane as reducing agent and no performance
decay [32].

No change of the initial CuO crystallite size was observed when
copper loading increased from 60 to 80 wt% (Table 6). Moreover, in
agreement with the observed stable cycling capacity, the measured
crystallite size of CuO in the Cu80FA oxygen carrier after CLOU cycles
(73 nm) resulted to be comparable with the one of Cu60- and Cu40FA
(52–83 μm, respectively), conﬁrming a good performance of the ma-
terial at the highest copper loading for CLOU.
Copper aluminate phase formation, which would have resulted in a
partial loss of CLOU activity ([33] and references therein) has not been
detected by XRD analysis after test. CuO-SiO2 interactions have not
been found according to a previous work [32].
As far as macro-TGA tests are concerned, XRD analysis of the ma-
terials after test has evidenced a mixture of Cu – Cu2O, together with
minor peaks of unconverted CuO (not shown). In order to evaluate solid
fuel conversion, the carbon content in the residual sample recovered
after test was analyzed, and the results are shown in Table 7. The
conversion of residual char has been found to be in the range of
90–98%.
4. Conclusions
Copper-based oxygen carriers supported on ﬂy ash have been pre-
pared, characterized and tested in TGA for CLC and CLOU applications.
Crushing strength results were satisfactory. The materials did not suﬀer
from sintering/deactivation under CLOU tests, showing high and stable
activity along 42 cycles. Deep copper reduction under a reactive gas
mixture evidenced a decline in activity at higher copper loading (from
second cycle, for Cu80FA). At 60 wt% CuO loading (Cu60FA), O2 ca-
pacity was stable over 90 cycles although an increase in copper crys-
tallite size and a slightly slower CLC apparent kinetics compared to
40 wt% CuO (Cu40FA) were measured. Neither copper aluminates nor
mixed copper-silica phases were observed before/after TGA tests using
ﬂy ash as support. Molecular oxygen release rates (CLOU eﬀect) were
found to be fast for all the CuO-FA combinations. CuO supported on ﬂy
ash have therefore been pointed out as good candidates for further
research on resource-eﬃcient use of low-value materials for oxygen
carriers in chemical looping applications.
Macro-TGA tests allowed prooﬁng the reactivity of oxygen carrier in
the presence of a solid fuel (coal). After volatiles decomposition, com-
bustion by reaction with gaseous oxygen evolved from the oxygen
carrier was observed, and the detected carbon conversion referred to
the residual char in the bed was reported to be in the range of 90–98%.
