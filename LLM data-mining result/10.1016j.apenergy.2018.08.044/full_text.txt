1. Introduction
Redox cycles for oxides with multivalent cations have been studied
for a wide range of applications including chemical looping combustion
[1–6],
water-splitting
for
H2
production
[7–11],
CO2
reduction
[11–16], oxygen separation [17–19], and recently thermochemical
energy storage, particularly for concentrating solar power (CSP)
[20–25]. The redox cycles require cyclic exposure of the oxide material
to relatively low and high O2 partial pressures (PO2) for oxide reduction
then reoxidation, respectively. The cyclic exposure can be achieved by
multiple ﬁxed beds with appropriate valving [26–29] or by moving
beds [30] or ﬂowing (i.e. ﬂuidized and/or falling) particle transport
[1,23,31,32] through diﬀerent PO2 environments.
The viability of oxide-based redox cycles for energy conversion and/
or chemical processes are determined in part by the phase and mor-
phological stability of the oxide materials during high-temperature re-
duction and reoxidation. The chemical ﬂexibility of the ABO3 per-
ovskite structure has drawn much attention for redox cycles, because
the numerous cations available for the perovskite structure allows for
tailoring of material chemistry and thermodynamics to address per-
formance and stability targets in various applications [11]. Recent re-
views of oxide-based thermochemical redox cycles highlight the po-
tential of various perovskite compositions that have been explored for
energy and chemical conversion applications [11,33,34].

materials because cost-eﬀective, high-temperature energy storage is
needed to convert non-dispatchable solar thermal energy into dis-
patchable stored thermal energy on the GWh scale [51,53]. Low ma-
terial costs are critical for redox cycles to compete with existing or
emerging high-temperature molten salt storage systems for CSP
[54–56]. In addition, inert oxide materials such as Al2O3 [57], bauxite
[58], or sand [59] may also provide an inexpensive option for sensible
energy storage in competition with the oxide-based TCES redox cycles.
For these reasons, only redox materials with low-cost, earth-abundant
cations may be economically viable for large-scale TCES applications.
Perovskite-based TCES oﬀers higher TH above the limits of current
molten nitrate salts as well as higher speciﬁc energy in kJ kg−1. Redox
cycles for TCES also provide the speciﬁc energy advantage over inert
oxides, which achieve ≈100 kJ kg−1 of purely sensible energy storage
for every 100 °C in temperature rise [60]. This study assesses the po-
tential of redox cycles with low-cost perovskites for high-temperature
TCES.
TCES subsystems based on redox cycles can provide higher speciﬁc
storage capacity at the expense of system complexity with gas ﬂows
and/or pumping to support reduction and reoxidation [23]. TCES redox
cycles of simple oxides with multivalent cations such as Co3O4/CoO,
Fe3O4/FeO, or Mn2O3/Mn3O4 have been studied [61–65], but these
materials have limited applicability for large-scale applications because
of cost and/or poor reversibility due to slow reoxidation kinetics. Re-
searchers have explored doped or mixed oxides to improve kinetics
[22,66], but no low-cost compositions have clearly demonstrated a

2.3. Phase change identiﬁcation
Because the perovskite structure can undergo phase transitions with
increases in oxygen vacancy concentrations in the lattice, it is important
to identify the range of δ and T over which the crystalline phase
changes occur to enable thermodynamic ﬁts for each prominent phase.
The documented phase changes for undoped CaMnO3−δ include the
transitions from an orthorhombic phase at room temperature to tetra-
gonal and eventually cubic phases at elevated T [36,78,79,77]. With the
low levels of doping in this study, the CaMnO3−δ compositions may
also undergo similar crystalline phase transitions [5,80]. These phase
transitions must be reversible to ensure that stored chemical energy in
the reduced perovskite can be fully recovered as thermal energy during
reoxidation.
The phase change can be qualitatively observed with a DSC during
heating or cooling, similar to diﬀerential thermal analysis (DTA) [78].
In these DTA-like experiments performed with a DSC, doped CaMnO3−δ
samples are heated to 500 °C in air where the mass signal was given
time to stabilize at a fully oxidized baseline point. The sample was then
heated to 1100 °C in diﬀerent PO2 environments at a rate of 5 °C min−1
before being cooled again at the same ramp rate. Phase transitions were
identiﬁed by peaks in the heat ﬂow measurement that occurred over the
heat ﬂow associated with reduction during heating and reoxidation
during cooling.
2.4. Redox cycling experiments
Kinetic measurements of the reduction and reoxidation of the Cr-
doped CaMnO3−δ were performed in an annular packed bed reactor in
a similar conﬁguration reported previously for the Sr-doped CaMnO3−δ
compositions [25]. Perovskite particles with diameters (dp) between
150 and 450 μm were packed into an annular bed with a 2 mm wide
annulus. The packed bed was approximately 20 mm long with 1.0 g of
particles pressed up against a downstream quartz frit in a 10.2 mm
inner diameter quartz tube. Along the central axis of the quartz tube, a
grooved alumina tube (6.2 mm outer diameter) held three K-type
thermocouples to monitor temperatures at the bed inlet, midpoint, and
outlet. The packed bed was heated using a near-IR radiant furnace
(Ulvac Riko VHT-44), which focused radiant energy along a 100-mm
long uniform zone to permit rapid heating of the bed. The O2 con-
centration and total pressure at the outlet were monitored using an
electrochemical PO2 sensor [81] and an Omegadyne pressure transducer
respectively.
Two types of experiments were performed in the packed bed reactor
– cyclic isothermal kinetic tests and non-isothermal redox cycling tests.
Isothermal redox cycling can be useful for evaluating kinetic rates. The
particle diameters represent particle sizes needed for adequate trans-
port in a full-scale TCES subsystem with a central solar receiver for
particle heating. The relatively high porosity of the particles facilitated
high surface area and reduced bulk-ionic diﬀusion length scales needed
for bulk-phase particle reduction and reoxidation. Cyclic reduction and
oxidation under isothermal conditions were performed for a range of

4.2. Kinetic measurements
The thermodynamic measurements in the previous section provide
the maximum possible speciﬁc TCES for the doped CaMnO3−δ com-
positions at a given set of redox cycle conditions. In a TCES subsystem
for a CSP plant or other applications, perovskite particle reduction
occurs for a limited time at a given TH and low PO2. In a CSP plant,
chemical reduction in a central solar receiver may involve a falling
particle stream [32,58], a limited on-sun time for a ﬁxed bed reactor
[63,87], or a ﬂuidized bed of particles [87,88]. Eﬀective capture of
solar radiation into sensible and chemical energy of the reduced oxide
in the limited receiver residence time requires not only favorable
thermodynamics but also adequately fast reduction kinetics at the re-
ceiver operating T and PO2. Time scales for reduction and reoxidation of
the Cr-doped and Sr-doped CaMnO3−δ particles in this study are com-
pared using the packed bed redox cycling experiments described in
Section 2.4.
