A B S T R A C T   
Anaerobic digestion is an important approach to treat and utilize kitchen waste, while kitchen waste derived 
biogas residue (BR) disposal remains a serious problem in this process. This study investigated the feasibility of 
chemical looping gasification (CLG) as an efficient way to treat and generate syngas from BR. Besides, Mn-doped 
Ca2Fe2O5 (MCF) as a novel oxygen carrier with mild oxygen uncoupling capability was developed, and its 
performance against traditional Ca2Fe2O5 (CF) oxygen carrier was compared. Results indicated that, significant 
oxygen release capability was observed on the prepared MCF oxygen carrier. In the gasification tests, CLG with 
MCF at 800 ℃ showed optimal total gas yield, carbon conversion, and cold gas efficiency, which increased 0.08 
Nm3/kg, 17.22%, and 13.37% compared to CF, respectively. According to the XPS, H2-TPR and XRD charac­
terization results, the optimal performance of MCF could be attributed to the oxygen uncoupling characteristic 
brought by Mn addition. The formation of the oxygen uncoupling capability of MCF could be explained by three 
reasons: (1) Mn in MCF mainly existed in form of Mn3+ and Mn4+, which resulted in the extremely strong 
oxidability at a temperature as low as 250 ℃. (2) The higher Fe3+/Fe2+ ratio of 1.55 in MCF than CF caused by 
Mn addition led to greater oxidability of Fe ions. (3) MCF had a higher lattice oxygen amount than CF at about 
3.86%, and the lattice oxygen in its crystal structure had been transformed into active lattice oxygen species, 
which were between lattice oxygen (O2–) and atomic adsorbed oxygen (O−). As a result, this paper investigated 
the potential of CLG to treat and utilized BR, and the superiority of CLG with oxygen uncoupling (CLGOU) on 
MCF was highlighted. It’s hoped that this study could provide a fundamental knowledge on CLG and CLGOU of 
BR, and benefit to better downstream process design of anaerobic digestion industry.   
1. Introduction 
Anaerobic digestion is a promising biomass utilization technology, 
which converts organic solid wastes (especially kitchen waste and 
livestock manure) into biogas[1]. Biogas had great application potential 
in heating, combined heat and power generation, transportation fuel, 
upgraded to natural gas quality, and so on[2]. The emerging installation 
of biogas plants has led to a growing concern for the disposal of its solid 
by-product, namely biogas residue. A number of studies have indicated 
that biogas residue could work as an organic fertilizer or used on soilless 
culture[3–4]. However, due to its high transportation cost and less 
satisfying dosage controllability, it’s usually not applicable in large scale 
utilization. Thus, it’s urgent to develop a suitable method to treat and 
utilize biogas residue. 
The gasification technology can make good use of the organic matter 
in the biogas residue to produce syngas, which is widely used in 
chemical industry, petrochemical industry, and so on. Besides, some 
elements such as K in biogas residue have a positive catalytic effect on 
gasification[5–6]. But biogas residue also contains a large amount of 
nitrogen (approximately 1.53% to 5.79%)[7], and the conventional 
gasification method could have serious NOx emission problem. The 
recently developed chemical looping gasification (CLG) method is thus 
potentially promising to treat biogas residue with significantly lower 
NOx emission. CLG uses the lattice oxygen contained in solid oxygen

carriers to replace the conventional gasification agents, namely O2, H2O, 
and air. At the same time, CLG can easily control the amount of oxygen 
released to increase hydrogen production and promote tar cracking[8]. 
Through the oxidation of oxygen carriers with oxygen in the air reactor, 
nitrogen-free gasification (compared to air gasification) in the fuel 
reactor is realized while reducing energy consumption and costs 
(compared to O2 gasification). 
Oxygen carriers play an important role in the chemical looping 
process. Its performance is the key factor affecting the CLG reaction 
process[9]. Among various oxygen carriers, Ca-Fe-based oxygen carriers 
is a promising one[10–15] due to the following facts. On the one hand, 
Fe-based oxygen carriers have the following advantages[10]: (1) Satis­
factory reactivity and recyclability. (2) Better environmental friendli­
ness. (3) Low cost. Therefore, it is favored by a wide range of 
researchers. On the other hand, Ca-based oxygen carrier also has many 
advantages [13]: (1) Reducing the CO2 of synthesis gas and increasing 
the purity of H2. (2) Reducing the output of tar. (3) As an alkaline 
catalyst. Hu et al[15] demonstrated that a simple one-step reduction and 
oxidation for Ca2Fe2O5 could benefit the hydrogen production through 
the re-oxidation started from Fe by steam. Li et al [12] find that Ca-Fe- 
based oxygen carriers are more conducive to CO production through the 
basic research on the reduction behavior and kinetics of CaO⋅Fe2O3 and 
Fe2O3 oxygen carriers. But Ca-Fe based oxygen carriers also have many 
shortcomings such as general performance. Although Ca addition can 
enhance the reusability of Fe-based oxygen carrier, but it is still not 
satisfactory enough for industrial application. Besides, the complex 
reduction intermediates of Ca-Fe-based oxygen carriers will result in the 
poor diffusion condition of lattice oxygen and gas products[12]. 
Therefore, it is necessary to optimize the performance of Ca-Fe-based 
oxygen carriers. 
Considering the oxygen uncoupling effect widely reported in CLC 
field[16–17], a hypothesis of this work is that, pruning the Ca-Fe-based 
oxygen carriers with oxygen uncoupling capability could be an effective 
method to enhance its performance in CLG. Unlike the generalized sol­
id–solid reaction between gasification feedstock and oxygen carriers in 
CLG, oxygen carriers with oxygen uncoupling capability can achieve 
oxygen release at a certain temperature and oxygen partial pressure. The 
released oxygen from oxygen carriers would react with the gasification 
feedstock, achieving CLG with oxygen uncoupling (CLGOU), which 
features in the generalized gas–solid reaction (reaction between mo­
lecular oxygen and feedstock). Under this circumstance, the gas–gas 
reactions of H2, CO, CH4 etc. are in fact included in generalized gas–solid 
reactions [18]. CLGOU is believed to have superior performance than 
CLG because generalized gas–solid reaction between the gasification 
feedstock and the molecular oxygen is supposed to be faster than the 
generalized solid–solid reaction between the gasification feedstock and 
the lattice oxygen[19]. 
Plenty of metals (e.g. Cu, Mn, and Co) based oxygen carriers have 
been reported to have oxygen uncoupling characteristics[17,20], which 
is illustrated in the Ellingham diagram[21]. In terms of chemical looping 
technology with oxygen uncoupling, Cu-based oxygen carriers have 
been widely used in chemical looping combustion (CLC) due to their 
high-temperature oxygen release characteristics. Zhou et al[19] indi­
cated that the reaction is dominated by the CLC reaction and converted 
to the chemical looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) reaction as the 
temperature in the reactor increases. Cu-based oxygen carriers have also 
been investigated in CLGOU, and its increased syngas yield indicates the 
feasibility of using oxygen carriers with oxygen uncoupling capability in 
CLG[22]. Worthwhile, the oxygen release temperature of Mn is much 
lower than that of Cu, and the appropriate reaction temperature of CLG 
is usually lower than that of CLC[21]. Besides, the use of Co-based ox­
ygen carriers will make the fuel reactor have the disadvantage of overall 
heat absorption[20]. Therefore, it is inferred that Mn may have great 
potential in CLGOU, just as its extensively studied strengths in CLOU 
[23–25]. Additionally, the benefits of Mn also include strong response 
performance, mechanical strength, environmental friendliness, natural 
abundance, and low cost [26–27]. Besides, utilizing Ca2Fe2O5 could 
regulate the lattice oxygen’s activity and mobility, achieving the ho­
mogenous release of the oxygen. Therefore, doping Mn into Ca2Fe2O5 
may be a promising way to prepare an oxygen uncoupling oxygen car­
rier, which is suitable for CLG. 
Accordingly, this study developed a novel Mn-doped Ca2Fe2O5 
(MCF) oxygen carrier to overcome the shortcomings of the traditional 
Ca2Fe2O5 (CF) oxygen carrier. Both CLG (using CF) and CLGOU (using 
MCF) of BR were conducted to validate their feasibility towards treat­
ment and utilization of BR. With help of a series of characterizations 
including XPS, H2-TPR, and XRD, the role of oxygen uncoupling during 
CLGOU was also comprehensively discussed. It’s hoped that this study 
could provide a fundamental knowledge on CLG and CLGOU of BR, and 
benefit to better downstream process design of anaerobic digestion 
industry. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
The BR feedstock was sourced from a biogas plant in Beijing, China, 
using kitchen waste as the feedstock. The BR feedstock was dried at 105 
℃ for 24 h and sieved to fine powders with particle size under 0.200 
mm. The ultimate analysis, proximate analysis, and lower heat value 
(LHV) results of the BR feedstock are shown in Table 1. 
2.2. Synthesis of oxygen carriers 
The (OCs) were prepared by the sol–gel method. Firstly, Fe 
(NO3)3⋅9H2O, Ca(NO3)2⋅4H2O, and Mn(NO3)2⋅4H2O were dissolved in 
deionized water. The molar ratio of metal cations of Mn: Fe: Ca were 
1:1:1 (MCF) and 0:1:1 (CF) respectively. Citric acid was added as a 
complexing agent and the molar ratio of citric acid to metal cations was 
1:1. Next, the solution was stirred and adjust its pH to 2.0 ± 0.1 with 
ammonia. Then the solution was heated at 70 ℃ and continued to be 
stirred until the viscous gel forms in a magnetic stirrer. Afterward, the 
resulting precipitate was dried in an oven at 105 ℃ for 24 h. Next, the 
precursors were calcined in a tube furnace from room temperature to 
500 ℃ at a heating rate of 10 ℃/min in an air atmosphere, and then the 
temperature was held at 500 ℃ for one hour. Then, the temperature of 
the tube furnace was raised to 900 ℃ at a heating rate of 10 ℃/min and 
maintained temperature for two hours. Finally, the calcined sample was 
ground and sieved to fine powders with particle size under 0.200 mm. 
2.3. Experiments 
To simplify the reaction between feedstock and oxygen carriers in 
dual fluidized bed, a tube furnace reactor was used in a different at­
mosphere to realize the reaction in Fuel Reactor and Air Reactor, as 
shown in Fig. 1. The main reasons were that, in this study, only the 
component optimization, comparison of gasification effect and reaction 
mechanism for the oxygen carriers were focused. And the fixed-bed 
reaction experimental conditions were easier to carry out in the labo­
ratory stage than the fluidized bed reactor due to the difficult to stabilize 
in fluidized state. The gaseous products were collected in gas sampling 
bags. Before the reduction stage, nitrogen was introduced into the tube 
furnace with the flow rate at 200 mL/min for 10 min ensuring the inert 
atmosphere. Meanwhile, the tube furnace was heated up to the target 
reaction temperature (600–1000 ℃) and kept it stable. The mixture 
samples of biogas residue (1 g) and oxygen carriers (0.5 g) were filled in 
a porcelain boat. Next, it was pushed into the tube furnace quickly and 
started to gasify. The gasification reaction time was set at 30 min and the 
collected gaseous products were transferred into gas sampling bags. 
When the reduction stage finished, the atmosphere of the tube furnace 
was converted to air with the flow rate at 200 mL/min and the oxidation 
stage lasted for 30 min at 800 ℃. After oxidation, biogas residue (1 g)

2.5. Characterization methods 
The composition of outlet gas collected in the gas sampling bag was 
analyzed by Gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 7890A, 6 Ft 1/8 2 mm 
Molsieve 5A). 
Thermogravimetric Analysis (TG, Netzsch STA 449 F3) was con­
ducted to study the oxygen release capability of oxygen carriers, and 
then oxygen carriers were oxidized to realize the circulation. Firstly, 5 
mg of oxygen carriers sample was placed in the crucible. Next, oxygen 
carriers were heated from 50 ℃ to 800 ℃ at a heating rate of 25 K/min 
under a pure argon atmosphere, and the temperature was maintained for 
10 min when it reached 800 ℃. Then, the atmosphere was switched 
from argon to air (21% O2 and 79% Ar) and the temperature was also 
maintained for 10 min. The flow rates of argon and air were both 50 mL/ 
min. 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific 
ESCALAB 250Xi) was employed with the X-ray source of Al Kα. And 
binding energies (BE) of the sample were corrected by the C1 s peak 
value of 284.6 eV. 
Temperature programmed reduction by hydrogen (H2-TPR) of oxy­
gen carriers was performed using an Automated Catalyst Characteriza­
tion System (Micromeritics AutoChem II 2920). For each text, 100 mg of 
sample was pretreated at 200 ℃ for 60 min under argon. Then the 
temperature was cooled down to 50 ℃ and maintained for 60 min. Later 
on, the treated sample was hearted from 50 ℃ to 1000 ℃ with a hearting 
rate of 10 ℃/min under 10 % H2/Ar with a flow rate of 50 mL/min. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected by using Bruker D8 
Advance with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) in the range of 5-90◦with 
a scan rate of 5◦/min. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characterization of oxygen carriers 
3.1.1. Oxygen uncoupling capability evaluation via TG 
TG can be used to prove the oxygen uncoupling ability of oxygen 
carriers and its cyclic stability. Because, compared with a non-oxygen 
uncoupling oxygen carrier, an oxygen uncoupling oxygen carrier can 
release oxygen at a certain temperature and oxygen partial pressure. As 
shown in Fig. 2, the mass changes with time of two oxygen carriers 
during the five isothermal cycles of reduction (argon atmosphere) and 
oxidation (air atmosphere: 21% oxygen and 79% argon) at 800 ℃ were 
compared. With the increase of time, CF maintained its weight stable 
within certain fluctuations, and its weight didn’t show periodic changes 
with the different gas atmosphere. Therefore, it can be proved that CF 
didn’t have oxygen uncoupling characteristics. But the mass corre­
sponding to the MCF had periodic changes. MCF completed the first 
cycle from 0 to 50 min, and the weight change during this period can be 
divided into three stages. For stage 1, both oxygen carriers showed the 
weight loss at 0–10 min corresponding to the temperature rise from 50 
℃ to 300 ℃, which may be related to the loss of adsorbed components 
such as adsorbed oxygen of the oxygen carriers during the preparation 
and storage process. This is a negligible effect because the curves cor­
responding to the two oxygen carriers are overlapped[28]. Stage 2 was

the reoxidation ability due to the reduction of metal ions and generation 
of oxygen vacancies during stage 2, and the oxidation process was 
realized under the synergistic effect of oxygen concentration and 
temperature. 
The results of the TG experiment in the subsequent four redox cycles 
were the same as the obtained from the first redox experiment. However, 
their mass loss was slightly smaller than that of the first cycle. The 
reason was that the reduction time of MCF in the next four cycles was 
shorter than the first cycle, which led to the incomplete release of lattice 
oxygen. It was worth noting that the mass loss of MCF was almost equal 
to the mass gain of regeneration, and the oxygen absorption rate was 
much higher than the oxygen uncoupling rate. Therefore, the oxygen 
release performance and its satisfactory recyclability of MCF indicated 
that it was a suitable oxygen carrier for chemical looping gasification.

composition and chemical states on the surface of oxygen carriers[30].

extraneous polluted carbon (284.8 eV). The XPS survey spectra of two 
samples were shown in Fig. 4. The main elements in the two oxygen

addition of Mn. These results indicated that Mn was successfully 
incorporated into the crystal structure of the CF to obtain the MCF ox­
ygen carrier. Due to the different environments of the atoms, even for 
the same element, the binding energy of these atoms will be different.

The oxygen elements in the oxygen carriers can generally be divided 
into lattice oxygen (labeled Olat) and surface adsorbed oxygen (labeled 
Oad). As shown in Fig. 5(b), the O 1 s spectra was divided into three 
peaks (O1, O2 and O3), which respectively corresponded to molecular 
absorbed oxygen, atomic absorbed oxygen and lattice oxygen[34–35]. 
O1 included weakly bonded oxygen species and CaCO3, which proved to 
exist in the C 1 s spectra [36]. Both O1 and O2 belonged to the category 
of Oad. The addition of Mn in MCF led to the change of the chemical 
environment of the O atoms, which enabled it to have the ability of 
oxygen uncoupling. For adsorbed oxygen, the binding energy of the O1 
peak was 533.3 eV. The peaks of O2 of the MCF and CF were observed at 
binding energies of 531.4 eV and 531.2 eV, respectively. The difference 
of binding energies wasn’t enough to illustrate the generation of sig­
nificant electron transfer of adsorbed oxygen[37]. Besides, MCF had 
more amount of oxygen, but CF had more amount of Oad than MCF. 
There was the same reason for the high amount of carbon, which was 
that a more open crystal structure caused by oxygen defects was 
conducive to the adsorption of oxygen. Although there was more 
adsorbed oxygen on the surface of CF, it didn’t show the mass reduction 
of oxygen carrier under an inert atmosphere in the thermogravimetric 
experiment, which meant that Oad wasn’t the reason for oxygen 
uncoupling of oxygen carriers. As for the lattice oxygen, MCF displayed 
a shift of 0.6 eV towards higher binding energy, which reveal that MCF 
had more high-charge oxygen anions than CF[38]. It meant that the 
addition of Mn caused part of the lattice oxygen (O2–) in the CF losing 
electrons, but its binding energy was still less than atomic absorbed 
oxygen (O−). Therefore, this part of oxygen (Oδ−) became a kind of 
active lattice oxygen between O2– and O−. Compared with the lattice 
oxygen in the CF oxygen carrier, Oδ−was more likely to transfer elec­
trons to metal ions under the action of oxygen partial pressure to become 
O2. In addition, unlike surface adsorbed oxygen, lattice oxygen not only 
existed on the surface of the oxygen carriers but also in large quantities 
inside. MCF had a higher amount of lattice oxygen on the surface so that 
the overall oxygen concentration of the oxygen carrier was much higher 
than that of the CF. For MCF, after oxygen vacancies were generated by 
the uncoupling of the oxygen on the surface and the subsurface, there 
will be a large number of a natural concentration gradient of oxygen 
vacancies, which provided pathways and energy for the lattice oxygen 
inside the oxygen carriers diffusing to the surface and continuing with 
oxygen uncoupling[39]. In general, the high-charge lattice oxygen that 
was easily converted to molecular oxygen, and the higher lattice oxygen 
concentration of the MCF may cause the phenomenon of oxygen 
uncoupling, which explained the decrease of the mass in the thermog­
ravimetric experiment. 
High-resolution Ca 2p spectra had been shown in Fig. 5(c), and its 
peaks were divided into four sub-peaks (Ca1, Ca2, Ca3 and Ca4). Among 
them, Ca1 and Ca3 were respectively assigned to CaCO3 2p1/2 and 
CaCO3 2p3/2, which was also reflected in the C1 peak of the C 1 s 
spectra. And the production of CaCO3 can be interpreted as carbonation 
conversion on the surface of the oxygen carriers (For Ca2Fe2O5, 
Ca2Fe2O5 + CO2 → CaCO3 + Fe2O3)[40]. For example, calcined the 
precursor of oxygen carriers in tube furnace under air atmosphere, 
which contained CO2, could lead to the form of CaCO3. Ca2 and Ca4 
corresponded to Ca2Fe2O5 2p1/2 and Ca2Fe2O5 2p3/2 for CF and Mn/ 
Ca2Fe2O5 2p1/2 and Mn/Ca2Fe2O5 2p3/2 for MCF[41]. From the Ca 2p 
spectra, there was almost no difference in the binding energy of the two 
oxygen carriers, so the addition of Mn in the CF didn’t cause the electron 
transfer and change the valence of Ca[37]. Besides, Ca2+ in the Ca-Fe- 
based oxygen carriers didn’t undergo the reduction process (Ca2Fe2O5 
+ 6e−→2CaO + 2Fe + 3O2–)[12,15]. Therefore, Ca was considered to 
the inactive component and showed no effect on the oxygen uncoupling 
performance of oxygen carriers.

located within the subsurface and inside of oxygen carriers can migrate 
to the surface when the lattice oxygen on the surface was consumed. The

reduction peaks in H2-TPR revealed that there were various bonding 
structures of oxygen elements in oxygen carriers.

cause. Besides, the diffraction peaks of MCF moved to a large diffraction 
degree, which was caused by the change of lattice constant by referring 
to the Bragg equation. It was further proved that the doping of manga­
nese caused a smaller crystallite size. The reason may be that the 
effective ionic radius of Mn4+ (0.53 Å) and Mn3+ (high spin 0.645 Å, low 
spin 0.58 Å) was similar to the ionic radius of Fe3+ (high spin 0.645 Å, 
low spin 0.55 Å) and Fe2+ (high spin 0.78 Å, low spin 0.61 Å), but their 
ionic radius was much smaller than that of Ca2+ (1 Å)[57–58]. The 
smaller crystallite size is also related to the high valence state of Mn. 
Their addition caused an electronic unbalance in the lattice that was 
compensated by reducing the formation of oxygen vacancies (VO
2+)[59]. 
This also verified the previous analysis that MCF had a more compact 
crystal structure and thus showed a higher lattice oxygen concentration. 
What’s more, the increase of average valence state of metal ions due to 
manganese doping improved the structural stability by inhibiting the 
effect of deformation of the coordination polyhedron caused by the 
mutual repulsion of electrons (Jahn-Teller effect)[57].

3.2. Gasification performance 
By the previous analysis, MCF had a certain oxygen uncoupling 
ability and the reasons had been studied for the better design of oxygen 
carriers. Therefore, whether CLGOU of MCF can improve the gasifica­
tion effect to effectively treat biogas residue should be further studied. 
The reaction temperature is a vital parameter that affects the perfor­
mance of gasification. This section compared the reactions of two oxy­
gen carriers at different reaction temperatures. And the main reactions 
involved during the CLG of biogas residue were shown in Table 4. 
As shown in Fig. 9, the gas yield of CLG with MCF and CF increased 
with the temperature in the range of 600–800 ℃. This can be attributed 
that high temperature is helpful to enhance the pyrolysis of biogas res­
idue, tar cracking and char gasification[60]. In addition, the water–gas 
shift reaction (WGS), Boudouard reaction, methane steam reforming 
and methane dry reforming were all endothermic reactions. The high 
temperature was conducive to push the balance to the right hand leading 
to the higher gas yield. The opposite trend appeared within the range of 
800–1000 ℃. According to the literature, when temperature was higher 
than 850 ℃, agglomeration and sintering would occur on Fe-based ox­
ygen carriers. Therefore, the opposite trend of gas yield was caused by 
the agglomeration and sintering of oxygen carriers, which inhibited the 
release of lattice oxygen and adverse for gasification[10,61]. By 
comparing of two oxygen carriers, it can be found that the gasification of 
MCF basically had a higher gas yield after 700 ℃. This could be 
attributed to the formation of new phases by Mn doping, such as 
Ca3Fe1.5Mn1.5O8. The formation of these new phases made the oxygen 
carrier possess oxygen uncoupling characteristics, which have been re­
ported to be effective for increasing gas yield in previous publications 
[22]. And its total gas yield at 800 ℃ can reach 0.56 Nm3/kg. 
For further research, Fig. 9 also showed the gas yield of each 
component in CLG with different oxygen carriers. The gas yield of each 
component corresponding to the gasification of two oxygen carriers 
showed an upward trend in the range of 600–700 ℃, which had the 
same reason for the increase in total gas yield. The yield of CO2 showed a 
downward trend with the increase of reaction temperature from 700 ℃ 
to 1000 ℃. This could be attributed to that the increased temperature 
would strongly enhance the char gasification rate and further react with 
CO2 (CO2 + C → CO), leading to the reduction of CO2 yield[62]. And 
previous analysis proved that CF had more CaCO3, which can explain 
that the highest CO yield of CF corresponded to the reaction temperature 
of 900 ℃, which was higher than 800 ℃ of MCF. What’s more, the re­
action of CO and lattice oxygen of oxygen carriers or molecular oxygen 
was exothermic, which will also inhibit the production of CO2. 
Compared with CF, when the temperature was higher than 700 ℃, the 
gasification of MCF had higher CO yield due to its extreme oxidability 
caused by oxygen uncoupling. However, MCF had less CO2 production. 
It indicated that the surface sites of MCF may have a catalytic effect on 
the conversion of CO2. As for hydrogen, the low H2 selectivity and CH4 
conversion of Fe-based oxygen carriers made the H2 yield of CF gasifi­
cation increase firstly with the increase of reaction temperature, and 
then remain stable[63]. But for MCF, its H2 yield increased with the 
increase of reaction temperature. And when the temperature was higher 
than 800 ℃, its hydrogen production was higher than that of CF. The 
reason could be that the addition of Mn promoted the rupture of hy­
droxyl (C-OH) at higher temperature. And the breaking of C-OH bond is 
the precursor reaction of the water–gas shift reaction, which can pro­
mote the generation of hydrogen. Therefore, the doping of Mn had great 
potential in improving the hydrogen production performance of Fe- 
based oxygen carriers.

3.3. Reaction mechanism 
The reaction of the oxygen carriers in chemical looping gasification 
includes three steps[21]: (1) Generation of oxygen vacancies. (2) 
Diffusion of ions. (3) Reaction of oxygen on the surface. And the reaction 
models of MCF and CF oxygen carriers are shown in Scheme. 1. The 
oxygen vacancy generation and ion diffusion of MCF and CF are the 
same. In the fuel reactor, oxygen carriers undergo a reduction reaction. 
The oxygen carriers generate oxygen vacancies and movable oxygen 
ions after absorbing the energy required to form oxygen vacancies 
(△Evac). Because of the different positions of the lattice oxygen, there 
are different situations about diffusion. Oxygen ions on the surface of the 
oxygen carriers directly participate in the surface reaction. The oxygen 
ions inside or on the surface of oxygen carriers should diffuse to the 
surface of oxygen carriers under the chemical potential gradient of 
oxygen, and then participate in the reaction. At this time, the charge 
balance is achieved by the reverse flow of electrons relative to oxygen 
ions. These electrons are fed back to the metal cation-oxygen network 
causing the change in the valence of the metal cations. As for the surface 
reaction, MCF and CF have different reaction patterns. For CF, the lattice 
oxygen on the surface reacts directly with the products after the pyrol­
ysis of biogas residue. For MCF, from the XPS results, the existence of 
high valance Mn (Mn4+ and Mn3+) and higher Fe3+/Fe2+ ratio led to the 
higher lattice oxygen amount and convert its lattice oxygen (O2–) to 
active lattice oxygen species (Oδ−). H2-TPR and XRD characterization 
also supported this conclusion. Therefore, MCF could provide molecular 
oxygen by the uncoupling of active lattice oxygen species at certain 
temperatures and oxygen partial pressure. Then, the molecular oxygen 
reacts with the pyrolysis products of biogas residue to produce the 
syngas with more CO yield and efficiency. In an air reactor, oxygen 
carriers undergo an oxidation reaction and both of the oxygen carriers 
have the same pattern: Oxygen is incorporated into the crystal lattice of 
oxygen carriers, and then oxygen ions diffuse into their interior and 
subsurface and combine with oxygen vacancies. 
4. Conclusions 
In this work, CF and MCF oxygen carriers were prepared by sol–gel 
method, and the oxygen uncoupling characteristics of MCF were verified 
by thermogravimetric analysis. XPS, H2-TPR and XRD were used to 
analyze the possible causes of oxygen uncoupling caused by Mn doping. 
Finally, CLG experiments on CF and MCF were conducted in a tube 
furnace reactor to validate its feasibility towards BR treatment and 
utilization. The primary conclusions from the studies were summarized 
as follows: 
(1) The oxygen uncoupling capability of MCF was validated, which 
could be attributed to two reasons. For metal cations, the doped Mn in 
phases of Mn3+ and Mn4+ led to higher Fe3+/Fe2+ ratio, and gave CF the 
oxygen uncoupling capability. For oxygen anions, Mn doping led to a 
higher lattice oxygen amount of MCF, and its lattice oxygen was thus 
transformed into active lattice oxygen species between lattice oxygen 
(O2–) and atomic adsorbed oxygen (O−). Both of them could enhance the 
oxygen uncoupling capability. 
(2) The potential of CLG to treat and utilized BR was investigated, 
and the superiority of CLG with oxygen uncoupling (CLGOU) on MCF 
was highlighted. CLG of BR on MCF showed optimal total gas yield, 
carbon conversion, and cold gas efficiency at 800 ℃, which increased
