A B S T R A C T
The prime aim of this study was to examine the thermal reduction (TR) and CO2 splitting (CS) capacity of each
PrxSr(1−x)MnO3−δ (PSM) perovskite. A solution combustion synthesis (SCS) approach was applied for the
synthesis of PSM perovskites wherein glycine was utilized as the fuel. As-synthesized PSM perovskites were first
annealed up to 1000 °C in air and the calcined powder obtained was further analyzed using multiple char-
acterization techniques. A high-temperature experimental set-up consisting of a Setaram SETSYS Evolution
thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) was developed and utilized for the examination of the SCS derived PSM
perovskites towards thermochemical CO2 splitting reactions. The obtained results indicate that the lower Pr and
higher Sr atomic concentrations are beneficial to achieve the maximum amounts of O2 released (nO2) and CO
produced () in each thermochemical cycle. Based on the average nO2 and , the Pr0.18Sr0.80Mn0.99O2.951 (PSM2)
and Pr0.39Sr0.63Mn0.98O2.979 (PSM4) were identified as the best choices for the solar thermochemical conversion
of CO2. When compared with the CeO2 material (which is considered as a yardstick), the average nO2 and by all
the PSM perovskites was considerably higher for each cycle.
1. Introduction
Exploration of the methodologies such as solar-driven metal oxide
(MO) based splitting of H2O/CO2 is essential for the production of
carbon free alternative fuels. In this process, the solar radiations are
concentrated at one location to generate the temperature required to
convert the thermodynamically stable H2O/CO2 molecules into H2 or
syngas. The H2 can be used directly as a fuel and the produced syngas
can be converted into the value added chemicals and fuels via catalytic
Fisher Tropshe (FT) technology [1]. This way of utilization of the solar
energy will help to reduce the usage and dependence on the non-
renewable fossil fuels. Solar fuels produced from this process provides a
promising option to fulfill the constantly rising demand of energy [2].
The productivity of the solar thermochemical fuel production pro-
cesses mainly depends upon the redox reactivity (RR) of the employed
MOs. Therefore, until now, several volatile and non-volatile MOs were
securitized for both H2O and CO2 splitting reactions which includes zinc
oxide [3–7], tin oxide [8–11], iron oxide [12–15], ceria and doped ceria
[16,17,26,18–25],

are considered as the most promising candidates for thermochemical
H2O/CO2 splitting due their faster surface kinetics, reduced cyclic
period, high oxygen storage capacity, long term material stability, and
high O2 diffusion rates as compared to other MOs. In contrast, the ceria
based materials possess the limitation allied to the requirement of
elevated thermal reduction (TR) temperatures for the liberation of
sufficient amount of O2 [24].
The findings reported in the recent investigations shows that the
perovskite based oxides (ABO3) can be utilized as an alternative to the
ceria based redox materials as the temperatures needed to drive the TR
of perovskites is considerably lower as compared to ceria based redox
materials. The two-step TR and H2O (WS) and CO2 splitting (CS) re-

2. Experimental
2.1. Chemicals
The precursors i.e., praseodymium (III) nitrate hexahydrate, man-
ganese (II) nitrate tetrahydrate, and strontium nitrate acquired from
Sigma Aldrich, USA were used directly without any further treatment.
An aqueous solution of metal precursors and glycine were prepared by
using deionized water prepared by using the reverse osmosis set-up
procured from Millipore, France. Gas cylinders containing a) inert Ar
and b) mixture of 50% CO2 in Ar were purchased from Buzwair Gases,
Qatar.
2.2. Synthesis of PSM perovskites
The PSM perovskites were synthesized by using the SCS approach.
Various combinations of the PSM perovskites were derived by varying
the atomic concentration of Pr in the range of 0.1 to 0.9 (Table 2). In
the SCS method, the glycine was used as a fuel (with a stoichiometric
ratio = 1). All the steps involved in the synthesis of PSM perovskites via
SCS approach are listed in Fig. 2. To start with, the amount of metal
precursors needed for the production of 1 g of the PSM perovskites were
computed. The calculated amounts of metal precursors and the corre-
sponding amount of glycine (estimated based on the summation of
oxidation states of metal cations) were dissolved in the deionized water
(around 50 ml). After dissolution, preheating of the precursor solution
up to 300 °C was attained by using a temperature-controlled hot plate.
Due to this preheating, the water from the precursor solution was
evaporated and a thick viscous gel containing the metal precursors and
glycine was developed. After achieving the formation of the think vis-
cous gel, the temperature of the gel was further increased up to the
auto-ignition temperature. After reaching the auto-ignition tempera-
ture, a sudden rise in the sample temperature was observed (e.g.,
around 640 K as per Fig. 2c) due to the exothermic heat generated
during the propagation of the combustion reaction and formation of
PSM powder was achieved. The obtained PSM powder was further
calcined up to 1000 °C for 3 h by using a muffle furnace (air environ-
ment). The calcined powder after after annealing experiment was fur-
ther stored in a dry place before undergoing any characterization of
TGA analysis.

2.3. Characterization of PSM perovskites
The phase composition of the PSM perovskites derived vis SCS
method was analyzed using a powder X-ray diffractometer (PXRD,
PANalytical XPert MPD/DY636). The elemental composition and the
surface material morphology of each PSM perovskite was further
scrutinized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Nova Nano
450, FEI, 200kx) equipped with the energy dispersive X-ray spectro-
scopy (EDS).
2.4. TGA experiments
A high temperature set-up consisting of a SETSYS Evolution TGA
(Setaram Instruments, France) was utilized for the evaluation of the TR
and CS ability of the PSM perovskites (Fig. 3). The major components of
the TGA set-up are: a graphite furnace, a gas control panel equipped
with multiple mass flow controllers, a vertical hanging down balance,
gas cylinders, double stage vacuum pump, quenching water chiller, and
Calisto software for data recording/processing. The reference and
sample alumina crucibles were stationed on a platinum pan. The sample
placed in the crucible was heated by using the graphite-heating furnace
consisting of an alumina tube and multiple thermocouples. The flow-
rates of an inert Ar and feed CO2/Ar gas mixture was monitored and
regulated by using two mass flow controllers. The reaction temperature
was controlled by using a Pt–Rh type-B thermocouple located at the
bottom of the crucible holding platinum pan. The influence of thermal
buoyancy on the findings associated with the TR and CS steps was
nullified by subtracting the TGA profiles obtained during a blank run
(conducted by using empty crucible) from the actual TGA experiment
(conducted by using PSM perovskites) [22,26]. Multiple CS cycles were
conducted by using approximately 70 mg of the PSM perovskite powder
situated precisely on the TGA balance with the help of the alumina
crucible. The Calisto software was utilized to monitor and record the
variation in the mass of the PSM perovskite during each TR (performed
at 1400 °C for 60 min, 100 ml/min Ar) and CS (conducted at 1000 °C for
30 min, 100 ml/min CO2/Ar mixture) step. By using Eqs. (7) and (8),
the variation in the mass recorded during each TR and CS step was
converted into nO2 and nCO by each PSM perovskite.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of SCS synthesized PSM perovskites
PXRD analysis was conducted to identify the crystallite phase
composition of each SCS derived PSM perovskite (2θ = 20–60°). The
PXRD profiles reported in Fig. 4 confirms the production of composi-
tionally phase pure PSM perovskites with absence of any metal i.e., Pr,
Sr, Mn, or metal oxide i.e., Pr-, Sr-, Mn-based oxide impurities. The
PXRD profiles shown in the Fig. 4 (2θ = 32–34°) further indicate a shift
in the peaks associated with the PSM perovskite towards the lower 2θ
angle, with the rise in the Pr and drop in the Sr atomic concentrations.
For instance, the 2θ angle of the peak associated with the PSM9 was
0.3° lower as compared to that of the PSM1 perovskite. The reason for
this shift of the 2θ angle towards the lower value with the rise in the Pr
and drop in the Sr atomic concentrations is the lesser crystal ionic radii
of Pr (113 pm) as compared to that of the Sr (132 pm). This shift in the
2θ angle also provided the confirmation that the Pr is successfully in-
corporated (partially in place of Sr) in the PSM perovskites. In addition
to the phase composition, the average crystallite size of each PSM
perovskite was also determined using the findings obtained in the PXRD
analysis and by applying the Scherrer formula [54]. In terms of num-
bers, the average crystallite size of each PSM perovskite was observed
to be less than 100 nm and there was no fixed trend associated with the

3.2. Thermochemical CO2 splitting ability of SCS synthesized PSM
perovskites
Previous studies associated with the solar thermochemical WS/CS
using perovskite based oxides were focused only on one or two ther-
mochemical cycles. Therefore, to be on the same page and to compare
with the formerly studied perovskite based oxides, we have initiated
this study by testing the PSM perovskites in one thermochemical CS
cycle. The TR and CS steps were carried out at 1400 °C (for 60 min) and
at 1000 °C (for 30 min), respectively and the TGA profiles obtained for
PSM2, PSM4, PSM6, and PSM8 (as an example) during the first CS cycle
are presented in Fig. 6.
The TGA profiles obtained during the first TR step were utilized to
identify the amount of mass reduced by all the PSM perovskites. This
drift in the mass was directly transformed into the nO2 by each PSM
perovskite during first TR step and the obtained numbers are reported
in Fig. 7. Among all the PSM perovskites, the PSM1 was observed to be
capable of releasing the highest nO2 = 1062.8 μmol/g. It was further
noticed that the nO2 by the PSM perovskites decreased as the atomic
concentration of Pr was increased (Table 3). The only exception for this
observation was the PSM5 perovskite as it released higher nO2 as

4. Summary and conclusions
Solution combustion synthesis approach was successfully utilized
for the development of PSM perovskites. The annealing performed at
1000 °C in air for multiple hours also assisted the production of all the
PSM perovskites. The composition purity of each SCS synthesized PSM
perovskite was determined by performing PXRD analysis. EDS analysis
was also applied to verify the findings obtained via PXRD analysis. The
material morphology of each PSM perovskite was scrutinized by using
SEM and the images obtained for each PSM perovskite were noticed to
be very similar to each other (average particle size less than 100 nm).
By utilizing a high temperature TGA set-up, the redox reactivity of SCS
derived PSM perovskites was estimated. Multiple thermochemical TR
and CS steps were conducted in the temperature range of 1000 to
1400 °C. The findings associated with the cycle 1 indicated tremendous
amount of O2 release by each PSM perovskite as compared to the re-
maining cycles (cycle 2 to cycle 10). Most of the PSM perovskites at-
tained the constant level of O2 release and CO production as the number
of thermochemical cycles were increased from cycle 1 to cycle 10. In
terms of numbers, PSM2 and PSM3 perovskites were noticed to the best
choice for the thermochemical CS cycles as these perovskites produced
the highest level of O2 and CO in multiple cycles. However, in terms of
re-oxidation ability (based on the average n
n
/
CO
O2 ratio), the PSM5
perovskite was observed to the top material. Overall results of this in-
vestigation indicate that the lower atomic concentration or Pr (from 0.2
to 0.4) and higher atomic concentration of Sr (from 0.6 to 0.8) were
more suitable to attain the maximum redox reactivity in case of the
PSM perovskites towards thermochemical CS reaction. Our research
team is currently focused towards exploring the reaction kinetics, long-
term redox reactivity and thermal stability of the PSM perovskites.
