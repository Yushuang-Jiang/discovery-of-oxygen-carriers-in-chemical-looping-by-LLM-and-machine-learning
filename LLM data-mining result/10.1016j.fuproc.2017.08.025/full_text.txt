A B S T R A C T
In this study, naturally occurring ilmenite and manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite were evaluated as oxygen carriers
with respect to their O2 uncoupling behaviour. The eﬀects of temperature and pre-treatment on the O2 un-
coupling properties of manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite were also investigated. The experimental results indicated
that both calcined ilmenite and manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite were capable of releasing gas-phase O2 at certain
temperatures with the amount of O2 released by the manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite being much more than that of
the calcined ilmenite. When the manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite was pre-treated by subjecting it to several cycles
of chemical looping combustion with CH4 as reductant, the Mn2O3 might react with iron species that had mi-
grated outwardly, resulting in the formation of a ferrimanganic oxide which exhibits favourable thermo-
dynamics for the oxidation reaction for the reduced oxygen carrier. As a result, the reduced pre-treated oxygen
carrier could be easily re-oxidized and more O2 could be released by the pre-treated oxygen carrier during every
uncoupling steps than that by the untreated one. More gas-phase O2 was released by the manganese-modiﬁed
ilmenite when more redox cycles were performed during pre-treatment. The temperature of isothermal un-
coupling-oxidation cycling showed a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the O2 uncoupling behaviour for manganese-
modiﬁed ilmenite. When temperatures were either higher or lower than 900 °C, less O2 was released from
manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite. 30%-Mn2O3/ilmenite released more O2 than 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite when the
temperature was below 950 °C while the same amount of O2 was released when the temperature was 1000 °C.
1. Introduction
Chemical looping combustion (CLC) is an emerging combustion
technology which has been gaining considerable attention over the last
two decades due to its high-eﬃciency in converting fuels to heat with
inherent CO2 separation [1,2]. CLC processes can be realized in dual-
reactor systems consisting of an air reactor (AR) and a fuel reactor (FR).
A metal oxide acting as an oxygen carrier (OC) is employed to transfer
oxygen from the AR to the FR, thereby avoiding direct contact between
fuel and air. As a result, CO2 and H2O are kept separated from the other
ﬂue gas components, and the costly and energy consuming separation
of CO2 is eliminated. For industrial applications, CLC processes depend
on the availability of OCs with suitable chemical and physical proper-
ties [3]. The selection of OCs with high performance is a critical part of
developing CLC technology. A suitable OC for CLC processes should be
highly stable under repeated redox cycles at high temperatures, re-
sistant to attrition and agglomeration, low-cost, environmentally
friendly, and highly reactive with the selected fuel and oxidant [3,4].
During CLC processes, the full conversion of the fuels can be limited
due to inadequate gas-solid contacting or due to thermodynamic limits
related to reaction between fuels and the lattice oxygen in OCs [5,6]. As
a result, the ﬂue gas produced from the FR will contain unconverted
gases such as H2, CO, and CH4. The concentration of unconverted
combustible gases in the ﬂue gas must be limited in order to meet CO2
pipeline speciﬁcations and to avoid wasting the fuel. There are several
approaches that can be taken to achieve complete combustion. These
include 1) the separation of unconverted gases including H2, CO, and
CH4 from the CO2 followed by the recirculation of these gases to the
fuel reactor, and 2) the introduction of pure O2 downstream of the FR to
oxidize the remaining unconverted gases (oxygen polishing) [7]. Un-
fortunately, both of these measures are high-cost and energy-intensive
due to the necessary gas separation unit operation. Another strategy to
improve fuel conversion in the FR is the generation of gas-phase O2 in
the FR during the combustion of the fuels [7]. An alternative chemical
looping concept, chemical looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU),
provides a strategy to supply gaseous O2 to the FR [8–10]. The CLOU
approach can be regarded as an improvement of conventional CLC
processes. The signiﬁcant diﬀerence between CLOU and conventional

CLC processes is the mechanisms for oxidation of fuels in the FR. CLOU
utilizes an OC that releases gas-phase O2 under reducing conditions,
and the fuels in the FR are oxidized by released O2 via gas-gas (for
gaseous fuels) or gas-solid (for solid fuels) reactions. In the AR, the OC
is reoxidized by air, which is exactly the same as in conventional CLC
processes. It has been observed that the rate of combustion by using
OCs with O2 uncoupling with CH4 is signiﬁcantly higher than in con-
ventional CLC processes [3]. Thus, for a given fuel ﬁring rate, the CLC
processes using O2-uncoupling materials require smaller solids in-
ventory, which in turn leads to smaller fuel reactors [11].
A series of studies has shown that oxidation and combustion of
gaseous fuels, e.g. CH4, can proceed more completely when Fe-, Mn- or
perovskite-based OCs exhibit an O2 uncoupling property. Kallen et al.
[12] prepared perovskite material CaMn0.9Mg0.1O3 −δ with O2 un-
coupling properties and examined its performance as an OC for CLC
with CH4. They found that complete combustion of CH4 was achieved
with an oxygen concentration in the outlet stream from the fuel reactor
of > 1 vol%, which suggests that a substantial part of the fuel is con-
verted by gaseous O2 released from the particles promoting the fuel
conversion [12]. Given such high oxygen concentrations it should be
noted that prior to CO2 transport, any remaining O2 present in the CO2
produced by CLC systems with O2 uncoupling will need to be removed
to a concentration less than about 10 vppm in order to meet pipeline
speciﬁcations that are in place to avoid pipeline corrosion [13]. This
can be done through a number of methods including cryogenic se-
paration and catalytic combustion. Shulman et al. developed a series of
Mn/Fe composite OCs with O2 release characteristics selected for CLC
of CH4. They observed low CO formation during combustion which they
attributed to O2 uncoupling from the OCs [14]. Shulman et al. [15] also
investigated O2 uncoupling properties and reaction rates for CH4
combustion in a ﬂuidized bed reactor with various OC particles, pro-
duced from mixtures of Mn- and Mg-based oxides. They concluded that
OC particles with enhanced O2 release showed superior methane
combustion properties achieving nearly complete conversion of the
fuel. They believed O2 uncoupling in commercial operations could fa-
cilitate very high or complete conversion of methane-rich fuels [15].
Although the CLOU approach largely relies on existing CLC tech-
nology conﬁgurations, the strategy presents the need for material de-
velopment. A suitable OC for CLOU should be thermodynamically
capable of releasing gas-phase O2 at an appropriate temperature under
relevant O2 partial pressures in the FR (lower than 1.0 × 10−5 atm
when considering the O2 limit in CO2 pipelines would be required to
avoid oxygen removal in the CO2 puriﬁcation unit), and should exhibit
a high enough oxygen releasing capacity to eﬀectively oxidize the fuel
gas. At the same time, the oxygen carrier's reduced form must be oxi-
dized easily in the AR. Similar to conventional CLC processes, OCs for
CLOU have to be mechanically stable, low-cost and environmentally
acceptable. Thermodynamic analysis concludes that the most com-
monly proposed OCs formed with monometallic oxides based on Fe2O3
and NiO are incapable of releasing gas-phase O2 at typical ﬂuidized bed
combustion (FBC) conditions [11]. OCs based on metal oxides such as
CuO, Mn2O3, and Co3O4 with appropriate equilibrium partial pressures
of O2 in the temperature range of 800 to 1200 °C are promising [11].
However, Co3O4 has not been widely considered as an OC due to its
high cost and high toxicity [11,16]. CuO and Cu2O are the most thor-
oughly examined oxide pair for CLOU and appears to be promising
technically, but the fairly high cost of CuO and high possibility of sin-
tering prompt us to look for alternates [17,18]. Mn2O3 is an abundant
and inexpensive metallic oxide with a relatively low decomposition
temperature favouring the release of gas-phase O2. However, assuming
that the reduced oxygen carrier is regenerated with air, the decom-
position temperature for Mn2O3 at 876 °C imposes a relatively low
thermodynamic maximum temperature for the AR [11,19]. In other
words, Mn2O3 will decompose into Mn3O4 when O2 partial pressure is
lower than or equal to 0.21 atm at atmospheric pressure. Therefore, at
atmospheric pressure the maximum operation temperature of the AR
would need to be lower than 876 °C in practice to achieve rapid oxi-
dation of Mn3O4 to Mn2O3. As a result, directly using pure Mn2O3 or
manganese ores as OCs for CLOU is unfavourable and impractical since
excessively low AR operating temperatures result in low overall system
eﬃciency.
Recent studies indicate that the temperature at which Mn3O4 oxi-
dation proceeds can be increased by combining Mn species with other
materials such as iron, nickel, silicon, magnesium and calcium resulting
in mixed oxides with improved thermodynamic properties [19–22]. The
most frequently investigated combinations are Fe2O3/Mn2O3 mixed
oxides which exhibit superior O2 uncoupling characteristics to Mn2O3
soley during reduction. The formation of bixbyite (MnxFe1 −x)O3
crystal phase enables the Mn species to be oxidized in the AR at high
temperature and low oxygen partial pressure [23–25]. OCs containing
Fe2O3/Mn2O3 mixed oxides employed in the majority of current studies
are fully synthetic materials which show superior O2 release capacity
and improved oxidation behaviour. However, the mechanical integrity
and attrition resistance of these synthetic particles are often un-
satisfactory in continuous operation, which need a carefully design on
the preparation method to improve their performance [26–29]. From
an economic point of view, the use of these synthetic particles in
practice, at current stage, is likely to be cost prohibitive even if the
attrition resistance becomes comparable to the naturally occurring
materials. This is due to the high cost of their precursors and their
complicated production processes; as a result, an extreme high opera-
tional cost will be imposed even a low attrition rate is achieved. On the
contrary, the lower cost of the naturally occurring materials can oﬀset
this operational cost when a reasonable attrition rate is achieved.
Therefore, we believe that looking for OCs with suitable O2 uncoupling
behaviour based on low cost materials such as naturally occurring
metal ores or minerals is still well worth considering.
Iron ores (e.g. hematite, mainly composed of Fe2O3) and titaniu-
m‑iron ores (e.g. ilmenite ore, mainly composed of FeTiO3) are two
series of natural materials which have been extensively examined to act
as low-cost OCs in various chemical looping processes. However, both
pure oxides Fe2O3 and Fe2TiO5 (FeTiO3 in oxidized form) should not be
capable of releasing gas-phase O2 during CLC-relevant temperatures
below 1050 °C according to one thermodynamic study [30]. To enable
O2 uncoupling to proceed with iron ore, Haider et al. immobilized
Mn2O3 on a Canadian iron ore resulting in a composite Fe2O3/Mn2O3
oxygen carrier [31]. They found that the impregnated iron ore ex-
hibited signiﬁcant O2 uncoupling in an inert atmosphere and showed
favourable oxidation kinetics in air. They concluded that the im-
pregnated iron ore OC should facilitate eﬃcient use of fuels for CLOU
processes.
Ilmenite ore (titanium‑iron) has been extensively investigated as an
OC for CLC. Ilmenite is considered a highly promising OC for chemical
looping applications due to its desirable thermodynamic properties,
demonstrated attrition resistance, low-cost and its potential 100%
combustion eﬃciency [32–37]. A recent study reported by Rydén et al.
has shown that ilmenite is capable of releasing small amounts of gas-
phase O2 during chemical-looping combustion experiments at a tem-
perature of ~950 °C [38]. They postulated that the reason for the O2
release was due to the presence of small amounts of manganese in the
sample or interactions between impurities and iron or titanium oxides
[38]. Unfortunately, there is little else reported in the literature on the
O2 release behaviour of titanium‑iron ore. The present research aims to
understand the mechanism of O2 uncoupling from ilmenite and to
promote O2 release.
In this paper, we report on an experimental investigation in which
an ilmenite ore was modiﬁed by manganese species via an impregnation
method. Evaluation of this manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite at relevant
CLC conditions was performed in a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA).
In this study we sought to evaluate this material's O2 uncoupling
properties by measuring gas-phase O2 release in an inert environment
and by determining its feasibility during oxidation in air at diﬀerent

2. Experiment
2.1. Chemicals and materials
Ilmenite ore used in this work is pre-calcined ilmenite concentrate
supplied by Rio Tinto Iron & Titanium, Québec, Canada. Table 1 shows
the chemical composition of this ilmenite (in fully oxidized form)
measured by X-ray ﬂuorescence (XRF) analysis following the ASTM
D4326 method. The ilmenite sample was crushed and sieved to the
particle size of interest (in the range of 212–300 μm), and then calcined
once more in a muﬄe furnace at 900 °C for 2 h in air for the following
use.
Chemicals including manganese (II) nitrate tetrahydrate (Mn
(NO3)2·4H2O), anhydrous methanol and concentrated ammonia solu-
tion (28 wt%) were purchased from Aldrich and used as received.
2.2. Oxygen carrier preparation
Manganese-modiﬁed ilmenites were prepared by impregnating the
double calcined ilmenite particles (shown in Table 1) with Mn2O3 by
using Mn(NO3)2 as the precursor [39]. The precursor was dissolved in
100 mL of methanol to give a 2 M solution. The calcined ilmenite, as
described in Section 2.1, was immersed in this precursor solution. The
mixture of precursor and calcined ilmenite was further dispersed by the
addition of 10 mL of methanol and stirred in an uncovered beaker
overnight at ambient temperature. After a wet paste had been obtained,
3 mL of 1 M ammonia solution was dropwise added to the paste while
stirring to facilitate the hydrolysis of the metal precursor and achieve a
uniform coating. Finally, the obtained composite was dried in air at
60 °C and then calcined at 900 °C for 2 h in air with a temperature ramp
rate of 10 °C/min. The mass ratio of the Mn2O3 to ilmenite was con-
trolled during preparation by applying the stoichiometric amount of
precursors to the ilmenite particles. Oxygen carriers containing Mn2O3:
ilmenite mass ratios of 15 wt% and 30 wt% were produced. The ﬁnal
products are denoted here as N %-Mn2O3/ilmenite, where N indicates
the loading mass ratio of the Mn2O3 with respect to the weight of cal-
cined ilmenite. Although Mn(NO3)2 with a lower Mn(II) valence was
employed as a precursor, Maneva et al. have shown that the dis-
proportionated decomposition of such a metal source ﬁrst results in the
generation of MnO2 with Mn (IV) valence (taking place at around
300 °C), which subsequently decomposes to Mn2O3 above 500 °C [40].
2.3. Reaction characterization
The temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) technique was
employed using a ﬁxed-bed reactor, which allowed the determination
of the O2 release temperature from the O2 evolution proﬁle. In a typical
test, approximately 1.5 g of sample was placed in a quartz glass reactor
coupled with a Pfeiﬀer Omnistar 320C mass spectrometer (MS)
(Detection threshold < 1 ppm). During continuous ﬂow TPD tests, an
inlet ﬂow of He (Linde, Grade 4.7, 99.997% N2, O2 ≤5 ppm) was
maintained at a total ﬂow rate of 60 cm3/min over the oxygen carrier at
atmospheric pressure while heating from 25 to 200 °C over 5 min. The
sample was subsequently degassed at 200 °C for 30 min under He to
remove surface contaminates. Then the reactor tube was heated from
200 to 1050 °C at a heating rate of 50 °C/min. After reaching 1050 °C,
the reactor temperature was maintained at 1050 °C for 10 min before
cooling. The reactor eﬄuent passed through a water trap and was then
monitored for molecular ion responses corresponding to O2 (m/z = 32).
TGA tests were conducted in a Thermo Cahn Instruments TherMax
700 thermogravimetric analyzer. The samples were placed in a 5-mm
deep and 10 mm diameter quartz crucible. Approximately 60 mg of OC
was heated in this quartz crucible from ambient temperature to desired
temperatures in the range of 750–1000 °C with a heating rate set at
30 °C/min under air at a ﬂow rate of 100 cm3/min. The sample tem-
perature was maintained isothermal for 20 min before O2 release and
oxidation cycles were initiated. The gas stream used during the O2 re-
lease steps was N2 (Linde, Grade 5.0, 99.999% N2, O2 ≤2 ppm)
ﬂowing at 50 cm3/min. Air at a total ﬂow rate of 100 cm3/min was
used during oxidation steps.

2.4. Oxygen carrier characterization
Wide-angle powder X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) measurements were
performed at room temperature to analyze and identify the crystal
phase of OCs before and after cycling using a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray
diﬀractometer. For XRD analysis, the sample was ground and put on a
silicon zero-background sample stage. The XRD instrument was set to
operate at 40 kV and 40 mA over a scanning range (2 Theta) between
15° and 100° at a scanning rate of 0.02°/min. The morphology of OCs
before cycling was observed by a Nikon SMZ18 optical microscope
mounted with a 1× objective lens. Digital photograph acquisition was
performed using a Lumenera INFINITY 2-1RC 1/2 inch CCD colour
camera. The surface of particles was further examined using a scanning
electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi S-3400 N). For SEM characteriza-
tion, the samples were coated with a thin layer of carbon (~2 nm).

2.5. Thermodynamic equilibrium predictions
For thermodynamic equilibrium predictions in this study, the
FactSage 6.4 Reaction, Equilib and Phase Diagram modules were uti-
lized with FToxid databases [41]. FactSage software predicts equili-
brium solid–liquid–gas phases and compositions based on Gibbs free

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of calcined ilmenite and manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite
Fig. 1 shows the digital optical photographs and SEM images of the
calcined ilmenite and 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite prior to CLC cycling taken
at diﬀerent magniﬁcations. It can be seen from the optical photograph
that the surface of the calcined ilmenite (Fig. 1a) appears relatively
smooth and exhibits a brown colour with reﬂective metallic luster,
which is in accordance with the previous observations indicating an
iron-rich surface after calcination [33,42]. After the introduction of
Mn2O3 onto the surface of ilmenite particles, the colour of the particles
turns from brown to black with increased metallic luster (Fig. 1b). SEM
images of calcined ilmenite (Fig. 1c and d) show that the surface of the
particle is composed of compactly arranged small grains with a size
below 5 μm, which is consistent with a previous study [33]. After
modiﬁcation by manganese species (Fig. 1e and f) the particle surface
becomes rougher, with large polycrystals of manganese species visible
which are distributed unevenly on the particle surface. The original
ilmenite surface can be seen to be exposed in some regions as indicated
by white arrows in Fig. 1f. The grain size of manganese phases for the
15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite sample was smaller when compared to the sur-
face grains of the calcined ilmenite. The colour variation and the sur-
face morphology change demonstrate introduction of manganese spe-
cies onto ilmenite particles and reveal the morphology of these guest
species.
Wide-angle XRD patterns of calcined ilmenite and 15%-Mn2O3/il-
menite samples are shown in Fig. 2. Patterns of both samples display
multiple resolved diﬀraction peaks (Fig. 2a and b), which can be as-
signed to the reﬂections of pseudobrookite phase (Fe2TiO5) (JCPDS
Card Number 70-2728), the alpha‑iron oxide phase (α-Fe2O3) (JCPDS
Card Number 80-2377) and rutile phase (TiO2) (JCPDS Card Number
84-1284). Since both samples were calcined at 900 °C in air, there are
no resolved peaks corresponding to the crystal phase of ilmenite
(FeTiO3) shown in the XRD patterns. This observation agrees well with
thermodynamic equilibrium predictions from our previous study [33].
Besides peaks related to iron oxide and pseudobrookite, characteristic
diﬀraction peaks indexed to cubic bixbyite Mn2O3 (JCPDS Card
Number 89-4836) (Fig. 2b) are clearly observed indicating the de-
position of Mn2O3 onto the ilmenite particles.

3.2. O2 release temperature by TPD
Fig. 3 presents the TPD proﬁles of the calcined ilmenite (red line)
and 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite (black line). It can be seen that both samples
exhibit O2 uncoupling behaviour when the temperature is elevated. The
15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite sample started to release gas-phase O2 when the
temperature reached ~690 °C, while the initial temperature of O2 un-
coupling for the calcined ilmenite sample was ~850 °C. When com-
paring the amount of O2 released qualitatively, it can be seen that the
15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite sample released much more gas-phase O2 than
the calcined ilmenite sample.

Fig. 4 presents an XRD pattern of the calcined ilmenite sample after
TPD at a maximum temperature of 1050 °C. Since ilmenite ore is
composed of mixed oxides doped with other impurities, it is quite dif-
ﬁcult to identify in detail the XRD peaks of several compounds that are
close and overlapped. However, when comparing the XRD patterns of
ilmenite before (Fig. 2a) and after (Fig. 4) TPD, it can be clearly seen
that an XRD peak at ~60.2° has split into two peaks that are centered at
~59.9° and 60.2°, respectively. The peak at ~59.9° coupled with the
one at ~58.6° looks very similar to the fourth strongest peak of
iron‑titanium oxide Fe3Ti3O10 (JCPDS Card Number 43-1011) whose
ﬁrst three strongest XRD peaks are overlapped with Fe2TiO5. If this is
the case, the O2 uncoupling of calcined ilmenite might happen due to
the phase transfer between Fe3Ti3O10 and Fe2TiO5. It should be noted
that the ilmenite employed in this study contained 1.72% MgO
(Table 1), and so the peak centered at ~59.9° might also be due to the
formation of armalcolite ((Mg0.5Fe0.5)Ti2O5) (JCPDS Card Number 74-
1507) since both armalcolite and Fe3Ti3O10 display a similar peak
centered at ~59.9° in their XRD pattern. If this is the case, the phase
transformation between Fe2TiO5 and armalcolite still results in O2 re-
lease because the latter is also composed of iron species (Fe2+) with
lower valence. Further investigations should be carried out to establish
which oxygen uncoupling mechanism is occurring.

The Mn2O3 present in the samples is a metallic oxide with bixbyite

and under low O2 partial pressure resulting in Mn3O4 formation with a

to decompose into Mn3O4 promptly and completely, the TGA curve
doesn't reach a steady value within 20 min. The re-oxidation tempera-
ture of 850 °C is close to the thermodynamic transition temperature
between Mn2O3 and Mn3O4 in air. At this temperature in air, the
driving force for oxidation to a higher manganese valence (from Mn2+/
3+ to Mn3+) is too small for reaction to proceed quickly [44]. As a
result, the Mn species are only partially re-oxidized to Mn3+ species
which are involved in the subsequent redox cycles after the ﬁrst oxi-

weight loss at the second and third cycles.

3.5. O2 uncoupling behaviour after a pretreatment
Since the generation of ferrimanganic oxide can alter the thermo-
dynamic properties of manganese oxides, resulting in an increase of the
temperature threshold for the oxidation of Mn species with low valence
state, the reversibility of O2 release from 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite samples
should be improved if more ferrimanganic oxide is generated. Many
studies have shown that when ilmenite is used after CLC cycles, the iron
species start to migrate outwards resulting in a titanium rich core
composed of a TiO2 and FeTiO3 phase and an outer shell composed of
pure Fe2O3 which is identical to the hematite crystal [47,48]. We as-
sume that more ferrimanganic oxide can be generated in manganese-
modiﬁed ilmenite if more iron species migrate outwards and accumu-
late at the boundary between the ilmenite and Mn2O3 layer. If this is the
case, reversibility of O2 release for manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite can be
maintained during repeated uncoupling-oxidation cycles.
In the following study, 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite OC is subjected to
several cycles of CLC with methane at 800 °C as a pre-treatment in
order to increase the accumulation of iron species at the boundary and
promote the generation of ferrimanganic oxide (the pre-treatment is
denoted as Z-Pre-CLC, where Z indicates the number of CLC cycles). The
OC was tested afterward for its O2 release characteristics to reveal the
impact of iron migration on the O2 uncoupling behaviour of manga-
nese-modiﬁed ilmenite.
Fig. 8 shows the cyclic performance of 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite sam-
ples with and without the pre-treatment measured at 850 °C over three
cycles of isothermal uncoupling-oxidation. It can be seen that the 15%-
Mn2O3/ilmenite sample after 15 cycles of pre-treatment exhibited an
enhanced O2 uncoupling behaviour compared to the sample without
the pre-treatment. During the uncoupling process during the ﬁrst cycle,
the 15-Pre-CLC sample shows a similar weight loss when compared to
the OC without the pre-treatment. However, the total weight of the 15-
Pre-CLC sample returned to its initial mass during the ﬁrst oxidation
step, which is signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from the OC without the pre-
treatment. The reversible uncoupling-oxidation behaviour of the 15-
Pre-CLC sample was maintained during the subsequent two cycles. It is
in accordance with our initial assumption that the reversibility might be
attributed to outwards migration of the iron species during the pre-
treatment, which react with the Mn2O3 present on the surface of the
ilmenite particles, resulting in the generation of more ferrimanganic
oxide.
To further investigate the inﬂuence of pre-treatment on the cyclic
performance of manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite, diﬀerent cycles of Pre-
CLC were applied to 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite samples, and their O2 un-
coupling behaviours were evaluated at various temperatures.

If assuming that both Mn2O3 and ilmenite can achieve full reduction
at 900 °C during the combustion of CH4, it means that Mn3+ (Mn2O3) is
converted to Mn2+ (MnO) resulting in 10.0% of oxygen from Mn2O3
being consumed by CH4, and that Fe3+ is converted to Fe2+ resulting
in 6.09% of oxygen from calcined ilmenite (the percentage of Fe2O3 in
calcined ilmenite is 60.9% measured by XRF) being consumed by CH4.
Thus the theoretical OTC of the 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite sample (mMn2O3/
milmenite = 0.15) should be around 6.60%. It can be found from Fig. 9
that 0.30% of oxygen can be released from 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite at
900 °C indicating that 6.30% of oxygen is in the lattice of 15%-Mn2O3/
ilmenite and 0.30% of oxygen can be released as gaseous O2. Here, we
assume that the O2 release and the solid-gas reaction between fuel and
lattice oxygen happen in sequence in a well-designed FR. From a re-
action stoichiometry perspective if the conversion of CH4 in the FR
reaches 95.45% at 900 °C through reaction with an OC with an eﬀective
OTC of 6.30% but without O2 uncoupling properties, then nearly full
combustion can be achieved by using 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite as OC since
0.30% of gaseous O2 released from the material can burn at least the
4.5% unconverted CH4 through the kinetically-favoured gas-gas reac-
tion.
In a number of previous studies, it has been observed that ilmenite
as an OC exhibits an activation process during CLC of gaseous fuels,
dominated by changes in morphologies with respect to the evolution of
pore structures, crystal growth and the migration of iron ions [49,50].
Here, the higher OTC for the calcined ilmenite after 15 cycles of pre-
treatment might be due to the morphology change imposed by the CLC
process that may have decreased the energy barrier to O2 release and
crystal phase transformation. The contribution to the increase in OTC
imposed by 15 cycles of pre-treatment for calcined ilmenite was lim-
ited; however, the OTC change for the manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite
sample imposed by pre-treatment was signiﬁcant. The 15%-Mn2O3/il-
menite sample treated by several cycles of Pre-CLC exhibited higher
OTC than the untreated one independent of temperature. Furthermore,
15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite showed an increased OTC when increasing the
number of pre-treatment cycles at all the tested temperatures, which
indicates that more O2 can be released by manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite
when this OC is treated by more cycles of pre-treatment. When in-
creasing the cycles of pre-treatment, more iron species migrate out-
wards from the interior of the particles to the surface. As a result, more
ferrimanganic oxide, which favours reversible O2 uncoupling, is gen-
erated. Samples of 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite after pre-treatment show an
OTC decrease when the temperature is elevated above 950 °C. As dis-
cussed in Section 3.4, 950 °C is too close to the thermodynamic tran-
sition temperature between (Fe1 −xMnx)2O3 and (Fe1 −xMnx)3O4 in
air,
and
the
driving
force
for
oxidation
of
the
component
(Fe1 −xMnx)3O4 in reduced samples is too small at elevated tempera-
tures. Therefore, the full oxidation of (Fe1 −xMnx)3O4 becomes diﬃcult
at a temperature above 900 °C and the reduced sample cannot be re-
oxidized to its initial stage at high temperature. In consequence, the
amount of O2 that is released in the subsequent uncoupling process
decreases.
Given a desired OTC value of OCs in a CLC system, improving the
reduction reactivity of the OC would provide the opportunity to lower
the operational temperature of the FR, as a beneﬁt, the AR could con-
sequently be operated at a lower temperature. Operation at lower but
reasonable temperatures would decrease the capital cost of both the AR
and FR since lowering the operational temperature would allow the use
of lower-cost materials for reactor construction. On the other hand,
achieving suﬃcient circulation rates of solids could be a challenge in a
practical CLC system. If the reduction reactivity of OCs could be in-
creased while maintaining the OTC, it would be possible to reduce the
circulation rate of OCs. In our previous study, it was found that the
addition of Mn2O3 improves the reduction reactivity of ilmenite to-
wards CH4 during multiple cycles [39]. When the O2 uncoupling per-
formance of the OCs is improved, the reduction reactivity of the OCs
can be increased further since the released O2 could proceed via
homogeneous reaction with gaseous fuels, which is much faster than
the heterogeneous reduction according to previous studies [11,15].
After 15 cycles of pre-treatment the 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite OC demon-
strated improved O2 uncoupling performance when the temperature
was near optimal at the 900 °C investigated here. We believe that this
improved O2 uncoupling property along with the improved reduction
reactivity due to the combination of Mn2O3 and ilmenite could provide
the opportunity of lowering the operational temperature of the FR
while resulting in improved gaseous fuel conversion and reduced FR
size. While the FR design should be well considered to suit the fuel
combustion since the homogeneous reaction between fuel and released
O2 mostly happens in the upper part of the reactor.
The inﬂuence of the Mn2O3 loading on the cyclic performance of
manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite was investigated with results as shown in
Fig. 10. Both samples were pre-treated by subjecting them to 15 cycles
of Pre-CLC before the uncoupling-oxidation test.
The 30%-Mn2O3/ilmenite sample, with higher loading of Mn2O3,
showed a slightly higher OTC from 750 to 950 °C compared to the 15%-
Mn2O3/ilmenite sample. However, the 30%-Mn2O3/ilmenite sample
exhibited the same amount of O2 release as that of 15%-Mn2O3/ilme-
nite at 1000 °C, which is due to the thermodynamic limit for the oxi-
dation of manganese ion as discussed previously (Section 3.4). At
1000 °C, the compound Mn2O3 doesn't contribute to the OTC during the
uncoupling step since Mn2O3 cannot exist stably at such high tem-
perature and it would have already decomposed into Mn3O4 during the
oxidation step before uncoupling. Only the transformation between
(Fe1 −xMnx)2O3 and (Fe1 −xMnx)3O4 contributes to the overall OTC.
However, the thermodynamic driving force for oxidation of the com-
ponent (Fe1 −xMnx)3O4 in manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite samples de-
creases when the temperature increases. Therefore, the full oxidation of
(Fe1 −xMnx)3O4 might be kinetically limited at high temperature
within 20 min of the uncoupling step. As a consequence, the OTCs of
both 15%-Mn2O3/ilmenite and 30%-Mn2O3/ilmenite start to decrease
when the temperature is above 900 °C. Since the ferrimanganic oxide
can only be formed possibly at the interface between Mn2O3 shell and
the ilmenite core. Thus, the amounts of ferrimanganic oxide generated
at this interface for samples 15%- and 30%-Mn2O3/ilmenite are similar
no matter how much Mn2O3 are introduced in the shell. As a result,
15%- and 30%-Mn2O3/ilmenite show similar OTCs at a very high
temperature (1000 °C).

4. Conclusions
In this study, composite ilmenite-based OCs modiﬁed with 15–30 wt
% Mn2O3 were prepared by using Mn(NO3)2 as an additive precursor.
The O2 uncoupling behaviour of manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite samples
were, for the ﬁrst time, investigated in a ﬁxed-bed reactor through the
use of the TPD technique, and the cyclic performance of the samples
during multiple uncoupling-oxidation cycles was investigated using a
TGA instrument.
The O2 uncoupling behaviour of calcined ilmenite was promoted
successfully by the introduction of Mn2O3. The results indicated that
both calcined ilmenite and manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite were capable
of releasing gas-phase O2 at high temperature, and the manganese
modiﬁcation increased the amount of O2 released. Ilmenite ore after a
high-temperature calcination exhibited O2 uncoupling behaviour,
which is in accordance with a previous study carried out by Ryden et al.
[38]. However, the intrinsic reason for this O2 release by ilmenite ore
should be further examined. Calcined ilmenite started to release gas-
phase O2 under an inert condition when the temperature reached
~850 °C; while the initial O2 uncoupling temperature for the manga-
nese-modiﬁed ilmenite sample was ~690 °C. The lower O2 uncoupling
temperature was attributed to the presence of Mn2O3. Several cycles of
chemical looping combustion with CH4 were carried out as a pre-
treatment for both the calcined ilmenite and manganese-modiﬁed il-
menite samples. After pre-treatment, the amount of O2 released by both
calcined ilmenite and manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite increased. A large
amount of O2 could be released during each uncoupling-oxidation cycle
by the pre-treated Mn2O3/ilmenite. The improvement in the reversi-
bility of oxygen release of manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite was attributed
to the reaction between Mn2O3 and the outward migrated iron species
during pre-treatment, resulting in the formation of ferrimanganic oxide
which exhibits favourable thermodynamics for the oxidation reaction
for the reduced oxygen carrier. More cycles of pre-treatment were
performed resulting in larger amounts of gas-phase O2 release by the
manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite samples. Ilmenite modiﬁed with 30%
Mn2O3 showed a larger amount of O2 release than the 15%-Mn2O3/
ilmenite sample in the temperature range of 750–900 °C. However, a
temperature above 900 °C led to a lesser amount of O2 release by
manganese-modiﬁed ilmenite due to the thermodynamic limit of the
materials during the oxidation step. It is expected that this manganese
modiﬁcation strategy can be further improved to enhance the O2 re-
lease behaviours and increase the reactivity of OCs so as to facilitate the
development of chemical looping technologies.
