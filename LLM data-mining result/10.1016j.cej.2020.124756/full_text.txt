Geopolymer-composites with thermomechanical stability as oxygen carriers
for fluidized bed chemical looping combustion with oxygen uncoupling
A. Natali Murri⁎, F. Miccio, V. Medri, E. Landi
National Research Council–Institute of Science and Technology for Ceramics, CNR-ISTEC, via Granarolo 64, 48018 Faenza, RA, Italy
H I G H L I G H T S
• Oxygen carriers (OCs) based on geopolymer and metal oxides were synthesized for CLOU.
• OCs were tested by TGA at 700/900 °C, in alternating inert and oxidizing atmospheres.
• CuO-based OC showed better CLOU performances (1% wt. carrying capacity).
• Results were confirmed in fluidized bed reactor with high rate index.
• OCs highlighted a good mechanical resistance from attrition and crushing tests.

A B S T R A C T
Oxygen carriers based on geopolymer composites with enhanced thermal and mechanical stability have been
synthesized and been proposed for chemical looping combustion with oxygen uncoupling. Geopolymer synthesis
belongs to green chemistry and ensures chemical and mechanical properties similar to those of high temperature
sintering. Suitable metal oxides, such as combinations of Cu, Mn and Fe oxides, have been used as active fillers in
geopolymer matrices. Thermogravimetric analysis conducted at 900 °C pointed out the excellent performance of
the CuO-based oxygen carrier, and the overall performance trend of CuO > CuMn-oxides > MnFe-oxides. The
performance of the CuO based material (around 1.0% wt. of transferred O2) was confirmed in tests carried out in
a fluidized bed at 900 °C, where a slightly lower carrying capacity, and a remarkable oxidation rate index
(37.1 ± 1.4%) were obtained. Tests of wood-char combustion confirmed the ability of the CuO-based oxygen
carrier to operate in a real chemical looping combustion process. Moreover, attrition and crushing tests de-
monstrated that the mechanical resistance of geopolymer-based oxygen carriers was comparable to that of
materials currently used for fluidized bed application.
1. Introduction
The need to reduce carbon dioxide emissions according to the Kyoto
protocol and Paris climate conference COP21 [1] calls for the devel-
opment of increasingly efficient methods for CO2 capture and separa-
tion from industrial processes. Several methods have been considered,
depending on the conditions of each specific industrial process. Both
conventional and innovative techniques have been studied, for example
wet absorption, dry adsorption, membrane separation, high tempera-
ture uptake by sorbents, and others [2]. In general, these operations are
very expensive both in terms of energy demand and plant engineering,
making the process penalty for CO2 capture/separation substantially
high, and leading to increase the final product price (e.g. electricity)
[3]. Chemical looping combustion (CLC) is recognized to be an effective
method for the inherent separation of the carbon dioxide in combus-
tion-based processes [4], and it is based on the idea of producing ex-
hausts that are highly concentrated in CO2 to avoid separation costs. In
fact, CLC allows generation of two distinct streams of effluents, the first
being rich in carbon dioxide, the second being air depleted in oxygen
that eliminates the dilution of the CO2 by the nitrogen in the air. Oxy-
combustion accomplishes the same concept, but requires an expensive
air separation unit. In contrast, CLC works by means of oxygen carriers
(OCs) that are able to transfer atomic oxygen from one reactor to a
second one [5]. The most likely configuration is two interconnected
fluidized bed (FB) reactors, where a granular carrier circulates and
transfers oxygen from the air to the fuel, without any direct contact
between the reactors [6]. Moreover, emissions of N-oxides by thermal
and prompt mechanisms are substantially reduced because nitrogen in

Naturally occurring metal oxides that have multiple oxidation states
such as Ni, Fe, Mn, and Cu oxides are possible OCs. Among these metal
oxides, manganese and copper exhibit good transport efficiency, and
demonstrate the ability to release O2 in the fuel reactor at elevated
temperatures, which makes them suitable for chemical looping com-
bustion with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) [8–10]. In particular, OCs
based on Mn and Cu oxides have been investigated because of the
formation of the mixed phase CuMn2O4, which is thought to enhance
oxygen transport [8,11]. The CLOU process exploits spontaneous
splitting of certain metal oxides at high temperatures releasing gaseous
O2, which avoids the gasification step and allows for use of less reactive
solid fuels [4].
For the CLC and CLOU processes, the carrier materials need to ex-
hibit long term stability, retention of the oxygen transport capacity, and
resistance to cracking or agglomeration, which can be accomplished by
coupling the metal oxides with an inert, strong support matrix that
prevents material losses and particle breakup [12]. Besides the funda-
mental requirement of having multiple oxidation states and high oxi-
dation/reduction activity, OCs should also be stable under repeated
temperature cycles in FBs, have a low tendency to agglomerate, and
exhibit adequate mechanical strength to withstand the stress during
high-speed circulation [13]. Metal oxides are usually used combined
with oxide support materials as SiO2, ZrO2 or Al2O3 to increase the
carrier reactivity and improve their mechanical stability. In addition,
the overall oxygen transport capacity of the carrier depends on the
relative ratio between the metal oxide and the inert material, as well as
physical characteristics such as the active surface, pore volume, and
particle size [13]. One of the main requirements for the support ma-
terial is that it does not react with the metal oxides to produce mixed
phases with lower melting temperatures or lower oxygen transport
capabilities, so that the overall performances of the OCs is not affected.
Taking into account all these aspects, geopolymers can be an in-
novative solution to be used as support for metal oxides, being porous
and mechanically and thermally resistant up to 1200 °C depending on
the composition. Geopolymers are inorganic synthetic polymers based
on alkali aluminosilicates that are produced through a simple and
sustainable one-step process [14]. Geopolymers are obtained by re-
acting amorphous aluminosilicate powders with aqueous solutions of
alkali silicates. The geopolymer mixture acts as binder for fillers and
additives thanks to the formation of nanoprecipitates during con-
solidation [14]. Indeed, the production of geopolymer composites is of
considerable importance to increase the application potentialities since
proper fillers impart special functionalities. Metal oxide powders can
easily be added to the mixture to incorporate them into the geopoly-
mers. This route is appealing for production of synthetic OCs from the
energy consumption perspective since geopolymerization consists of
curing at 80 °C and a short conditioning treatment at the maximum
working temperature (i.e., 900 °C in this case), which is appreciably
lower than the sintering temperatures of conventional processes for
preparation of OCs [15]. More interestingly, geopolymer-based OCs
have excellent mechanical properties and thermal stability due to the
strong bonds in the polysialate matrix, which are able to withstand high
temperatures without any loss of mechanical strength [16,17]. This is
particularly attractive for FB application, where fluidized particles ex-
perience significant mechanical stresses, particle breakup, and loss by
attrition, so durability of the OC particles is desirable. Moreover, the
nanoscaled microstructure and the intrinsic pore network in the geo-
polymer matrix enhance oxygen transport in OCs, and the presence of a
highly stable alkali-aluminosilicate matrix prevents detrimental inter-
actions with the metal oxides. The authors have been the first to in-
troduce geopolymers in CLC applications and in previous works they
first investigated the use of these materials as inert supports for OCs in
CLC applications [18,19]. Geopolymer composites have been conceived
and synthesized by the authors in the form of granules with embedded
Fe and Mn oxides, then tested as OCs in conventional CLC systems for
methane and syngas combustion [18,19]. The granules offered a me-
chanical resistance to abrasion higher than that of a FCC catalyst and of
the same order of magnitude as γ-alumina. In details, thermogravi-
metric texts for the reduction in a CH4 atmosphere and oxidation in air
of hematite/geopolymer OCs [18] demonstrated a capacity very close
to the theoretical value 1.3 after repeated cycles for transferring oxygen
to a gaseous fuel. Tests conducted at 900 °C in a laboratory-scale dif-
ferential reactor gave rise to a lower O-carrying capacity (< 1.0%), but
rather high kinetics (i.e. rate index) by comparison with Fe oxide and
data in the literature. Furthermore, novel Mn2O3 and Mn2O3-Fe2O3
geopolymer based OCs were tested in a laboratory CLC fixed bed plant,
fueled with coal char and operated at 800, 850 and 900 °C [19]. They
were able to release oxygen in inert atmosphere, thereby demonstrating
potentialities for CLOU applications.
This article follows and implements these previous studies on geo-
polymer-based OCs [18,19], reporting for the first time on the use of
these materials for the production of OCs to be used in CLOU operation
mode. To this aim, suitable oxides such as Cu, Mn and Fe oxides, have
been used alone or in combination as filler in a geopolymer matrix. To
the author’s knowledge, no other research has been conducted on
geopolymers as support materials for OCs in CLC or CLOU operation
modes. In this research, the CLOU ability of the oxygen carriers was
investigated in sequential steps: 1) definition of thermodynamic fra-
mework for operation; 2) testing in thermogravimetric alternating
oxidation and reduction cycles for selection of best OC formulation; 3)
demonstration in batch FB experiments of the oxygen uncoupling be-
havior also for char combustion; 4) characterization of OC resistance to
attrition and mechanical impact. The study was complemented by SEM
and XRD analyses of collected samples, for highlighting possible mi-
crostructural and phase modifications.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials
Metal oxide powder mixtures were prepared by mixing CuO and
Mn2O3 or Fe2O3 and Mn2O3 powders to produce Cu:Mn and Mn:Fe
molar ratios equal to 1:2 based on the ratio of active phases suggested
in other studies [19–21]. The specific surface areas (by BET method)
were 8.8 m2/g for CuO, 2.4 m2/g for Mn2O3 and 11.4 m2/g for Fe2O3.
Geopolymer composites were synthetized according to a previously-
developed formulation [20] by mixing slurries produced by mechanical

2.2. Methods
Thermochemical software (HSC®) was used to evaluate the ther-
modynamic equilibrium composition of the system formed by oxides
(Cu, Mn and Fe) at different temperatures. Thermogravimetric analysis
(TGA) was carried out in a STA 449 Jupiter (Netzsch Geratebau, Selb,
Germany) to measure weight changes in alternating inert and oxidizing
atmospheres and, hence, determine oxygen loss and enrichment. Air
and argon were used as working gases with flow rates of 100 mL/min
and of 180 mL/min plus 20 mL/min of Ar as protective stream for in-
strument safe operation. The sample weight was in the range 70–80 mg.
The tests were conducted at the isothermal temperature of 900 °C for
both the oxidizing and inert steps, lasting 30 and 40 min respectively,
or adopting alternating temperature of 700 °C and 900 °C respectively
for the oxidizing and inert steps, lasting 30 min each. Heating and
cooling rates of 30 °C/min were used for the dynamic steps, which were
conducted in air up to 700 °C and from 900 to 700 °C. An inert atmo-
sphere (Ar) was used up to the reaction temperature of 900 °C. The total
(oxygen) weight loss of the material was also detected performing a run
up to 1000 °C in inert atmosphere.
A FB reactor composed by a quartz tube (30 mm ID) installed in a
tubular furnace (Carbolite 1200) was used for tests of particle attrition
and CLOU process. The fluidization column was equipped with a porous
distributor and the atmosphere was either air or N2 dosed into the
system by an electronic mass-flow controller (Mod. Brooks 5850S
100L/h). The pressure before the gas distributor was measured by
means of an electronic transducer (AMECal Mod. ST-8890 0–5 PSI). The
CLOU tests were carried out by periodically switching the fluidization
gas from air to nitrogen. The gas exiting the fluidization column was
monitored with a continuous analyzer for O2, CO2, CO, CH4 and H2
(GEIT mod. 3100). Additional tests were conducted by dropping a small
amount of wood char granules (80 mg) into the column at the begin-
ning of the nitrogen step to determine the capability of the oxygen
carrier to provide oxygen to combust the solid fuel. For attrition tests
under constant flow of air, the mass of the elutriated fines was de-
termined by periodically collecting them on a paper filter (Whatman N.
4) assembly mounted downstream from the reactor.
A particle crushing test was carried out at room temperature by
means of a manually operated crush tester equipped with an electronic
load cell (piezoresistive transducer mod. RS 828–5723). The crushing
test was repeated 12 times with particles in the nominal size range of
the oxygen carrier (i.e. 0.2–0.4 mm).
Qualitative crystallographic analysis was carried out by X-ray dif-
fraction (Bruker D8 Advance powder diffractometer with CuKα radia-
tion Karlsruhe, Germany) on unprocessed and cycled OCs to evaluate
the phase evolution in the material upon cycling and any interactions
between the active oxide phases and the geopolymer matrix.
Microstructural and chemical characterization of unprocessed and
cycled OCs was carried out by field emission gun-scanning electron
microscopy (FEG-SEM, ΣIGMA Zeiss) and energy dispersion spectro-
scopy (EDS-Oxford Instruments).

3.2. Thermogravimetric analysis
The results of thermogravimetric tests under cycling conditions are
summarized in Table 3.
Fig. 2A compares weight changes with time (t) for the three OCs
during repeated isothermal cycles of reduction and oxidation at 900 °C.
Repeatability in mass change with repeated cycling was good apart
from drift of the baseline. GPCu had the highest weight changes in both
isothermal and non-isothermal conditions, either for oxidation and re-
duction steps, indicating better overall performances over the other two
OCs.
Non-isothermal conditions (Fig. 2B) resulted in similar behaviors,
but produced different trends among the three OCs. The smother shape
of curves in Fig. 2B in comparison to Fig. 2A is due to the contemporary
change in temperature, when passing from oxidation to reduction en-
vironment. In fact, the weight change started during heating from 700
to 900 °C in Ar and the cooling step from 900 to 700 °C in air and some
delay occurred because of the non-instantaneous temperature change of
the instrument. According to the thermodynamic simulations (section
3.1), similar temperature effects are expected for oxidation and
reduction for all oxides. In contrast, GPCuMn2 and GPMnFe2 seem to
better perform in non-isothermal conditions, when the temperature was
cycled between 700 and 900 °C, whilst GPCu exhibited an inverse
trend, with higher weight changes at fixed temperature of 900 °C
compared to the alternating temperature regime. Even though GPCu
still maintains the overall highest values in all conditions, such a dif-
ferent trend could be due to kinetic effects on copper oxidation at
700 °C overcoming the benefits of the thermodynamic driving force.
Weight changes (ΔW/W0%), and specific O2 gain/loss (nO2/W0
mmol/g) are reported in Table 3 for the first and last cycle of each test
with nO2 derived from ΔW using the molecular weight of O2. All data in
Table 3 were computed on the basis of the sample weight W0 just before
each segment, in order to minimize the effect of the baseline drift.
GPCu showed the highest oxygen mass change, which increased
moving from the first to the last cycle without evidence of decay. In all
cases, the loss value was higher than the corresponding gain. The
composite performance was better when the oxidation and reduction
were conducted at the same temperature (900 °C): in this case the O2
gain increased by the 23% moving from the first to the last cycle (from
0.26 to 0.32 mmol/g) while the O2 loss increased only by the 3%
keeping a value around 0.30 mmol/g. The weight gain at the 5th cycle
was 1.02 wt%, thus approached but did not achieved the theoretical
one (calculated as 1.19 wt%, Table 2).
The GP-mixed oxides had lower performance in O2 exchange at
900 °C. The weight gains and losses relative to GPCu were 38% (0.12/
0.32) and 45% (-0.14/-0.31) for GPCuMn2 and 19% (0.06/0.32) and
22% (-0.07/-0.31) for GPMnFe2. In contrast to GPCu, the OCs con-
taining Mn performed better when oxidizing treatment was carried out
at lower temperature (700 °C). In case of GPCuMn2, although a de-
crease of 15% in the mass gain was detected from the first to the last
cycle (from 0.17 to 0.14 mmol/g) while the weight loss value changed
by 22% (from −0.19 to −0.23 mmol/g). When alternating tempera-
tures were used, the oxygen exchange of GPCuMn2 improved,
achieving fractions up to 55% (0.14/0.26) and 78% (-0.23/-0.30) of the
corresponding GPCu values.
On the whole, the performance trend in oxygen carrying ability was
GPCu > GPCuMn2 > GPMnFe2 based on TG analysis, and the
asymptotic values for weight change at 900 °C were 1.0% for GPCu,
0.4% for GPCuMn2 and 0.2% for GPMnFe2. The alternating tempera-
tures in the oxidation and reduction steps only had beneficial effects for
Mn based oxygen carriers. The oxygen delivery capacity of GPCu equal
to 1.0% would collocate this material in a medium rank position with
respect to other synthetic oxygen carriers [10].

coupling ability and enhance the reactivity of the system [9,26].
A bimetallic Fe-Mn oxide (jacobsite, MnFe2O4) was identified in
GPMnFe2, in addition to quartz and hematite. The formation of the
ferrite spinel jacobsite is consistent with previous reports of Mn-Fe CLC
systems [19,27], which found is as an intermediate phase during the
regeneration of the reduced monometallic oxides.
The complete conversion to the oxidized phases of Fe2O3 and Mn2O3
during the oxidation step could not be verified in the present study
because their formation could be due to exposure to the ambient air
during XRD analysis. The transformation of jacobsite into Mn and Fe
monometallic oxides has been shown to require a relatively long time
[27], and might not be favored at low temperature. As a result, he-
matite, possibly formed by re-oxidation of Fe3O, was identified as the
predominant phase, although minor amounts of Mn2O3 were also
spotted. However, the difference between manganese and iron-bearing

3.3.2. Microstructural characterization
All OCs exhibited a homogeneous microstructure, made of the ty-
pical aluminosilicate nanoprecipitates formed by the alkali activation
process, along with well dispersed mineral oxide particles (Fig. 5). The
metal oxides maps obtained by EDS analysis and reported in Fig. 6
confirms the homogeneous distribution of the active phases within the
geopolymer matrix. Furthermore, no microcracks were detected,
pointing out the good refractoriness of the geopolymer starting mate-
rials and confirming sufficient stability at the operating conditions.
In all OCs, the mineral oxide particles were uniformly distributed in
the matrix. Some CuO agglomeration was noticed for GPCu, likely due
to the finer particle size of the CuO powder compared to the other
oxides. Elemental mapping also shows that the particle size of Mn oxide
was larger compared to Fe2O3 and CuO as previously found by particle
size measurements. Such difference in grain size and, correspondingly,
in the specific surface area (active surface) of the oxide particles em-
bedded in the matrix, are likely responsible for the lower performances
of the Mn-bearing samples compared to GPCu, especially considering
the effective molar ratio Mn:Cu = 2 used in the formulation. The larger
size of Mn2O3 particles might have decreased the O2 gain and loss ratio,
slowing the reaction rate of the Mn-bearing phases such as CuMn2O4
and Mn7SiO12, thus decreasing their oxygen uncoupling ability.
3.4. Fluidized bed reactor experiments using a GPCu oxygen carrier
On the basis of the better performance of the GPCu oxygen carrier in
TG cycling tests, FB reactor tests were carried out only for this material
in order to ascertain the suitability of GPCu to undergo real process
conditions. A comparison with unfilled geopolymer GP was also per-
formed under same operation conditions.
Firstly, the minimum fluidization velocity (Umf) of GPCu was de-
termined at T = 800 °C by measuring the pressure drop in the FB
column at increasing/decreasing fluidization velocity. An inflection
point was found in the pressure diagram at U = 0.045 m/s. The Ergun
equation [29] predicted a slightly lower value of 0.040 mm/s. Conse-
quently, GPCu granules with size 0.2–0.4 mm were categorized as B
group in the Geldart classification [29], i.e. sand-like and easily flui-
dizable powder.
The CLOU function of GPCu was tested in fluidized bed by peri-
odically switching the reactor atmosphere from N2 to air with a period
τ = 30 or 40 min. A number of repeated cycles in excess of 20 was
carried at T = 900 °C. Examples of the measured oxygen concentration
profile are shown in Fig. 7. The test started with complete oxidation of
GPCu (t = 0 in Fig. 7). The measured concentration shows good re-
peatability with increasing cycle number, with release from 0.8 to 0.5%
vol. of molecular oxygen during the reduction stage. The duration of
30 min was not sufficient for the complete depletion of oxygen in ni-
trogen, but it was enough to achieve complete re-oxidation in air, i.e.
21.0% vol. concentration of O2. The profiles obtained for the unfilled
geopolymer GP (red curve) showed no oxygen carrier activity, since no
residual oxygen was found after oxidation stage, which confirmed the
inert character of GP. Thus, catalytic activity of GPCu due to Mars and
van-Krevelen mechanism [30] via oxygen adsorption in geopolymer
matrix is likely excluded: GPCu material actually behaves as an oxygen
carrier more than a catalyst.

The ability of the GPCu to operate in a real CLOU process was de-
monstrated by feeding wood (pine) char into the FB reactor. In these
runs, just before the fluidization stream was switched from air to ni-

the reactor. The CO2 – CO (Fig. 10A) and O2 (Fig. 10B) concentrations
confirmed that GPCu delivered O2 to the fuel for oxidation. CO2 was
detected during the entire N2 stage and, by comparison with Fig. 7, the
released O2 was depleted by char combustion. In addition, CO was also
detected as consequence of the transient high temperature step and
possible reaction with residual volatiles in the wood char. The peak CO2
concentrations up to 19.5% vol. at beginning of the reduction step
(marked by star symbols) are also due to traces of residual air from the
oxidation cycle. The variation of the CO2 peaks among the cycles
(standard deviation equal to 3.4% vol.) may be attributed to the manual
feeding of the fuel, with likely changes in the feeding interval. The
small CO2 peaks noted at beginning of the oxidation step (marked by
triangle symbols) are due to residual unconverted char in the reactor,
which promptly burnt in air. However, the tests confirmed that GPCu
was able to convert most of the fuel in absence of air during OC re-
duction step. The integration of CO2 profile during the reduction step,
just before switching of air into reactor, provided the quantitative de-
termination of carbon burned by chemical looping. The average value
over the six cycles was equal to 25.6 ± 2.1 mg, accounting for around
60% of fixed carbon in the wood-char.
The final test of the suitability of GPCu for FB operation was an
attrition test that was carried out in a FB at 800 °C in air. The fluidi-
zation velocity U was set at 0.20 m/s (U/Umf = 3.8). The elutriation
rate diagram is displayed in Fig. 11, showing a typical asymptotic trend
with time. The asymptotic value was 6.3 ± 2.2 × 10-5h−1, which was
similar to attrition rates reported for other oxygen carriers [33] and
typical FB catalysts [34]. The crushing strength of the GPCu granules
was 6.4 ± 0.9 N, which was comparable to or higher than values re-
ported for natural or modified oxygen carriers of similar size [8,15,28].
The good structural integrity of the material after the FB tests was
confirmed by SEM analysis. Image analysis repeated on different sam-
plings highlighted how the OC grain size was scarcely affected by the
FB cycling tests (Fig. 12A). The process only resulted in the exfoliation
of the external layer of the granules and in the removal of superficial
debris, leaving the active phase directly exposed (Fig. 12B) but grafted
to the matrix by connecting bridges (Fig. 12B inset). Overall, the results
of both attrition and crushing tests demonstrated that the geopolymer
oxygen carrier was suitable for application in fluidized beds.
4. Conclusions
Enhanced OCs intended for CLOU applications were produced using
geopolymer composites based on CuO, Mn2O3/CuO and Fe2O3/Mn2O3.
Thermogravimetric analysis assessed the oxygen transfer ability of the
formulated materials and found that GPCu had the best performance in
isothermal conditions at 900 °C. The measured O2 loss and gain was
found just above around 1.0 wt% for GPCu, referred to a total active
phase of 37 wt%. Mixed Mn/Fe and Mn/Cu oxide-based OCs were less
effective because their performance was affected by the formation of
bimetallic Mn oxides and silicates.
Fluidized bed tests in alternating atmospheres of N2 and air con-
firmed the results of TG analysis for GPCu. The kinetics of oxidation is
faster than reduction. The rate index was higher than values reported
for common oxygen carriers. The results of attrition and crushing tests
demonstrated GPCu is a suitable oxygen carrier for fluidized bed ap-
plications.
Confirmation of the ability of GPCu to operate in a real CLOU
process was provided by batch tests of wood char combustion. Evidence
of CO2 formation was observed during repeated cycles under inert at-
mosphere and in presence of char particles, with GPCu being able to
convert more than 80% of fixed carbon in the wood-char. The research
indicates that novel geopolymer-based oxygen carriers are suitable for
CLOU combustion of solid fuels operated in complex process schemes.
