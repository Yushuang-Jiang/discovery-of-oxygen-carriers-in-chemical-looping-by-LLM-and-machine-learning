A B S T R A C T   
Chemical looping combustion with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU) enables solid fuel combustion with CO2 capture at 
low cost due to the capacity of the oxygen carrier to release molecular oxygen. The present study is focused on 
testing the performance of a magnetic Cu-based oxygen carrier with reinforced mechanical properties owing to 
the addition of kaolin via the combustion of three types of biomass in a 1.5 kWth continuous CLOU unit. The 
effect of the main operating variables on process performance parameters was studied. Complete combustion of 
the different biomass types was achieved. Higher CO2 capture efficiencies were observed at 800–950 ◦C 
compared to the previous work done with this oxygen carrier without kaolin doping. The highest value of 97.5% 
CO2 capture efficiency was obtained at 950 ◦C during the combustion of pine sawdust. Moreover, the oxygen 
carrier retained its magnetic properties without any agglomeration problems after 80 h of hot circulation 
operation.

1. Introduction 
CO2 emissions are at their highest historical levels, with a value of 
420 ppm recorded in November 2022, which is approximately 49% 
higher than the peak of 280 ppm achieved in the preindustrial period 
[1]. The latest IPCC report shows that if there is a continuing trend of 
increasing CO2 and other greenhouse gas emissions, global warming will 
exceed 1.5 ◦C and there will be irreversible impacts for humanity [2]. 
The aim of the Paris Agreement was to lead the way to disrupting global 
warming by inhibiting the CO2 released into the atmosphere [3]. Carbon 
capture and storage (CCS) is one of the technologies being developed to 
meet this goal, particularly bioenergy with carbon capture and storage 
(BECCS), which is accepted as a promising method in terms of processes 
producing negative CO2 emissions. Chemical looping combustion (CLC) 
technologies stand out as having the most potential of all BECCS 
methods [4–6]. 
CLC defines a system consisting of two interconnected fluidized bed 
reactors with a solid oxygen carrier (OC) circulating between them. 
Combustion takes place in the fuel reactor with oxygen provided by the 
OC. The depleted OC is subsequently circulated to the air reactor and 
reoxidized. This process yields CO2 and steam are as combustion prod­
ucts in the fuel reactor. Thus, once the steam is condensed, a highly 
concentrated stream of CO2 is effortlessly achieved that can be stored 
without the extra costs incurred by gas separation. Given that CO2 
capture is inherent to CLC processes, these technologies have a very low 
global energy penalty [7–9]. 
CLC technologies were initially developed for use with gaseous fuels, 
with later developments enabling it to be applied to different solid fuels 
[4–7,10]. When solid fuels are used, there are two possible combustion 
modes, in-situ gasification chemical looping (iG-CLC) and chemical 
looping with oxygen uncoupling (CLOU). In iG-CLC mode, volatile 
matter is generated during the devolatilization of the solid fuel with the 
OC during gasification of the char with CO2 or steam for conversion to 
CO and H2, which further react with the OC. In CLOU mode, char and 
volatiles react directly with the gaseous O2 released by the OC, see Fig. 1 
[7]. Since the oxidation rate is higher in CLOU mode than in iG-CLC 
mode, the fuel conversion rate is also higher in CLOU mode [7]. In recent 
research, numerical simulation of a 150 kWth chemical looping 
combustion plant of numerically was performed for biomass fuel com­
bustion [11]. Comparison of the obtained results with experimental data 
showed good accuracy for predictions in terms of instantaneous and 
local behaviour inside the fluidized bed system. Moreover, Reinking and 
et al. [12] presented various computational fluid dynamic (CFD) simu­
lations of the performance of copper OCs in a fluidized bed system. They 
found that a reduction in the OC inventory caused the fuel reactor 
temperature to increase. It was also mentioned that changing the fuel 
feed from 200 kW to 100 kW led to a lower amount of OC being needed. 
A number of studies have been conducted on different Cu-based OCs 
[13]. One of these studies [14] considered the effects of adding different 
components of varying composition to them. Therefore, four Cu-based 
OCs were reinforced with 20 wt% manganese oxide, 60 wt% iron 
oxide, 40 wt% cobalt oxide and 40 wt% nickel oxide by the sol gel 
method. All the OCs presented a decline in reaction rates during the test 
cycles. Agglomeration, sintering, ash deposition and transformation of 
active components cause the deterioration in reactivity. Therefore, it is 
essential to improve the reactivity and mechanical stability of OCs. Cu, 
Mn and Co-based OCs, Cu–Mn mixed oxides, Ca–Mn perovskites and 
other mixed oxides containing Ti, Ca, Mg, Fe and Mn are preferred in 
CLOU mode owing to their capacity to release O2 gas [15–20]. Gayan et 
el. [15] prepared OCs based on copper oxide doped with SiO2, ZrO2, 
Al2O3, MgAl2O4, MgO and TiO2 using different methods of impregnation 
and mechanical mixing. As a result, Cu-based OCs including Al2O3 
showed low reactivity and surface reduction after redox cycles. The 
application of MgAl2O4, ZrO2 by the preparation method of mechanical 
mixing followed by palletization showed a positive effect on mechanical 
strength. 
In another study, the modification of a CuO-based OC with chryso­
lite, limestone and iron ore were investigated [21]. As a result of 
applying these three ores, it was found that chrysolite could be applied 
as a support to increase the rate of oxygen release, and limestone could 
be utilized to enhance the oxidization rate. In all cases this modification 
improved the stability of the Cu-oxide OC at high temperature. Imtiaz 
et al. [22] considered the agglomeration behaviour of six different 
synthesized Cu-based OCs containing 60 wt% CuO. Among them, the 
MgO support showed the highest agglomeration resistance during 
evaluation in a fluidized bed reactor. Yan et al. [23] boosted chemical 
looping combustion performance of red mud by adding copper oxide. 
Although improvement in performance was observed, deterioration still 
occurred during the redox cycles caused by particle agglomeration and 
sintering. Therefore, further studies are necessary to improve thermal 
resistance of OCs. OCs based on geopolymer and metal oxides are an 
alternative for increasing their mechanical and thermal properties [24]. 
A. Natali Murri et al. [25] investigated three different geopolymer-based 
OCs containing copper oxide, copper and manganese oxide, manganese 
oxide and iron oxide. Results of the batch tests of the geopolymer OC 
containing copper oxide showed that this new type of OC could be a 
suitable alternative for the CLOU process. 
Recent studies have focused on Cu-based and Cu–Mn OCs for 
biomass combustion due to their fast-redox kinetics and high oxygen 
transport capacity [26–29]. Given the good performance of CuO and 
Cu–Mn OCs for the combustion of biomass and coal, efforts are being 
focused on these materials to improve the properties that show the 
greatest interest. 
During solid fuel combustion, ashes are generated and may accu­
mulate in the CLC system, causing operational problems. Ash draining 
should then be considered, although this step could also lead to loss of 
the OC. To prevent this problem, the use of OCs with magnetic prop­
erties was proposed because they can easily be separated from the ash 
and OC mixture and reutilized [29]. OC separation from ashes is of 
special interest in the case of synthetic OCs, such as those developed for 
CLOU, which are often more expensive than low-cost materials such as 
industrial residues or minerals. Mn-Fe-based OCs with magnetic prop­
erties retain their magnetic properties throughout the process [30] This 
type of OC demonstrated magnetism at calcining temperatures higher

term of mechanical properties, crushing strength of the OC during the 
combustion operation was almost constant, although the air jet index 
(AJI) value increased from 0.6 to 3.4% [29].

kaolin was added. The OCs were also tested in the batch fluidized bed 
unit with no observed agglomeration of particles and achieving 
acceptable results with regard to reactivity, mechanical strength and 
magnetic permeability. One improved OC was selected and tested. It was 
synthesized in a granulator with the addition of kaolin to the previous 
formulation to improve its mechanical strength, but maintaining the 
same ratio of CuO, Mn3O4, and Fe2O3. In this study, the effects of tem­
perature, excess air and OC-to-fuel ratio on process performance were 
investigated in a 1.5 kWth continuous unit under CLOU conditions 
during the combustion of three types of biomass. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. OC preparation 
The OC tested in the present work was prepared in a spouted fluid­
ized bed spray granulator (Glatt W51530 + OPP1) using CuO (Panreac), 
Mn3O4 (Micromax, Elkem), Fe2O3 (Acros Organics) and kaolin (Sumi­
tomo Seika) as raw materials. Different additives, including PEO-1 
(Sumitomo), were also used in the granulation process. The OC con­
tained 30 wt% CuO, 30.55 wt% Mn3O4, 31.95 wt% Fe2O3 and 7.5 wt% 
kaolin. The obtained granules were calcined at 1050 ◦C for 4 h. After 
calcination, particles were sieved to the desired range of 100–300 μm. 
This OC will subsequently be denoted as Cu30MnFekao7.5. Table 1

2.2. Biomass 
Three types of biomass were used as fuel during the experiments: 
pine sawdust (Pinus sylvestris), olive stones (Olea europaea) and almond 
shells (Prunus dulcis), with a particle size of +500-–2000 μm. Properties 
of the three types of biomass are shown in Table 2. 
2.3. Experimental set-up 
Combustion experiments were performed in a 1.5 kWth continuous 
CLOU unit with a duration of 42 h of combustion operation and 80 h of 
hot fluidization. The CLOU unit was loaded with 2.8 kg of OC. As 
observed in Fig. 2, the unit consisted of two interconnected fluidized 
beds acting as fuel and air reactors, with a loop seal to prevent gas 
mixing between the fuel and air reactors; one cyclone; and one solid 
control valve. The reactors were heated by electrical furnaces, and gas 
flow rates were controlled by mass flow. An air-N2 mixture was used in 
the air reactor (2100 LN/h). N2 was used in fuel reactor and loop seal at 
180 LN/h and 100 LN/h, respectively. 
Test temperatures were varied between 800 and 950 ◦C for three 
types of biomass used as fuel to assess the effect of this variable on the 
process performance. The effects of changes in the circulation rate be­
tween reactors (20–28 kg/h) and the air excess ratio (λ) changing from 1 
to 2.1 were studied with pine sawdust, see Table 3. 
Thermal input was between 625 and 850 Wth for any biomass used: 
pine sawdust, almond shells and olive stones.

3.1.1. Effect of temperature in the fuel reactor 
The effect of the temperature on ɳcc and char conversion, as well as 
on combustion during operation with pine saw dust, almond shells and 
olive stones are plotted in Fig. 4. It should also be mentioned that a 
negligible amount of elutriated carbon was found for all the biomass 
types. 
In all experiments, even at the lowest temperature of 800 ◦C, com­
plete combustion of combustion gases was achieved, see Fig. 4(a). This 
result represents an advantage since there would be no need for a post- 
treatment step to complete the combustion, such as oxygen polishing 
[34]. In all cases, ɳcc increased as the temperature in the fuel reactor rose 
[35]. Therefore, the highest ɳcc values were achieved for all three types 
of biomass at the highest temperature of 950 ◦C, reaching values close to 
97.4% for pine sawdust. The increase found in ɳcc was related to the rise 
in the char conversion in the fuel reactor, see Fig. 4 [31], which shows 
char conversion for the different fuel reactor temperatures. As can also 
be seen in Fig. 4, char reactivity increases from olive stone to pine 
sawdust. The highest char conversion was obtained at 950 ◦C for pine 
sawdust (90%, corresponding to the highest CO2 capture efficiency 
value of 97.4%). As char conversion increased, lower amounts of un­
burnt char were transported to the air reactor, causing ɳcc to increase. 
The biomass with highest volatile content can also be considered to have 
the highest combustion rate, resulting in the highest ɳcc. The highest ɳcc 
value was achieved with pine sawdust, which has the highest level of 
volatile matter of the three types of biomass tested. 
3.1.2. Effect of solids circulation rate and air excess ratio 
Furthermore, the effect of the circulation rate (ɸ) and the air excess 
ratio (λ) was studied during pine sawdust combustion at a constant 
temperature of 850 ◦C in the fuel reactor. In all cases, thermal input was 
625 Wth. The results are plotted in Fig. 5. 
Fig. 5(a) illustrates the effect of excess air on the CO2 capture, 
combustion efficiency and char conversion at 850 ◦C. By changing λ 
from 1.3 to 2.1, CO2 capture, combustion efficiency and char conversion 
were almost constant, with values of 92, 100 and 69% respectively. This 
clearly indicates the complete regeneration of the OC in the air reactor 
for all the λ values used. 
Moreover, in order to consider the effect of ɸ on the same parame­
ters, the circulation rate was changed from 20 to 28 kg/h, leading to ɸ 
values between 2.9 and 4. CO2 capture, combustion efficiency and char 
conversion were not significantly affected, as shown in Fig. 5. Com­
bustion efficiencies >99.9% were observed in all cases for all the 
different ɸ values. Moreover, ɳcc was constant for all the oxygen-to-fuel 
ratios analysed. This means that OC reactivity was high enough to burn 
the solid fuel in the fuel reactor and be regenerated in the air reactor 
within the studied interval. 
3.2. OC characterization after reaction 
Characterization was performed on Cu30MnFekao7.5 after 80 h of 
hot circulation and 42 h of combustion, see Table 1. Oxygen transport 
capacity and the reactivity were studied by multiple redox cycles in 
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) using 100% N2 as the reduction gas 
and air as the oxidation gas, with the results considered for comparison 
being those of the third redox cycle. In order to explore the evolution of 
mechanical resistance, the crushing strength of particles extracted at 
different times of operation was measured using a Shimpo FGN-5×
apparatus. A decrease was found with a final value of 1.1 N, slightly 
higher than the 1 N that was considered an acceptable value for oper­
ation in a fluidized bed [36]. 
The reactivity of Cu30MnFekao7.5 was slightly higher after previous 
use in the unit. Fig. 6 demonstrates this activation of the particles, as 
solids conversion after 20 min of reaction increased from 0.77 in the 
fresh material to 0.83 for the used OC. 
Table 1 also shows that there was one important increase in the 
oxygen release rate, confirming the activation of the OC during reaction 
in the continuous unit. This increase can be correlated to the increase in 
the particle porosity and oxygen transport capacity of the particles. The 
evolution of the magnetic permeability of the particles was also 
considered and is shown in Fig. 7. A decrease was observed from 3.6 to 
1.6, the latter value being acceptable to allow separation from ashes. In 
order to roughly estimate the efficiency of OC separation from ashes, 
fines were collected from the fuel reactor and air reactor filters after 80 h 
of operation in the continuous unit. Using a simple magnet, 99.3% of OC 
particles were able to be separated from the ashes, indicating that it 
would be possible to utilize Cu30MnFekao7.5 on an industrial scale with 
easy separation and regranulation of its fines. 
In order to ascertain attrition performance, the AJI [37] of the fresh 
and the used OC was measured. The AJI of the OC increased from 0.2 to 
2.6%, although the latter value was considered adequate since it was 
still below the 5% limit set for fluidized bed operation. 
Finally, the morphological changes in the used particles were 
investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As can be observed 
in Fig. 8, the SEM images show a more porous structure after 80 h of 
fluidization operation. The increase in porosity combined with a 
reduction in crushing strength led to the higher AJI value of AJI than 
that of the fresh OC, see Table 1. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDX) analysis was also performed on the used particles to look for 
possible changes in the distribution of elements. Cu, Mn, and Fe were 
found to be homogeneously distributed throughout the particles. The

3.3. Discussion 
The present paper investigates the improvement in performance 
achieved with the kaolin doping of a previously developed Cu/Mn/Fe- 
based OC with magnetic properties [29]. A comparison between the 
results found in this work with those found with a similar OC without the 
addition of kaolin is plotted in Fig. 9. The results obtained in the current 
work showed an improvement in ɳcc for all fuels and different temper­
atures with respect to those found with the previously studied OC. The 
addition of kaolin to the OC led to increased combustion, in turn leading 
to a reduction in the amount of char transferred to the air reactor and 
increasing ɳcc. Combustion efficiency was always 100%, similar to that 
found in the OC without kaolin [29]. 
For olive stones at 900 ◦C, the ɳcc value improved from 80 to 88% for 
the newly developed kaolin-doped OC. Additionally, a rise in ɳcc to 95% 
was observed at the same temperature during the combustion of pine 
sawdust for the newly developed OC compared to the previous work. By 
introducing kaolin to the OC, operation temperature in the fuel reactor 
was able to be increased up to 950 ◦C, which meant that ɳcc increased 
when compared to 900 ◦C for both the new and previously studied OCs. 
Fig. 9 shows higher char conversion for the new kaolin-doped OC 
against that of the previous OC without kaolin, which was restricted 
from use at temperatures higher than 900 ◦C. 
A comparison was made with other Cu-based OCs tested for the 
combustion of biomass in the continuous unit under CLOU conditions. 
Fig. 10 shows the effect of fuel reactor temperature on ɳcc during the 
combustion of pine sawdust with three OCs: Cu60MgAl OC [29] with 
60% CuO in its composition, Cu–Mn OC [28] with 34 wt% copper 
oxide, and kaolin-doped Cu30MnFekao7.5 with 30% CuO. 
The three OCs present different significantly different behaviours, 
particularly with regard to the range of operating temperatures in the 
fuel reactor and ɳcc. Cu60MgAl has the lowest range of operating tem­
peratures, achieving a very high ɳcc value of 98% during the combustion 
of pine sawdust at 900 ◦C [26,29]. Cu–Mn OC [28] showed a limited 
range of operating temperatures that starts at a low 770 ◦C, with its use 
restricted to temperatures lower than 900 ◦C due to agglomeration 
problems. However, a high ɳcc value of 98.7% was reached at 850 ◦C. 
The Cu30MnFekao7.5 material presented a broad range of operating 
temperatures, between 800 and 950 ◦C, although the ɳcc values for pine 
sawdust combustion were 91 and 95% at 850 and 900 ◦C, respectively. 
The different range of operating temperatures can be attributed to the 
different concentration profiles of the oxygen released. For Cu60MgAl, 
the oxygen concentration corresponds to the equilibrium of the CuO/ 
Cu2O redox system, while for Cu–Mn, the oxygen concentration is be­
tween that corresponding to CuO/Cu2O and to Mn2O3/Mn3O4 [17]. For 
Cu30MnFekao7.5, the concentration of oxygen released corresponds to 
a system of mixed Cu, Fe and Mn oxides. The oxygen concentration 
profiles of these redox systems are shown in Fig. 11. Differences in ɳcc 
values are associated with the rate of oxygen released and its

On the whole, Cu30MnFekao7.5 showed good performance in terms 
of reactivity, Cu utilization for the CLOU reaction and process perfor­
mance in the combustion of different types of biomass. Nevertheless, a 
reduction in the mechanical strength and a rise in the AJI index indi­

h of combustion. However, the magnetic properties were retained to 
make Cu30MnFekao7.5 easily separable from the ashes. Furthermore, 
this OC uses less copper oxide rather than previous OCs developed for 
CLOU, and it is possible to operate in a wide temperature window be­
tween 800 and 950 ◦C in the fuel reactor without any problems of 
fluidization and agglomeration. Long-term experiments are needed to 
provide a more realistic lifetime when operating under low chemical 
stress conditions (with low ΔX) in order to reduce damage in the 
particles. 
4. Conclusions 
Combustion of three types of biomass was carried out in a 1.5 kWth 
continuous CLOU unit using Cu30MnFekao7.5 OC. The performance of 
this magnetic Cu-based OC at various temperatures ranging between 
800 and 950 ◦C was evaluated, including the effects of air excess and the 
solids circulation rate. Complete combustion of gases was achieved 
when all the types of biomass were burnt, even at the lowest tempera­
ture of 800 ◦C. A newly developed OC, Cu30MnFekao7.5, showed good 
results for ɳcc for all the types of biomass tested. The presence of kaolin 
in the OC increased the ɳcc found with respect to the same OC without 
kaolin. By increasing the temperature, ɳcc increased due to higher char 
conversion at higher temperature. At 950 ◦C, the highest ɳcc of 97.4% 
was obtained during combustion of pine sawdust. Due to the high 
reactivity of the OC, effects of air excess and solids circulation rate on 
the performance of Cu30MnFekao7.5 were found to be negligible. After 
80 h of operation without any agglomeration problems, Cu30MnFe­
kao7.5 retained its magnetic properties, which made it easy to separate 
from ashes for reuse in the plant.
