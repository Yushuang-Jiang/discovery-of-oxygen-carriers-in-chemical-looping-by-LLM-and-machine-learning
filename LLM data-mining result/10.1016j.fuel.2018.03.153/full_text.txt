A B S T R A C T
Chemical looping combustion allows a simple separation of CO2 during the combustion of fossil fuels, thanks to
the use of regenerable oxygen carriers. In this work, novel materials containing manganese and iron/manganese
oxides have been developed via geopolymerization, and characterized in thermogravimetric apparatus and ﬁxed
bed reactor. The materials demonstrated suitable characteristics for chemical looping combustion (CLC). The
tests conducted in the temperature range 800–900 °C revealed the good performance of the developed oxygen
carriers, which also exhibited the ability to release O2 in inert conditions. Eﬃciencies in CO conversion up to
99% were achieved, as well as some synergies between Fe and Mn oxides gave a beneﬁcial eﬀect toward the
oxygen yield. X-ray diﬀraction analyses of the samples conﬁrmed the eﬀective reduction/oxidation behavior of
the materials, as well as the morphological characterization did not reveal dramatic changes of the internal
microstructure up to 900 °C.
1. Introduction
The separation of carbon dioxide from the exhausts of power plants
with conventional processes (e.g. wet absorption) may signiﬁcantly
impact the ﬁnal price of the produced energy [1]. As alternative to
more innovative and eﬃcient methods, like membrane separation or Ca
looping [2], the chemical looping combustion (CLC) accomplishes in-
direct fuel combustion by use of a solid oxygen carrier (OC), generally a
metal oxide, with the function of transporting the combustion oxygen
from an air reactor to a fuel reactor, usually designed as two coupled
ﬂuidized beds [3]. The combustion takes place in the fuel reactor
through the reaction between the fuel and the solid OC, which is sub-
sequently reduced to a lower oxidation form. The reduced OC is then
transferred to the air reactor where it is oxidized and regenerated by an
air stream without any need of pure oxygen [4]. A further improvement
of CLC is represented by the chemical looping combustion with oxygen
uncoupling (CLOU), where the oxygen carrier, e.g. Cu oxides, is also
able to release O2 in inert conditions at high temperature [5].

The CLC process is particularly promising since it enables the in-
herent separation of carbon dioxide from diluting species, ﬁrst of all the
air nitrogen. In fact, the stream exiting the fuel reactor is only com-
posed of CO2 and easily separable water vapor. Furthermore, under
certain conditions both reduction and oxidation steps can be exo-
thermic, e.g. the CLC of carbon monoxide by means of Ni/NiO oxygen
carriers [6]. Thus, heat can be obtained from both air and fuel reactors,
with contained temperature diﬀerence between them. This concern is
detrimental in other cycled processes, for instance the calcium looping,
where high temperature diﬀerence occurs [7].
Potential OC materials have to meet a series of precise requirements
to be suitable for the CLC process, such as: high oxygen transport ca-
pacity, negligible decline in the performance after repeated cycles, re-
sistance to comminution and agglomeration, large availability and low
cost. Moreover the choice of the OC has to take into account also the
related environmental and safety concerns [4].
Ni and Cu based OCs boast elevated eﬀectiveness; however they are
aﬀected by a low stability to multiple redox cycles, and are not en-
vironmental and health neutral because of their toxicity [8–11]. Dif-
ferently, Fe and Mn oxides are cost-eﬀective, non-toxic and environ-
mental friendly, although less eﬀective that Ni and Cu oxides [4,12].
The combination of these properties to good performance makes these
compounds highly interesting OCs for CLC. Iron oxide generally be-
haves by reduction/oxidation of the Fe2O3/Fe3O4 pair, with a theore-
tical oxygen carrier capacity of 3%. The most active couple of manga-
nese oxide is Mn3O4/MnO, which gives an oxygen carrier capacity of
7%; Mn2O3 can be also used but its re-oxidation is generally not com-
plete due to higher stability of Mn3O4 at temperature above 800 °C
[12]. The active metal oxide is commonly embedded into a temperature
stable and porous matrix, which may also increase the impact/abrasion
resistance and the oxygen diﬀusion ability of the OC. However, this is
usually achieved by traditional wet impregnation methods of sup-
porting media. This process requires drying and calcination steps that,
together with the cost of the support, aﬀect the cost eﬀectiveness of the
OC for a large scale application.
With the aim of overcoming this fundamental limitation, the au-
thors recently reported for the ﬁrst time the production of an OC based
on Fe2O3 powders embedded into a geopolymer (GP) matrix [13].
Geopolymers are nanostructured ceramic materials that can be pro-
duced at moderate temperature (25–100 °C) by alkaline activation of
alumino-silicate precursors through an easy, low-cost and green pre-
paration. These materials, if purposely doped, can be suitable for CLC
since they combine a diﬀused meso-porosity and a high surface area to
substantially high mechanical strength, abrasion resistance and stability
to temperature up to 1000 °C [14,15].
In this work, the study on geopolymer-based oxygen carriers was
further put forward by considering geopolymer composites based on
manganese and iron-manganese oxides. The synergistic action of the
two oxides was investigated. The performances of the novel OCs were
compared in thermo-gravimetric apparatus and in a CLC laboratory
scale plant (ﬁxed bed), also in relation to their morphological and
structural characteristics.

Thus, the actual CH4 composition during reduction was 3.6% vol. The

heated up to the reaction temperature at a rate of 30 °C/min in inert
atmosphere (Ar). Then, the oxidizing and reducing gas ﬂows were al-

thermal segment. The test was also conducted using pure Ar instead of
CH4/Ar both during heating tests performed up to 1000 °C as well as
during the isothermal segments at 800 and 900 °C. The diﬀerential
scanning calorimetric (DSC) signal was also simultaneously detected
with the thermogravimetric (TG) one to determine the endo/exo-
thermic character of the reactions occurring during the tests.
The schematics of the CLC plant is shown in Fig. 1. It is composed of
two cylindrical reactors made in AISI 316 (22 mm ID and 500 mm
length) that are kept at the operating temperature by means of an
electric furnace (Carbolite 1200). The ﬁrst one (gasiﬁcation reactor in
Fig. 1) produces the combustible gas mixture that is converted via CLC
in the subsequent CLC reactor (Fig. 1). Further details about the ex-
perimental facility can be found in Bendoni et al. [16].
The gasiﬁcation reactor is ﬁlled with coal char (10–20 g), whilst the
CLC reactor is loaded with a preﬁxed weight of OC granules in between
two layers of alumina wool. The switching of the operation in the two
reactors is possible by means of control valves (Fig. 1). The con-
centration of CO, CO2, and O2 in the gas stream exiting the plant was
measured after clean-up using a multi-component continuous gas ana-
lyzer (mon. GEIT GAS 3160). The pressure at the base of the gasiﬁca-
tion reactor is monitored through a digital manometer. A mass-ﬂow
controller (mod. Brooks F21780-001) regulates the ﬂow rate of CO2
from a bottle, while the air for the regeneration is provided by a vo-
lumetric pump.
Three diﬀerent operation modes are possible by switching the
control valves:
I. feeding CO2 to the gasiﬁcation reactor, the exiting stream being
analyzed for determining the syngas composition;
II. the gas exiting the gasiﬁer is fed to the CLC reactor, the analyzer
monitoring the syngas conversion in the CLC reactor;
III. CO2 stream is switched-oﬀand air is fed to the CLC tube for the OC
regeneration until O2 concentration of 21% vol. is achieved.
The characterization of the materials was done as follows. Open
pore size distribution in the range 0.0058–100 µm was ascertained by
mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP-Thermo/Finnigan 240), morpho-
logical and microstructural characterization was done by environ-
mental scanning electron microscopy (E-SEM FEI Quanta 200, FEI
Company, USA) and qualitative crystallographic analysis by X-ray dif-
fraction (Bruker D8 Advance powder diﬀractometer with CuKα radia-
tion, Karlsruhe, Germany).
2.2. Oxygen carrier materials
The geopolymer composite oxygen carriers were prepared ac-
cording to a previously-developed formulation [13,16], by adding a
metakaolin powder (Argical M-1200S, IMERYS Minerals, UK) to an
aqueous solution of potassium poly-silicate (SiO2:K2O = 2.0, H2O/
K2O = 13.5) obtained by mixing KOH pellets (purity > 99%, Merck)
in distilled water and adding fumed silica powder (99.8%, Sigma-Al-
drich) under magnetic stirring. The slurry, with solid to liquid ratio of
2.3, was mixed through mechanical stirring for 10 min, then the dif-
ferent metal oxides were added to the mixtures to the extent of 51% by
weight of dry solid geopolymer reactants. Fe2O3 (MRC, Toulouse,
France) and Mn2O3 (Sigma-Aldrich) 325 mesh powders were used.
Two samples were obtained and labeled as GPMn and GPMnFe, in
which equal amounts of Mn2O3 and/or Fe2O3 were mixed to the geo-
polymer matrix [16]. The obtained monoliths were crashed in a mortar
and the fragments were sieved in the size fraction of 200–300 μm, the
produced granules being shown in Fig. 2.
2.3. Fuel
Sulcis lignite from Sardinia (I) was selected because of the sub-
stantially high ﬁxed carbon content (44% by weight on dry basis). The
char produced under inert atmosphere at 800 °C for one hour was
crushed in a mortar and sieved in the range 1000–2000 μm. The re-
activity of the Sulcis char in CO2/Ar atmosphere was investigated in a
TGA test at 900 °C and compared to those of a wood char (beech) and a
graphite (Fig. 3). It resulted a rather high reactivity, intermediate be-
tween wood char and graphite, achieving full conversion in less than
three times with respect to the wood char.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Thermodynamic assessment
Firstly, a theoretical evaluation was carried out of the thermo-
dynamic equilibrium for comparing the activities of the two metal
oxides in the temperature range of interest for CLC. Fig. 4 displays the
results of chemical equilibrium computations obtained by using HSC®
software tool for the system Me2O3-Me3O4-MeO-O2, where Me means
Fe or Mn. One mole of Me2O3 was assumed as input for the elaboration.
It clearly appears that in the lower temperature range, i.e. 700–900 °C,
Mn-oxides are more active than Fe-oxides for CLC. Since the reduced
phases Mn3O4 and MnO are more stable than the respective Fe phases, a
larger amount of oxygen can be made available for fuel oxidation. It is
also worth noting that in the whole temperature range 700–1200 °C an

The DSC signal during a single redox cycle is shown in Fig. 6 along
with the corresponding weight change curve for GPMnFe at 900 °C. The

The weight change under inert conditions is shown in Fig. 7 as
function of the temperature for GPMnFe and GPMn. Upon the initial
decrease due to the release of water from the geopolymeric matrix
lasting up to 300 °C, a marked weight decrease occurs over 900 °C as
consequence of the shift from more to less oxidized forms of the metal
oxides, with associated release of oxygen. However, GPMnFe exhibits a
lower temperature threshold (around 900 °C) with respect to GPMn
(925 °C), thanks to synergistic eﬀects among the mixed oxides in the
former OC, though the ﬁnal weight decrease at 1000 °C is higher for
GPMn (0.9%) than for GPMnFe (0.7%). The inset in Fig. 7 shows the
behavior of GPMnFe when subjected to alternating atmospheres of Ar
and air at 900 °C. After few cycles, the response is fairly repeatable,
indicating the possibility of using GPMnFe for accomplishing CLOU
combustion of solid fuels. Accordingly to the weight change curve in
function of temperature shown in Fig. 7, the GPMn composite did not
show any detectable weight change when cycled in air/Ar atmosphere
at 900 °C, being the oxygen loss retarded in comparison with GPMnFe.
At T = 1000 °C, the oxygen yield amounts to 0.23 and 0.28 mmol/g for
GPMnFe and GPMn, respectively (Table 1). Even if with some level of
uncertainty due to the limited amount of sample from TGA tests, the
XRD analysis pointed out that a conversion to less oxidized phase
preferentially occurs for Mn than for Fe oxides, only Mn3O4 being de-
tected besides to Fe2O3 and mixed (Fe3+Mn3+) oxides after the thermal
treatment in Ar up to 1000 °C.

4. Conclusions
Novel synthetic oxygen carriers for CLC based on geopolymer
composites can be produced with a simple and one-step fabrication
protocol. In this research they were obtained by adding powder of Mn
and Fe oxides to a standard geopolymeric slurry, producing monoliths
with good mechanical resistance to be granulated by milling and
sieving. The metal oxide content achieved values of around 40% by
weight.
TGA tests carried out with Mn and Mn-Fe based oxygen carriers
proved that they are eﬀective in the temperature range 800–900 for
combustion of methane at 3.6% vol., whilst tests in a lab scale reactor
demonstrated the ability to oxidize CO in CO2 at more high con-
centration (> 10% vol.) with high eﬃciency. Both steps of OC reduc-
tion and oxidation are exothermic, as proven from DSC analysis.
GPMnFe is more eﬀective than GPMn in the lower temperature limit
thanks to a synergy between the two metal oxides. The temperature of
900 °C is eﬀective for accomplishing the CLC of syngas, as well as the
CLOU of solid carbons. XRD analyses of the samples conﬁrmed the
reduction/oxidation behavior of the Mn-based OC, as well as the
morphological characterization did not reveal strong modiﬁcations of
the internal microstructure up to 900 °C. Further research is required
for extending the results obtained in ﬁxed bed conﬁguration to ﬂuidized
bed that is more realistic for application at industrial scale.
