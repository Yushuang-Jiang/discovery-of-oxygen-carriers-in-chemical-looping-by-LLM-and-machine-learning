A B S T R A C T   
The solar-driven thermochemical CO2 conversion is an effective way to achieve the mission of carbon peaking 
and carbon neutrality. However, finding a material with excellent thermal stability, oxygen exchange capacity, 
and CO selectivity is one of the most important challenges in the thermochemical CO2 conversion approach. This 
study investigated the solar thermochemical CO2-splitting characteristics through the synergistic thermal cata­
lytic activity of CuO and NiFe2O4 and the effect of SiC photothermal properties. The thermochemical redox 
cycling performance of CO2 conversion through thermally activated catalysts was experimentally investigated 
via thermogravimetric combined quantum chemical calculations to provide comprehensive insights into the 
physicochemical principles underlying the thermochemical catalytic CO2-splitting. The X-ray diffraction, scan­
ning electron microscopy, and energy spectrum detector technics were developed to gain detail on the crystal 
structure, surface morphology, and chemical composition changes in the reacting media. The newly synthesized 
composite material consisting of 25%CuO and 75%NiFe2O4 (CNF-13) coated SiC porous skeleton resulted in 
higher CO2-splitting catalytic activity with an instantaneous CO production of 10.98mL ⋅ min-1gCNF-13
−1 
and direct 
CO2-to-CO conversion rate of 33.9% at 1000–1200 K reaction temperature. The thermochemical CO2-splitting 
activity and redox stability of CNF-13@SiC was promoted by the photothermal properties of SiC support and the 
formation of Cu2O and Fe3O4 new active material phases easing CO2 reduction at low energy. This study pro­
vided experimental and theoretical findings that can be used as guidance for the fundamental research and 
application of CO2 catalytic conversion into fuels through a solar thermochemical driven strategy.   
1. Introduction 
The world’s largest source of greenhouse gas emissions from human 
activity is the burning of fossil fuels to generate electricity and heat, 
accounting for more than half of all carbon dioxide emissions in the 
atmosphere [1]. CO2 emissions have been found to grow at 2.6% per 
year of the average rate from 2000 to 2014 [2]. According to the chal­
lenges and opportunities associated with the Chinese government’s en­
ergy transitions for carbon neutrality, there is an urgent need to 
vigorously develop carbon dioxide emission reduction technology. 
Carbon dioxide capture and storage technology have shown great 
application value in the face of increasing environmental problems. The 
main ways to reduce CO2 emission while promoting chemical energy are 
electrochemistry, photochemistry, and thermochemistry. In addition, 
CO2 capture and sequestration technology are also an effective way of 
CO2 storage and utilization. Intensive research has been made on the 
electrochemical reduction of CO2 [3–6]. Considering the attractive 
application prospects of the high-temperature thermochemical process 
[7–15], the solar-driven thermochemical cyclic conversion approach 
can contribute to efficient CO2 utilization. The most promising approach

is CO2 conversion into important chemical feedstocks through a ther­
mochemical process using clean and renewable energy. Currently, re­
searchers are conducting experimental tests through the combination of 
solar simulators and thermochemical reactors [16–20] that generate 
encouraging results, thus reflecting the reliability and feasibility of such 
a process. 
Processing CO2 conversion through a multi-step thermochemical 
cycle was found more practical than the one-step CO2 reduction because 
the multi-step cycle is thermodynamically advantageous and can be 
operated at lower temperatures. Especially, the two-step thermochem­
ical redox cycles using metal oxides are more flexible since the process is 
based on gas-solid reaction mechanisms and produces gaseous products. 
This facilitates the isolation of the metal oxides from the products [21]. 
The two-step thermochemical redox cycles are typically characterized 
by the thermal reduction step followed by oxidation of the reduced 
metal oxides with CO2. 
Thermal reduction (TR) : MOox→MOred + δ/2O2
(1)  
CO2 −splitting (CDS) : MOred + δCO2→MOox + δCO
(2)  
where MOox and MOred represent the oxidized and reduced state of the 
redox materials, respectively. The thermal reduction of the metal oxide 
materials is typically characterized by a high-temperature process. 
However, regarding the thermal stability of most of the metal oxides, 
proceeding under such a high-temperature operating condition may 
result in material sintering and low thermochemical performance. For 
example, the thermal reduction temperature of the raw material can be 
limited to Tred = 1200 ◦C to overcome these issues. Then, oxidation of 
MOred with CO2 could be carried out at the 800–1200 ◦C temperature 
range. 
The CO2-splitting performance of different redox materials including 
CeO2 [22–25], perovskite [26–28], ferrite [29], NiFe2O4 [16–18,30], 
etc. have been extensively studied through a two-step thermochemical 
process. However, the properties of some materials are not ideal due to 
many factors such as sintering resistance, stability, and oxygen release 
capacity. The metal oxide compound Fe2O3/Fe3O4 was long considered 
as a cheap oxide material, which can be applied to large-scale thermo­
chemical energy storage processes [31]. Loutzenhiser [32] has reported 
that a cycle efficiency of 46% can be obtained with Fe2O3/Fe3O4 redox 
cycles coupled to an Air Brayton cycle for thermochemical energy 
storage and electricity production. Bhosale [33] and Gorakshnath et al. 
[34] have thermodynamically demonstrated the Co-ferrite CO2-splitting 
reactivity and reported a maximum of 139.6 μmol/g of CO production 
per cycle. Owing to its good ability to store/release oxygen, CuO/Cu2O 
has been used in the processes of thermochemical energy storage, pho­
tothermal, and electrochemical reduction of carbon dioxide [35–38]. 
Qiu et al. [39] have first reduced the raw oxide material with hydrogen 
before performing CO2 thermochemical reduction. They found that 
Cu0.2Co0.8Fe2O4 presents a high average CO2-splitting rate of ~144.9 
μmol.g−1
. min−1 and CO yield of ~9.0 mmol.g−1 with satisfactory sta­
bility over cyclic redox operating at 650 ◦C. Usually, the materials with 
good thermal stability include alumina [40], zirconia [41], silicon [29], 
silicon carbide [42], and yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) [43] are used as 
carriers to ensure the structural stability of the catalytic materials. 
Selecting appropriate support is of great help to improve the catalyst’s 
thermal stability and CO2-splitting catalytic activity for higher ther­
mochemical performance. The main research focus in the field of solar 
chemistry includes: (i) studying the redox reaction kinetics of materials 
to select the best and most stable redox materials; (ii) synthesis of the 
materials into a porous structure; (iii) design and build a scalable and 
flexible solar reactor prototype able to support different heating con­
cepts and operating modes. Moreover, the experimental performance 
assessment of redox materials under representative conditions in solar 
reactors, which could provide major insights into the performance of 
real-time on-sun reactors is scarce to date. 
In this work, the thermochemical redox kinetics and thermochemical 
properties of CuO, NiFe2O4, ZrO2, and Al2O3 were studied in detail. The 
morphology of the material was characterized by SEM and EDS. The 
thermochemical properties were verified by thermogravimetric analysis 
using pure CeO2 as reference. ZrO2 and Al2O3 supported catalysts were 
also used for the thermogravimetric analysis to simulate the real 
catalyst-support structure. The crystal structure was characterized by X- 
ray diffraction. In addition, the influencing factors in terms of effects of 
reduction temperature, heating rate, and CO2 entry stage on the per­
formance of the thermochemical cycles were studied. To obtain the best- 
mixed CuO and NiFe2O4 oxide catalyst systems, the ratio of the selected 
materials was optimized to obtain the most appropriate material. The 
resulted material’s CO2 reduction performance was investigated 
through quantum chemistry calculations. This work provides new in­
sights useful for the theoretical research and application of solar ther­
mochemical CO2 conversion into fuels. 
2. Experimental proceeding 
2.1. Material characterization 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded via the X’PERT PRO 
X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. The power of the ceramic X- 
ray tube was set at 2.2 kW. The material characterization test was car­
ried out at temperatures varied from room temperature to 1200 ◦C. The 
changes in the diffraction angle (2θ) from 10◦to 100◦were regulated 
with a step size of 0.02◦and a scan time of 0.2 s per step. The phase 
structures, chemical compositions, and lattice parameters of these 
powders were obtained using HighScore Plus software. Identification of 
different crystalline phases was conducted by comparing experimental 
data with the standards database. The surface morphologies of the 
samples were obtained by field emission scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM, ZEISS, and TESCAN), and the elemental compositions and con­
tents were determined by using the large window energy spectrum de­
tector (EDS, Oxford) attached to the SEM. The characterization of the 
investigated materials was developed to provide a comprehensive 
background of the physicochemical principles underlying the thermo­
chemical effect in high-temperature gas-solid CO2 catalytic conversion. 
2.2. CO2 thermochemical reduction principles 
High purity 99.999% nano-raw oxide materials including CuO, 
NiFe2O4, Al2O3, ZrO2, CeO2, and SiC were all purchased from Aladdin 
for the analysis of CO2 conversion and active materials thermal catalytic 
activity over the reaction temperature changes. The thermochemical 
properties of the samples were tested by a thermogravimetric analyzer 
(TGA, SETARAM SETSYS EVO16/18). The system adopts the upper 
balance and suspended sensor design, which are put into the furnace by 
moving the suspension test bracket up and down. Two crucibles were 
placed on both sides of the bracket, the left side is used for blank control, 
and the right side is used for the sample testing. The sensor adopts a 
plug-and-play interface, which is convenient for users to replace them­
selves. The heating furnace is equipped with a water-cooling system, and 
the performance is extremely stable. In terms of thermogravimetry, 
SETARAM’s unique photoelectric balance technology is used to provide 
ultra-high-test accuracy and meantime overcome the inherent stability 
problems of thermogravimetric systems that usually use electronic bal­
ances. The system can be stable for a long time without additional water 
bath protection and preheating. The device uses an S-type thermocouple 
with the highest temperature measurement of 1600 ◦C. The device has 
two carrier gases and one protective gas. The carrier gas flows into the 
furnace from top to bottom to ensure complete contact and reaction with 
the sample. Each furnace carrier gas has a flow controller to measure the 
carrier gas flow rate. In this study, the inert gas Ar is used as the pro­
tective gas. Approximately 10 mg of sample was placed in the crucible 
and put into the furnace through the lifting device. Purging with high-

the oxygen-deficient oxide material obtained from the first step for the 
formation of CO. To assess the material oxygen exchange capacity and 
CO2 reduction performance, we subtracted the test weight of the empty 
crucible from the test weight of the sample. The total amount of CO2 
consumption in a single cycle is 70 mmol, which is calculated consid­
ering the ideal gas state equation at 20 ◦C and atmospheric pressure. 
This value is used to calculate the conversion efficiency of CO2. The set 
of temperatures, heating rate, and cooling has been verified to gain the 
optimal operating parameters for the subsequent tests. The yield of O2 
release and CO formation through the weight change of the active 
sample is calculated based on the two-step thermochemical redox cycles.

2.3. Computational methods 
The material that exhibited the highest performance was considered 
for further analysis via quantum chemistry calculation. A powerful 
comprehensive software package based on the Gaussian is utilized for 
quantum chemistry. The numerical model is developed considering the 
Gaussian functions, thermodynamic properties, and the reaction path 
which could guarantee the accuracy of the finding and provide 
comprehensive insight into the physicochemical changes and thermo­
chemical CO2 conversion effectiveness. The calculation can be per­
formed on the ground state or excited state of the system. In this study, 
the Gaussian 09 program was used to optimize the geometric structure at 
the PBE0-D3/DEF2-SVP level, and the single point energy was further 
calculated at the PBE0-D3/DEF2-TZVPP level to accurately calculate the 
energy change during the reaction. MultiWFN 3.7 program was used to 
perform the wave function analysis for obtaining the electron density 
difference diagrams of interactions [49]. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Material characterization 
Through the TG experimental research in section 3.2, the material 
composite of 25%CuO and 75%NiFe2O4 (CNF-13) exhibited excellent 
redox properties. In this section, the X-ray diffraction pattern of CNF-13 
nanocomposite catalysts before and after being heated at high temper­
atures is introduced as shown in Fig. 1. A high purity phase composition 
was obtained with the fresh CNF-13. The peaks of each material were 
determined by comparing them with the standard card (PDF and 
JCPDS). The corresponding standard spectral map of fresh samples in 
JADE6 software was CuO (#89–5899) and NiFe2O4 (#86–2267). XRD 
pattern of the CNF-13 nanocomposite oxide material heated to 1300 ◦C 
in the Ar atmosphere indicated the peaks of Fe0.64Ni0.36, Cu, and FeO 
that could be regarded as the active components of CuO and NiFe2O4 at 
high temperatures. Compared to the fresh samples, after heating the 
sample corresponds to the standard spectrum map Fe0.64Ni0.36 (JCPDS 
00-047-1405), Cu (JCPDS 03–065-9743), and FeO (JCPDS 01–075- 
1550). The observed phase transition could be the key enhancing factor 
of the CNF-13 thermochemical CO2-splitting performance. By changing 
the working fluid, new material phases including CuFe2O4 (#77–0010), 
Cu (JCPDS 03–065-9743), Ni (#04–0850), and (Cu0.2Ni0.8)O 
(#25–1049) were identified as main products when CNF-13 is heated to 
1000 ◦C in the CO2 atmosphere. The synergistic effect of Fe–Ni bime­
tallic at the interface of NiO-Fe2O3 mixed oxide could result in signifi­
cant H2 and CO production capacity compared to pure Fe2O3 [44].

into corresponding oxides, elements, and alloy under a high- 
temperature inert (Ar) atmosphere. However, a relatively complex re­

of CNF-13 is carried under the CO2 atmosphere. The CNF-13 composite

and EDS is shown in Fig. 2. Considering the microstructural analysis of

3.2. The thermochemical cycle performance 
3.2.1. Selectivity based on material oxygen release and catalyst activity 
The thermochemical redox cycle characteristics and reactivity of the 
active samples were investigated by TGA. NiFe2O4, which is a commonly 
used thermochemical material, was tested under the same conditions. 
The samples with compositions of (CuO)0.25(NiFe2O4)0.75, (CuO)0.5(Ni­
Fe2O4)0.5, (CuO)0.75(NiFe2O4)0.25, (CuO)0.125(NiFe2O4)0.375(ZrO2)0.5, 
(CuO)0.5(ZrO2)0.5, 
(CuO)0.5(Al2O3)0.5, 
(NiFe2O4)0.4(ZrO2)0.6, 
(NiFe2O4)0.2(ZrO2)0.8 and (NiFe2O4)0.1(ZrO2)0.9 were abbreviated as 
CNF-13, CNF-11, CNF-31, CNFZ-134, CZ-11, CA-11, NFZ-23, NFZ-14, 
and NFZ-19, respectively. The weight changes and redox performance 
analysis of different ratios of NiFe2O4, CuO, and other materials are 
depicted in Fig. 3. The fast weight loss for all the samples containing 
CuO at 800–900 ◦C could be attributed to the rapid thermal decompo­
sition of CuO. The good catalyst stability of NiFe2O4 and the higher 
oxygen release ability of CuO were considered for the synthesis of mixed 
composite oxides to obtain a functional material with both advantages. 
The oxide material composition and design strategy were performed 
with the aid of the theory of thermodynamics. Moreover, CeO2 was used 
as reference material to assess the performance of the materials tested. 
Higher oxygen release was obtained with CuO and NiFe2O4 compared to 
CeO2. This proves that both materials are more conducive to high- 
temperature thermal reduction. As shown in Fig. 3(b) the weightless­
ness without oxygen release at high temperatures may be due to Cu2O 
melting. The introduction of CO2 induces an increase in the sample 
weight. As shown in Table 1, the weight gain of CuO-containing mate­
rials was higher than that of NiFe2O4 and CeO2. The yields of CO across 
CZ and CA media decreased from 386.3 to 126.5 μmol/g and 438.6 to 
123.3 μmol/g, respectively. The change observed could be associated 
with the cycle stability of the two samples. As for NFZ materials, the 
higher content of nickel ferrite resulted in a higher CO yield in the 
second cycle. The pure NiFe2O4 exhibited the best oxygen exchange 
stability among the oxide materials investigated. A close yield of CO was 
obtained with CNF-13 and CNF-31 in the first cycle compared to the 
second cycle where the reactivity of CNF-13 exhibited a CO yield of two 
times higher than CNF-31. Considering the performance of various 
materials investigated, CNF-13 exhibited the highest stability and oxy­
gen release capability. A CO2 conversion rate of 5.11% and 3.36% was 
obtained in the first and second cycles by using 13.08 mg of CNF-13 
sample weight. 
3.2.2. Influence of reduction temperature 
The effect of the thermal reduction temperatures on the thermo­
chemical redox cycling performance of CNF-13 is further investigated by 
varying the reduction temperature from 1000 ◦C to 1300 ◦C at a 20 ◦C/ 
min heating rate while maintaining the oxidation temperature at 800 ◦C. 
The yield of O2 and CO calculated through the weight changes of the 
sample are shown in Fig. 4. A stable O2 release of 800 μmol/g was ob­
tained at 800 ◦C in the first thermochemical cycle. However, the weight 
of the sample keeps decreasing at temperatures exceeding 1100 ◦C. As 
shown in Table 2, the CNF-13 sample is more sensitive to a high- 
temperature thermal process since the weight loss of the active mate­
rial increases as the reduction temperature increases. Also, increasing 
CO yield was observed with the increase in the reduction temperature. 
As for O2 release, the higher the reduction temperature, the higher the 
O2 release can be observed. Thus, a higher reduction temperature could 
induce higher oxygen exchange in the reaction medium. Besides, a 
relatively stable weight change resulting in a CO/O2 ratio close to the 
theoretical value of 2 was obtained at 1000 ◦C reduction temperature. 
This indicates the redox cycle of CNF-13 can be carried in 800–1000 ◦C 
range to ensure better cyclic stability. However, NiFe2O4 may not 
release oxygen completely under 1000 ◦C reaction temperature due to 
the low reduction. Therefore, 1300 ◦C was selected as an appropriate 
reduction temperature for the subsequent test. As depicted in Fig. 5, the 
size of particle agglomeration gradually increases with the increase in 
the reduction temperature. A large number of nanoparticles were 
observed when the reduction temperature was limited to 1000 ◦C and 
1100 ◦C. However, a large number of materials were aggregated into 
micron mass at 1200 ◦C and 1300 ◦C reaction temperatures. By 
considering the EDS analysis, CNF-13 exhibited different phases with 
the increase in the reduction temperature. A very similar material 
composition close to the initial material ratio was observed at 1100 ◦C 
and 1000 ◦C reduction temperatures. At 1200 ◦C, the higher

20 ◦C/min heating rates. The heating rates of 10 ◦C/min and 20 ◦C/min 
exhibited similar yields in the second cycle. We believe that the re- 
oxidation yield could be maintained at a higher level by processing at 
a lower heating rate. Through EDS analysis, we found that Cu element 
on the surface of the material was less than 0.5% atomic ratio, which is 
about 1% of O. This shows that Cu infiltrated into the material and 
cannot be fully analyzed. The heating rate also affects the material 
composition since the proportion of Fe element decreases slightly while 
the proportion of Ni and Cu elements increases with the increase in the 
heating rate. (See Fig. 7.)

3.3. Experimental test of CO2 thermochemical cyclic conversion process 
The experimental test process with a solar thermochemical reactor 
heated by high-flux solar irradiation is shown in Fig. 11. The prototype 
reactor model refers to the study in reference [17, 18, 47]. The power of 
the solar energy input is continuously adjusted as the temperature rises. 
A high-purity Ar is used to purge the air in the reactor before the 
experiment starts. The inlet flow rate of Ar gas was set at 200 sccm via a 
gas mass flow controller (Sierra C100L). At the CO2 reduction stage, the 
flow rate of Ar and CO2 gas was 100 sccm, respectively. The gas flow 
entering the reactor’s cavity passes through the light-receiving side. The 
thermochemical reaction occurs in the porous material filled in the 
reactor’s cavity. The product gases exhaust the reactor from the rear 
where the reactor’s outlet is located. The exhaust gas through the rear is 
connected directly to the gas chromatograph (Scion 456C) for the 
analysis of product selectivity every 12 min. The front and back of the 
reactor were equipped with a water-cooling system to ensure the cooling 
of the quartz window and the gas exhausting the reactor’s outlet. S-type 
and K-type thermocouples were set in the middle of the reactor to 
monitor the operating temperature of the reactor chamber filled with 
porous oxide catalyst and the temperature of the insulation layer, 
respectively. A porous oxide material having 60 mm thickness, 60 mm 
diameter, and 10ppi is filled in the reactor chamber. The temperature 
measuring point is set at 20 mm from the surface receiving light at the 
front section. The structure of the SiC porous support and the coated 
porous material are shown in Fig. 12. 50 g of SiC substrate was firstly 
prepared by sintering the porous polyurethane foam template before 
coating it with 4 g of CNF-13 using anhydrous ethanol.

4. Conclusion 
In this study, the solar thermochemical CO2-splitting characteristics 
through the synergistic thermal catalytic activity of CuO and NiFe2O4 
and the effect of SiC photothermal properties were investigated. The 
thermochemical CO2-splitting characteristics of different ratios of CuO 
and NiFe2O4 were experimentally investigated to explore efficient 
mixing ratios and obtain practical thermochemical CO2 catalytic con­
version materials. The material characterization methods including 
XRD, SEM, and EDS were used to provide comprehensive insight into the 
crystal structure, surface morphology, and chemical composition 
changes for a given reaction temperature. Moreover, quantum chemical 
calculations were conducted to identify the most significant CO2-split­
ting catalysts. Compared with the most investigated NiFe2O4, the yield 
of CO and the thermochemical cycle stability could increase by 
exploiting the synergistic thermal catalytic activity of CuO and NiFe2O4. 
An average CO production of 229 μmol/g was obtained through the 
optimal ratio of materials consisting of 25%CuO and 75%NiFe2O4 (CNF- 
13). The heating rate of 20 ◦C/min was the most appropriate heating 
rate resulting in the conservation of the thermochemical properties of 
CNF-13 with great catalyst activity towards high product selectivity. The 
effect of reduction and oxidation temperature along with CO2 entry- 
stage are the main factors for improving the thermochemical CO2 
reduction performance. Through quantum chemical calculations, it was 
observed that the active components (Cu2O and Fe3O4) of CNF-13 can 
reduce the energy required for CO2 reduction. During the thermo­
chemical cyclic process, the highest instantaneous CO yield was 
10.98mL ⋅ min-1gCNF-13
−1
. The thermochemical CO2 reduction across CNF- 
13@SiC exhibited a total amount of 3055 mL of CO production with an 
average thermochemical energy storage power of 0.16 kJ/min approx­
imately. The average CO2 conversion rate of direct CO2-to-CO over three 
cycles was 23.3% with single-cycle conversion rates of 33.9%, 23.7%, 
and 12.1%, respectively. The contribution of SiC photothermal proper­
ties to the direct CO2-to-CO conversion rate was about half, which be­
comes higher with the increase in the cycle times. After the 
thermochemical cycling redox process, the catalyst load decreased 
significantly resulting in a low catalyst content and uneven catalyst 
distribution on the spent material. This study provides valuable insight 
into the thermochemical CO2 catalytic conversion and raises new critical 
issues for further studies on the research development on CO2 capture 
and utilization.
