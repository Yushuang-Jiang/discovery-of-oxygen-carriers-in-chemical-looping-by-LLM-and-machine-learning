A B S T R A C T   
The development of oxygen carrier particles that can be prepared on an industrial scale with good cycling sta­
bility remains a challenge in the field of chemical looping. In this work, a perovskite oxygen carrier (CaMn0.5­
Ti0.375Fe0.125O3-δ) was prepared by an industrial-scale spray drying granulation and tested on a fluidized bed 
thermogravimetric analyzer for long-term chemical looping combustion. The results showed that the attrition of 
oxygen carrier particles originated from irregular parts of the fresh particle surface early in the cycle, and the 
chemical stress on particles gradually increased with the number of redox cycles, increasing the attrition and 
sintering of the particles. In addition, as the cycle proceeds, the perovskite structure was destroyed and the 
oxygen carrier reacted chemically with the quartz tube wall and the air distribution plate leading to bonding. The 
deep redox cycling reactions indicated that agglomeration of particles occurred mainly in the reduction stage, 
with the bed entering repeated periods of defluidization during the 37th-43rd and 47th-55th cycles due to the 
changes in particle bonding and bed pressure. Fixed bed experiment showed that particle activation came from 
an increase in the active area of the particles during the cycle, independent of whether they were fluidized or not. 
In addition, agglomeration started at the contact surface between the air distribution plate and the particles, 
resulting in blocked oxidation of the particles and deepening agglomeration due to excessive reduction of the 
particles.   
1. Introduction 
Chemical looping combustion (CLC) technology, a key component of 
the second generation of carbon capture technology, has the benefit of 
low cost and low energy consumption to collect CO2 [1], which is pro­
jected to break the bottleneck of high cost and energy consumption. 
Chemical looping combustion typically divides the fuel conversion into 
two stages, which take place in two reactors [2]. Conventional com­
bustion technology burns fuel and air directly to generate heat energy, 
and the resultant CO2 is diluted by huge amounts of N2. The basic 
components of the chemical looping combustion system are an air 
reactor (AR) and a fuel reactor (FR), and the oxygen is transported via an 
oxygen carrier (OC) [3]. The reduced oxygen carrier (MexOy-1) is 
oxidized by air in the air reactor, producing heat in the process [4]. At 
the fuel reactor’s exit, the CO2 is not diluted by the N2, and the H2O is 
condensed to produce pure CO2, which can be compressed, transported, 
used as a resource, etc. [5]. CLC hence can automatically enrich CO2 
throughout the combustion process without using additional energy [6]. 
The oxygen carrier is the medium to realize the chemical looping 
conversion of the fuel, and the reactor system is the significant location 
for the oxidation and reduction reactions of the oxygen carrier, heat 
release, and fuel conversion. These are the key technologies of chemical 
looping combustion: Good thermal qualities, quick kinetic properties, 
long cycle stability, suitable mechanical properties, good fluidization 
properties, and outstanding economic and environmental performance, 
which are all necessary for appropriate oxygen carriers [7]. Researchers 
have created a variety of bimetallic and polymetallic composite oxides 
as oxygen carriers to increase reaction performance, mechanical

Perovskites are ceramic oxides with the general molecular formula 
ABX3-δ, where A is for rare earth and alkaline earth metals with large 
atomic radii (La, Ca, Sr, etc.) and B is for transition metals with small 
atomic radii (Cu, Mn, Ni, Cr, etc.) [14,15]. For oxygen carriers, X is the O 
element, which refers to the oxygen ion vacancy [16]. The ideal struc­
ture of perovskite shows cubic symmetry with a skeleton structure ar­
ranged in a point-sharing manner [17]. Perovskite materials can 
selectively obtain physical and chemical properties such as specific ox­
ygen ion transport properties, catalytic properties, and structural prop­
erties by doping at A and B sites, and have been widely used in 
electrochemistry, non-homogeneous catalysis, oxygen separation 
membranes, electronic devices, and other fields [18]. Due to their good 
lattice structure and chemical stability, perovskites have been used as 
oxygen carriers for chemical looping combustion in recent years [19]. 
The A and B sites of the majority of perovskite include rare gold, La [11], 
Sr [20], Co [21], Ni [22], or other precious metals, which negatively 
affect their economic and environmental performance. Research has 
recently begun to concentrate on perovskite oxygen carriers with low 
costs potential, primarily based on CaMnO3-δ-based materials, which 
have high reactivity and oxygen decoupling ability, which are beneficial 
to enhance the reducing gas conversion, but need to be adjusted for the 
oxygen transport properties and mechanical stability of CaMnO3-δ-based 
materials when used directly in chemical looping combustion [23]. The 
thermodynamic characteristics, oxidation–reduction properties, and 
structural stability of CaMnO3-δ can all be greatly enhanced by the 
doping of additional elements like La, Sr, Cu, Ti, Mg, etc. at the A- or B- 
site [24]. Therefore, a variety of CaMnO3-δ-based perovskite materials 
have been formed for chemical looping combustion, such as 
Ca0.8Sr0.2MnO3-δ [25], Ca0.9Mn0.5Ti0.5O3-δ [26], CaMn0.9Mg0.1O3-δ [27] 
and CaMn0.775Ti0.125Mg0.1O3-δ [28]. However, only three perovskites, 
CaMn0.875Ti0.125O3-δ, CaMn0.9Mg0.1O3-δ, and CaMn0.775Ti0.125Mg0.1O3-δ, 
have undergone continuous assessment in the chemical looping com­
bustion reactor system. In our previous study, CaMn0.5Ti0.375Fe0.125O3-δ 
perovskite was developed to improve stability by adding Ti and reac­
tivity by adding Fe [29]. This perovskite has faster oxygen uncoupling 
and redox kinetic properties compared to Cu- and Mn-based oxygen 
carriers and other reported perovskite. Currently, most of the studies on 
synthetic OCs have been prepared at laboratory scale, and few large- 
scale fabrications have been reported. However, when it comes to CLC 
demonstration and industrial applications, scale-up remains a huge 
challenge to be solved. 
The most common procedures used to prepare perovskite oxygen 
carriers are sol–gel, chemical co-precipitation, mechanical mixing, and 
spray drying [30]. Among them, the sol–gel and co-precipitation 
methods are suitable for synthesizing micro-powder oxygen carriers 
[5,31], which are typically prepared at the gram level with particle size 
less than 10 μm or nano-materials and are significantly different from 
the actual fluidized bed applications. The prepared oxygen carriers are 
primarily used to study reaction mechanisms. The mechanical mixing 
approach, on the other hand, frequently results in an uneven distribu­
tion of the active ingredients [32], while the freeze-drying process is 
laborious and expensive to make [33]. In the food, pharmaceutical, and 
chemical sectors, spray drying is a widely used drying or granulation 
technique, primarily for the manufacturing of particles with particle 
sizes of tens of microns, such as fluid catalytic cracking catalysts for the 
petroleum industry [34]. Due to its capacity to continually produce 
particles with consistent homogeneity and sphericity at a reduced cost, 
the spray drying process is better ideal for producing perovskite particles 
on a wide scale.

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
Commercial Mn3O4 was used as the manganese (Mn) source and was 
supplied by Hunan Special Metal Material Factory of Sinosteel Tianyuan 
Group, commercial titanium dioxide (TiO2) was used as the titanium 
(Ti) source and was supplied by Jinan Yuxing Chemical Co. Zibo Qilu 
Ethylene Chemical Co., Ltd. provided the Ca(OH)2 for this experiment. 
The Xinhua Branch of Shijiazhuang Changli Mineral Products Co. sup­
plied the commercial Fe2O3. Table 1 displays the findings of the X-Ray 
Fluorescence (XRF) investigation of the aforementioned four raw 
materials. 
Fig. 1 displays the four basic materials’ particle size distributions. Ca 
(OH)2 has an average particle size (d50) of 3.18 μm, Mn3O4 has an 
average particle size (d50) of 2.83 μm, TiO2 has an average particle size 
(d50) of 0.99 μm, and Fe2O3 has an average particle size (d50) of 4.44 μm. 
The combined maximum particle size of all raw materials is 20 μm. 
When the raw ingredients are physically fine enough, the granulation 
process may accomplish homogeneous mixing of the components. 
2.2. Preparation process 
In this experiment, hundred-micron-scale CaMn0.5Ti0.375Fe0.125O3-δ 
perovskite oxygen carrier particles were prepared by spray granulation 
method, and the main process included three steps. The flow schematic 
is shown in Fig. 2. 
2.2.1. Slurry preparation 
Firstly, a solid powder mixture consisting of commercial Ca(OH)2, 
Mn3O4, TiO2, and Fe2O3 was prepared according to the chemical 
equivalence ratio of Ca, Mn, Ti, and Fe in CaMn0.5Ti0.375Fe0.125O3-δ and 
the prepared solid mixture was placed in a mixer with a propeller blade. 
A large amount of water was added to the mixer to maintain a solid to 
water ratio of 2:1. After two hours of continuous stirring, a binder was 
added to increase the viscosity of the suspension and then stirred for 
another hour. A small amount of defoamer was used in order to elimi­
nate the air bubbles generated during the mixing process. A small 
amount of dispersant and lubricant was then used to improve the 
rheology and flow of the suspension. The total mass fraction of binder, 
defoamer, dispersant and lubricant was 1.20 wt%. Finally, the slurry 
was then continuously stirred for 1 h and the final slurry was obtained 
for the production of particles. 
2.2.2. Spray granulation 
Spray granulation is carried out on a pressure spray drying granu­
lation plant with a maximum evaporation capacity of ~ 2800 kg/h. 
First, the spray drying tower is heated up to 180℃, and the vacuum 
inside the tower is maintained at ~ -80 Pa under the action of the tail 
fan; then, the prepared slurry is pumped to the top of the spray granu­
lation tower using a pressurized liquid transfer pump with a pumping 
pressure of 3.5 MPa; second, the slurry is ejected through a single fluid 
high-pressure nozzle with a vertical downward opening and is atomized 
into small droplets, which move downward by gravity and hot air, and 
as the droplets descend, the water is rapidly evaporated, producing 
spherical particles; finally, the dried spherical particles fall into the 
collector located at the bottom of the spray tower.

2.3. Fluidized bed thermogravimetric analyzer 
The FB-TGA was utilized to analyze the OC’s redox properties. As 
shown in Fig. 4, the FB-TGA equipment used in the study has three main 
parts: a bubbling fluidized bed reactor, a fast mass measurement, and 
sensing unit, and an inlet gas management system. The bubbling fluid­
ized bed reactor is a straight quartz tube with a 25 mm inner diameter 
and a height of 200 mm, and a porous plate at the bottom of the reactor 
for feeding OC particles. The temperature inside the reactor is controlled 
and measured by a K-type thermocouple. The mass of the reactor and 
bed material is continuously measured by a high-precision electronic 
balance with a 0.1 mg measuring accuracy. The input gas flow is accu­
rately controlled using a calibrated mass flow meter.

Define the mass loss and increment during reduction and oxidation 
in each redox cycle as Δmr, n and Δmo, n, respectively.

The net mass lost for the nth redox cycle is defined as Δmn, which is

3. Discussion 
3.1. Redox cycles behavior of CMTF 
The cycling characteristics of the CMTF were tested using FB-TGA for 
180 redox processes at a reaction temperature of 900 ◦C. It can be seen 
that the mass curve in the reactor tends to decrease rapidly at the 
beginning of the redox cycle, with the attrition rate of particles rapidly 
decreasing from 0.96 %/h to 0.24 %/h during the 0th-45th cycle and 
tending to 0 with the gradual increase in the number of cycles. This is 
due to the presence of some smaller particles in the fresh OC, which were 
easily blown out of the reaction gas at the beginning of the fluidization 
leading to a decrease in the mass in the bed. In addition, the surfaces of 
some particles were irregular, which led to a high attrition rate of par­
ticles at the beginning of fluidization according to the rounding effect of 
irregular particles at the beginning of the cycle [35]. At the same time, 
there was a tendency for the mass fluctuation of individual cycles to 
increase during 0-60th cycles, which indicates that there was an acti­
vation of OC at this stage and the redox properties of OC gradually in­
crease. Fig. 5(b) shows the mass change curve of a typical redox cycle, 
and it can be seen that OC was reduced by H2 in the reduction stage with 
decreasing mass, and OC was oxidized by O2 in the oxidation stage with 
increasing mass. It is noteworthy that OC was completely oxidized 
before the end of the oxidation stage, indicating that the oxidation rate 
was faster, about twice the reduction rate, and the oxygen transfer ca­
pacity (ROC) of OC was 1.4% at this time. In addition, a decrease in the 
mass of OC was observed in the purging phase at the end of the oxidation 
stage, indicating that the CMTF perovskite oxygen carrier possesses 
oxygen uncoupling ability, and the amount of uncoupling at this time 
was 0.2%. 
The mass signal of OC during the whole cycle was not affected by the 
switching between different fluidizing gases, indicating that the re­
quirements of the experimental operation were met. Fig. 5(c) shows the 
mass variation curves in the reactor for different cycle numbers. It is 
obvious that the mass change curves of OC were approximately the same 
as the number of cycles increases, indicating that OC still maintains good 
reaction performance after many redox cycles over a long period. 
The crystalline phase composition of CMTF perovskite OC was 
analyzed by X-Ray diffraction (XRD), and the results are shown in Fig. 6. 
It can be seen that the crystalline phase composition of fresh CMTF is 
CaMnO2.56 (29.6 wt%) and CaFeTi2O6 (70.4 wt%), and the crystalline 
phase structure of CMTF becomes three types after a long cycle of 
oxidation and reduction reactions, in which the same components of 
oxidized and reduced perovskite are CaMnO2.56 and CaFeTi2O6, with the 
difference that oxidized perovskite contains CaMnO3 phase, while 
reduced perovskite contains CaTiO3 phase. In addition, the XRD peak 
positions of the reacted perovskite were slightly shifted to a lower 2θ 
position compared with those before the reaction, indicating that the 
crystal structure of OC was slightly changed, which shifted the OC 
diffraction peaks.

3.2. Deep redox cycles performance of CMTF 
To further test the cycling performance and sintering resistance of 
CMTF, FB-TGA was used to investigate the effect of deep oxida­
tion–reduction cycling of CMTF on its crystal structure. Firstly, the 
maximum oxygen transfer capacity of CMTF was tested by continuously 
passing 1.1 L/min of N2 and 100 mL/min of H2 into the reactor to keep 
the OC particles in a bubbling fluidized state, and the mass change in the 
reactor is shown in Fig. 12. It is clear that the mass change of CMTF 
plateaued when the reaction proceeded to 10 min, at which point the 
maximum oxygen transfer capacity of CMTF was 4.75%. 
Based on this, the cycling performance of OC in the deep redox cycle 
reaction was further tested. Fig. 13 shows the change in mass within the 
reactor over all 80 cycles and it can be seen that there was a slight 
decrease in mass between the 0 and 20th cycles due to the attrition of OC 
particles during the early cycles. At this stage the particle attrition rate 
rapidly decreased from 15.1 %/h to about 0 over the process of 20 cy­
cles. However, after 20 cycles there was a gradual increase in mass 
within the reactor, indicating a gradual increase in agglomeration 
within the bed, with some particles beginning to agglomerate on the air 
distribution plate and quartz tube walls. During the 37th −
43rd cycle, 
there was a significant drop and rapid increase in the mass in the bed, 
partly because the agglomerates that accumulated initially were less 
viscous and could be easily blown away in the presence of increased 
pressure in the bed. On the other hand, as the cycle progressed, the 
chemical stress on the OC particles gradually increased and some of the 
particles fractured, leading to the destruction of some agglomerates, 
resulting in a decrease and increase in the mass signal. In addition, it is 
worth noting that the drop in weight in the oxidation stage precedes the 
reduction stage, indicating that agglomeration of the particles occurred 
mainly in the reduction stage[36], so when agglomeration occurred the 
O2 in the oxidation stage was unable to oxide the upper particles from

severe sintering and agglomeration compared to Fig. 11, and the ag­

As shown in Fig. 14(d) and (e), the agglomerates were observed 
through microscopy, at which point the OC particles in the agglomerates 
still maintained a relatively intact ellipsoidal structure, indicating that 
the physical and mechanical strength of the CMTF particles was high, 
but the particles were adhering to each other at the tangents. In addi­
tion, cracks of varying degrees were observed on the surfaces of a large 
number of particles, which were due to the accumulation of chemical 
stresses in the OC particles after multiple deep reduction–oxidation cy­
cles over a long period [39], resulting in cracks and fractures on the 
surfaces and within the particles. Therefore, the deep redox cycling 
process, achieved by prolonging the oxidation and reduction times 
during the cycle, will exacerbate the sintering and agglomeration of 
CMTF during the cycle, mainly due to the prolonged reduction process 
resulting in the OC crystal deconstruction and bonding to each other, in 
addition, the deconstructed OC will also react with the quartz tube wall 
and the air distribution plate, resulting in the particles bonding to the 
inner reactor wall surface.

4. Mechanistic analysis 
Fig. 17 shows the sintering and agglomeration mechanism of the 
CMTF oxygen carrier particles during the cycling process. At the early 
stage of cycling, the particles fluidized well and the reactive reaction 
area was increased during the cycling process, and the reaction perfor­
mance of the particles was gradually enhanced. In addition, because 
some of the fresh particles were irregularly ellipsoidal, collisions 
occurred between the particles or between the particles and the reactor 
during the fluidization process, resulting in partial particle attrition. 
With the redox cycle, the perovskite structure of CMTF particles un­
derwent lattice distortion, and some of the free calcium compounds 
reacted with the air distribution plate and quartz tube wall and caused 
some particles to bond, which also made some particles start to 
agglomerate at the bottom of the bed. In addition, the gradual increase 
of chemical stress inside the particles led to cracks or fractures in some 
particles. Due to the gradual increase of sintering and agglomeration, 
some of the upper particles could not be completely oxidized in the 
oxidation stage resulting in a continuous decrease of the bed weight 
until the gas pressure gradually established gas channels in the 
agglomerated layer, and the upper particles could resume oxidation, at 
which time the bed entered the repeated defluidization period. And with 
the intensification of particle sintering and uneven gas distribution, the 
newly established agglomerate layer became more compact, and the 
agglomerate layer gradually spreads to the upper layer particles and 
leads to complete bed defluidization. 
5. Conclusion 
In this study, the attrition, redox cycling properties and sintering 
characteristics of CMTF perovskite prepared by spray drying granulation 
on an industrial scale were investigated on FB-TGA. The main findings 
are as follows: 
At the beginning of the long-term multiple redox reaction cycles, the 
attrition rate of the particles was high due to the small size and uneven 
shape of some CMTF oxygen carrier particles, based on the rounding 
effect of irregular particles. the XRD results showed that the crystal 
diffraction peaks of CMTF were shifted after the cycling reaction, indi­
cating that the crystal structure of CMTF changed after the cycling. In 
addition, the partial sintering of OC particles during the redox cycling 
increased the adhesion of the particle surface and caused particle 
adhesion due to uneven fluidization at the quartz tube wall and air 
distribution plate during the fluidization process. On the other hand, 
because the CMTF crystal structure was destroyed during the cycling

whole bed was defluidized. Fixed-bed high concentration reaction gas

on whether they are fluidized or not, but comes from the increase of 
active reaction area during the redox cycle. In addition, agglomeration 
of particles starts at the connection of the particles with the air distri­
bution plate. Therefore, longer residence time and higher reaction gas 
concentration are direct factors leading to CMTF agglomeration, while 
higher ROC is the root cause of agglomeration. From the point of view of 
inhibiting CMTF agglomeration and sintering, controlling the ROC of OC 
and reducing the residence time and reaction gas concentration of OC in 
the fuel reactor can prolong the safe operation of CMTF peroxides in 
industry.
