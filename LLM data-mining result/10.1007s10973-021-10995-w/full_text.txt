 Abstract
Alternative energy technologies at their current state cannot fully replace the existing fossil fuel-based energy production 
that has led to a rise in the atmospheric ­CO2 concentration. ­CO2 sequestration is a compelling solution to greatly reduce 
­CO2 emissions from sources like fossil fuel-based power plants. Chemical-looping oxygen uncoupling process releases 
gaseous oxygen for combustion at high temperatures in the fuel reactor. The choice of oxygen carrier material (OCM) is 
pivotal to the chemical-looping process. In this study, citrate gel technique has been used to prepare the OCMs. A total of 5 
(five) perovskites were prepared with varying degree of substitution of general formula ­La0.6A′0.4B′1-yFeyO3 where y = (0.8, 
0.9) including the base ­LaFeO3. Calcined OCMs were characterized with XRD, SEM and BET analysis. OCMs showed an 
increase in surface area after oxygen release in a thermogravimetric experiment. Oxygen evolution rate ranged from 0.0043 
to 0.0521 Kg ­O2/Kg OCM in a single cycle operation.
Keywords  LaFeO3 · Perovskites · Carbon capture · Citrate gel · Oxygen carrier · Oxygen uncoupling
Introduction
It is widely believed in scientific and research community 
that ­CO2, the staple greenhouse gas, is at the heart of climate 
change. Fossil fuels-driven power plants have contributed 
significantly to the increase in atmospheric ­CO2 concentra-
tion in the last century. Coal-fired power plants are currently 
producing 37% of global electricity worldwide [1]. In 2017, 
coal was used to produce 36.53% of total global electric-
ity. International Energy Agency (IEA) reported (2019) that 
coal combustion was responsible for 45% (~ 14.535 billion 
tons) of the world’s ­CO2 emissions in 2017 [2]. It is there-
fore essential to mitigate the impacts of coal use and in the 
recent years there has been a growing global desire to reduce 
carbon emissions and greenhouse gases (GHGs) by using 
the advanced low emission energy technologies, improving 
the efficiency of energy use as well as by increasing the 
use of non-fossil energy sources. One of the solution to a 
low-carbon economy, while meeting the existing demand 
for energy by utilizing fossil fuels, is the carbon capture 
and storage (CCS) scheme. Among the available strategies 
to capture ­CO2: post-combustion, pre-combustion, oxyfuel 
combustion and chemical-looping combustion technology, 
the initial three technologies impose a considerable penalty 
to the overall economics of a power plant. Chemical-looping 
combustion (CLC), on the other hand, offers the possibility 
of combusting fossil fuels while producing a pure stream of 
­CO2 (which is easily sequesterable) without additional cost 
of gas separation [3].

special case of chemical-looping combustion, involves the 
use of oxygen carriers capable of releasing gas-phase oxygen 
at high temperatures. The three-step process of CLOU is

Perovskites are a special type of oxides has applications 
in many areas and have managed to stir a lot of inter-
est among researchers due to its ability to accommodate 
significant oxygen non-stoichiometry, allowing oxidation 
or reduction without phase changes potentially improv-
ing cyclic stability. Perovskite, originated from ­CaTiO3, 
is now a general name for oxides having the structural 
formula ­ABO3 or ­A2BO4. In the ­ABO3 structure, the 
A-site cation is 12-fold coordinated and the B-site cation 
is 6-fold coordinated with oxygen anions [8]. Due to the 
different valence states and radii of the cations A and B, 
oxygen vacancies in combination with slight distortions 
in the crystal structure are created in perovskites. These 
vacancies facilitate the migration of oxygen ions during 
the decomposition and re-oxidation of the material [9]. 
Thus perovskites represent an alternative class of reduc-
ible oxides with potential as partial oxidation catalysts. 
The transition metals serve as both lattice oxygen carriers 
and catalysts for activating hydrocarbons. They usually 
exhibit excellent redox properties, high oxygen mobil-
ity, and thermal stability, good oxygen suppliers, and the 
high selectivity to synthesis gas instead to ­CO2 and ­H2O; 
therefore, they are quite effective for catalytic oxidation 
reactions including hydrogenation, CO oxidation, and 
catalytic combustion [10]. Perovskites exhibit oxygen 
non-stoichiometry and can release substantial amounts of 
oxygen via CLOU [4].

Experimental
Preparation of Oxygen Carriers
The perovskites oxides were prepared by citrate gel method. 
Here, stoichiometric amounts of an aqueous solution of the 
nitrates of the corresponding metals were mixed with a 10% 
of excess in equivalent of an aqueous solution of citric acid. 
The resulting mixture was stirred for 15 min at room temper-
ature and evaporated on a hot plate stirrer at 80 °C until gel 
was formed. Then, it was dried in an oven, slowly increas-
ing the temperature up to 200 °C and maintaining it over-
night. The resulting amorphous citrate precursor powders 
were crushed and sieved to obtain the required particle size. 
Finally, the solids were calcined at 900 °C in a muffle fur-
nace for 6 h. Throughout the paper, we will use the nomen-
clature in Table 1 to refer the oxygen carrier materials-
It is notable that although Cobalt (Co) can be intro-
duced to make perovskites ­(LaFe1-xCoxO3), use of iron can 
give better performance of pervoskites e.g., Lanthanum-
strontium-iron oxide ­(La1-xSrxFeO3) [14]. Dueso et al.[15] 
tested perovskites composites containing 11 – 13% ­Fe2O3 
for hydrogen production via chemical looping and found a 
stable hydrogen yield in cycling experiments. Furthermore, 
it was reported that Aluminum can be used to replace Lan-
thanum some portion [16] as well as Copper can be used 
to replace Cobalt [17] in perovskites structure. Hence, this 
study intended to evaluate the perovskites structure by 
doping Al in replacement of Sr and doping Fe in replace-
ment of Co.
Characterization of Oxygen Carriers
The morphologies of the prepared oxygen carriers were 
imaged by high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (HR-
SEM) using a Thermo Fisher FEI scanning electron micro-
scope operated at 15–20 kV. All samples were sputter-coated 
with platinum prior to imaging. Powder X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) analysis (PANalytical, Aeris) was conducted to deter-
mine the crystalline phases in the synthesized oxygen carriers. 
The diffraction patterns were acquired within the range of 2θ 
= 10°−70° with a step size of 0.015° per second using Cu Kα 
radiation and a power of 40 kV × 30 mA.
Nitrogen ­(N2) gas adsorption-desorption analysis was car-
ried out using Belsorp mini II (BEL Japan Inc) at a constant 
temperature (−196 °C) maintained by liquid nitrogen as cool-
ant. About 65 mg of each of the samples was used for the 
adsorption measurement. Prior to measurements, the powdery 
samples were pre-treated for 3 hours at 150 °C by purging 
with flow of dry, pure N2 gas by Belprep flow II to remove any 
surface adsorbed water vapor or other gas molecules. N2 gas 
(cross-sectional area of N2 molecule = 13.5 Å2) was used as 
the adsorbent for studying the adsorption-desorption behavior 
at relative pressures, P/P0, ranging from 0.01 to 0.99 on the 
surface of the solid samples, in order to determine surface 
area and pore size distribution. Helium (He) gas was used to 
measure the dead volume of the sample cells. The specific 
surface area and total pore volume of the porous samples were 
calculated using the Brunauer-Emmet-Teller (BET) equation. 
The BET-specific surface area was evaluated using adsorp-
tion data at relative pressures, P/P0, ranging from 0.1 to 0.2. 
The total pore volume was estimated from the amount of N2 
adsorbed at a relative pressure of about 0.99.
Oxygen Uncoupling Capacity
To determine the oxygen uncoupling capacity, i.e., the amount 
of lattice oxygen released in an inert atmosphere, decomposi-
tion of the perovskite oxygen carriers was carried out in a ­N2 
atmosphere in a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA-50, Shi-
madzu, Japan). In a typical experiment, approximately 5 mg of 
the oxygen carrier material was heated from room temperature 
to 400 °C at a rate of 10  °C ­min−1 under a flow of ­N2 of 10 
mL ­min−1 and held at 400 °C for 15 min to remove moisture. 
Thermodynamic calculations showed that the partial pressure 
of ­O2 is negligible at 400 °C. Subsequently, the temperature 
was increased to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C ­min−1 in a flow of ­N2 
(10 mL ­min−1). Oxygen carriers release oxygen according to 
various decomposition reactions and oxygen-carrying capacity

Results and Discussions
Morphological Investigation of the Materials
Prepared materials were scanned under electron micro-
scope to obtain morphological information. From the 
SEM images in Fig. 2, it was found that the perovskites, in 
general, showed laminar-type crystal structure with open 
pore-type surface texture. The white spots are inferred to 
be the crystal cross and overlap. Addition of doping mate-
rials like Cu/Co did not greatly alter the generic morpho-
logical characteristics of LFO. Pore structures are irregular 
and contain sharp corners and ridges. However, Al-doped 
LACUF-6419 (e) and Co-doped LSCOF-6428 (c) showed 
signs of particle agglomeration.

a real fuel reactor. The materials are supposed to with-

without significant wear and tear. However, still the TGA 
environment is suitable for initial screening of materials 
by imitating near-real test conditions. Figure 5 shows the 
normalized mass chage (i.e., oxygen release behaviour)

Oxygen Uncoupling Capacity
The oxygen uncoupling capacity of the materials is calcu-
lated from TGA data using mass change in solids. Before 
each analysis, initial mass of the sample was measured. 
After the decomposition reaction in TGA, mass of the 
sample was weighed again, and ­N2 correction was made. 
After oxygen uncoupling, each sample was subjected to 
BET analysis to investigate change in specific surface area 
of the oxygen carriers.
As listed in Table 4, LFO has the highest oxygen uncou-
pling capacity among the prepared materials. Stoichio-
metric calculations indicate a maximum oxygen-carrying 
capacity of 19.77% by mass, while experimental values 
show a maximum of 5.38% oxygen uncoupling capacity in 
pure LFO perovskite. The lowest uncoupling rate that was 
found is 0.43% in case of LACUF-6428. All of the perovs-
kites showed an increase in surface area after decomposi-
tion. Although there was no precise relationship between 
initial surface area and oxygen release rate, the fact that 
the decomposition increased the surface area indicates the 
development of more porous and oxygen-deficient perovs-
kites after the initial decomposition reaction. Final surface 
area after TGA test ranged from 5.5 to 10.3 ­m2g−1 indicat-
ing slight increase. However, detailed study is required to 
find out the synergy and exact reasoning between doping 
material and oxygen carrying capacity, which is beyond 
the scope of this paper.

Conclusions
CLOU is a promising technology that eliminates some of 
the limitations of CLC and has excellent provision for solid 
fuels like coal. Chemical-looping oxygen uncoupling uti-
lizing appropriate oxygen carriers can therefore facilitate 
the clean burning of solid fuels better than CLC. Prepared 
perovskites show potential for use in an uncoupling system 
due to their stability and high oxygen release rate. Oxygen 
evolution rates found in this study ranging from 0.0043 to 
0.0538 Kg ­O2/Kg for LFO OCMs are substantially higher 
than previously reported values for CMTM systems. ­LaFeO3 
which has numerous applications as conductor and nano-
catalysis had never been considered as CLOU carrier. It had 
the highest uncoupling rate among prepared materials in 
this study. The developed materials can be used in uncou-
pling systems which might open new avenues of research 
and commercialization. However, consistency of oxygen 
uncoupling rate over multiple cycles is yet to be investigated.
