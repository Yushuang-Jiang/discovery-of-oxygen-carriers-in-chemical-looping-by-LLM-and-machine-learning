A B S T R A C T   
Co-based oxides have been considered as one of the most promising materials for thermochemical energy storage 
(TCES) systems, however, the high operation temperature limits their applications. Specially, when Co3O4-based 
materials are used in concentrated solar power (CSP) system, a large mirror field area is required to meet the high 
endothermic temperature, which significantly increases the cost of a CSP system. In addition, the high cost of Co- 
based materials also limits its application. Improving the energy density can effectively reduce the use of the 
materials. Thus, in this work, to reduce the reduction onset temperature and increase the energy density of Co- 
based oxides, the TCES performance of Co3-xMgxO4 oxides is investigated. Co2.8Mg0.2O4 shows the best TCES 
performance. The reduction onset temperature of Co2.8Mg0.2O4 is ~775.6 ◦C, which is 133.7 ◦C lower than that 
of the pure Co3O4 (~909.3 ◦C). Mg-decoration can also improve the energy density of Co3O4 by 8.02 %. 
Furthermore, the in situ XRD spectra of the oxides indicate that precipitation of MgO from the Co3O4 lattice 
during the reduction reaction result in a metastable structure of the oxide, which is conducive to its reduction 
reaction. Density functional theory calculations indicate that Co2.8Mg0.2O4 possessed a higher defect formation 
energy and shorter chemical bonds than the pure cobalt oxide, which result in its lower reduction onset tem­
perature and higher energy density. The cost-benefit analysis results showed that Mg-decoration could signifi­
cantly reduce the area of mirror field and the use of energy storage material for a CSP system.   
1. Introduction 
The emission of anthropogenic CO2 due to the excessive use of 
conventional fossil fuels is believed to be the main contributor to global 
warming [1,2], climate change, and environmental pollution, which 
have been adversely affecting ecosystems globally [3–5]. Thus, the 
development of renewable energy is significant to the reduction of the 
global emission of CO2 [6,7]. Solar energy is a promising source of 
abundant and clean renewable energy, however, its periodic availability 
on earth has limited its extensive application [8,9]. At present, 
concentrated solar power (CSP) plants use thermal energy storage (TES) 
systems, which enable the absorption and release of solar-derived 
thermal energy during on-sun and off-sun hours, respectively, to 
enhance the dispatchability of solar energy [10,11]. 
Presently, heat storage systems operate via three different mecha­
nisms: sensible (SES), latent (LES), and thermochemical (TCES) energy 
storage [12,13]. In the SES and LES mechanisms, which are employed in 
commercial CSP plants, molten salts are the main heat storage media, 
and the absorption and release of heat are dependent on the specific heat 
capacity and the phase transition of the materials, respectively [12,14]. 
However, as the operating temperature in upcoming third-generation 
CSP plants is over 700 ◦C [15], which is higher than the decomposi­
tion temperature of molten salts, the use of molten salts as heat storage 
media exhibits drawbacks such as salt decomposition and corrosion 
[16,17]. To obtain a highly efficient TES system, the conceptual design 
of the system must lead to a high energy density, and the operating 
temperature must be suitable for the heat storage medium. In a TCES 
system, the absorption and release of heat occur via the breakage and 
recombination of chemical bonds, respectively, and consequently, the 
energy density and operating temperature are considerably higher than 
those of SES and LES systems [18–20]. Thus, TCES systems are consid­
ered to be highly efficient energy storage systems for CSP plants. 
The most extensively studied types of TCES systems are hydroxide 
looping reactions (Ca(OH)2/CaO most widely investigated) and car­
bonate looping reactions (particularly CaCO3/CaO), which have been 
extensively studied for carbon capture and now TCES [21]. There are 
definitely fewer studies of metal oxide redox reactions [22–24]. In 
addition, the metal oxide redox system, which is considered to be an

open system, employs air as the heat transfer fluid and reactant and a 
reversible redox reaction for charging and discharging [25,26]. In this 
system, no product separation occurs, which significantly reduces its 
complexity. Among the various redox couples of pure metal oxides, such 
as Co3O4/CoO [27], Mn2O3/Mn3O4 [28], BaO2/BaO [29,30], CuO/ 
Cu2O [31] and Fe2O3/Fe3O4 [32], that have been investigated for TCES 
systems, Co3O4/CoO has been reported to exhibit favorable TCES 
properties owing to its higher energy density and superior redox ki­
netics. For instance, the enthalpy and rate of the redox reaction of 
Mn2O3/Mn3O4 are approximately 202 kJ/kg and 0–10 μmol/min/g 
[28,33], respectively, while those of Co3O4/CoO are approximately 844 
kJ/kg and 0–400 μmol/min/g [34,35]. However, despite the favorable 
TCES performance of Co3O4/CoO, its practical application has been 
hindered owing to the high material cost of Co. The main costs for a CSP 
plant are those of the heat storage material (9 %) and mirror field (62 %) 
[36], which are approximately $23,861.40/t (for Co) and $11.5–12.5/ 
m2, respectively [37]. Thus, to facilitate the use of Co3O4/CoO in CSP 
plants, it is significant to reduce the cost of the raw materials and mirror 
field. It is worth mentioning that with the decreasing of system tem­
perature, the thermodynamic cycle efficiency for CSP plants will be 
decreased, while the solar collecting efficiency will be increased. 
Meanwhile, in the temperature range of 650–900 ◦C by lowering system 
temperature, the decreased efficiency for thermodynamic cycle is less 
than that of increased for solar collecting [38]. 
Furthermore, the cost of the raw materials can be reduced through 
the enhancement of the energy density of the Co3O4-based system as it 
would lower the amount of Co required to store the same amount of 
energy. Although Mn-doped Co3O4 has been reported to effectively 
enhance the energy density (from 844 to 1264 kJ/kg) of a Co3O4-based 
heat storage system [39], they exhibit a significantly higher reduction 
reaction onset temperature than those of pure cobalt oxides 
(897–1230 ◦C), which increases the required area of the solar mirror 
field and consequently the cost of the mirror field. Furthermore, the use 
of spinel Co3-xNixO4 has been reported to reduce the onset reduction 
temperature, which could reduce the cost of the mirror field, while it did 
not exhibit an enhanced energy density [40]. Therefore, it is significant 
to investigate the development a Co-based system that operates at a low 
reduction onset temperature and simultaneously exhibits a high energy 
density. 
As well known, adding the secondary element to Co3O4 may cause 
the lattice distortion of Co-based materials. After lattice distortion, its 
structure is a metastable structure with higher defect formation energy, 
which can be decomposed at a lower temperature. Meanwhile, adding 
the secondary element can enhance the energy density of Co-based 
materials by increasing the Co–O bond energy. In our previous work, 
9 different elements (Yb, Si, Ce, La, Mg, Zr, Cu, Fe and Al) with mole 
fraction of 15 % were added into Co-based materials. It was found that 
Mg can lower its reduction onset temperature significantly. However, no 
in-depth investigation on its redox mechanism and cost-benefit analysis 
was carried out. Therefore, in this work, a Co-based TCES system con­
sisting of binary oxides of Co3-xMgxO4 (X = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 
0.8) was investigated with the aim of reducing cost of TCES system via 
the decrease in its reduction onset temperature and the enhancement for 
the oxide energy density. Finally, the effects of reduction onset tem­
perature and energy density of optimum Co–Mg binary oxide on the 
reduction in the cost of a CSP plant was investigated. The results of this 
study provide guidelines for the rational design of advanced materials 
for solar TCES systems. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Raw materials 
Cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O AR 99 %), magnesium 
nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO3)2⋅6H2O AR 99 %), glycol (C2H6O2 AR 98 
%), and citric acid (C6H8O7 AR ≥99.5 %) were purchased from 
Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). 
2.2. Synthesis method 
The metal oxides were synthesized using a modified Pechini method, 
where Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O and Mg(NO3)2⋅6H2O served as the precursors, 
and glycol and citric acid as the dispersant and chelating ligand, 
respectively. Based on the general formula Co3-xMgxO4, the amounts of 
Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O and Mg(NO3)2⋅6H2O were chosen such that the mass of 
the obtained oxide was 2 g. To synthesize the oxides, 10 mL of glycol was 
stirred for 1 h, and subsequently Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O, Mg(NO3)2⋅6H2O, and 
citric acid were added to it. After vigorous magnetic stirring for 1 h, the 
obtained colloid was dried at 180 ◦C for 12 h. Then, the obtained black 
powder was calcined in air at 800 ◦C for 10 h to obtain the final product 
with the required stoichiometry. The samples were labelled according to 
the proportion of Mg in the obtained oxide (for example, the 
Co2.8Mg0.2O4 and Co2.95Mg0.05O4 samples were labelled as 20 M and 5 
M, respectively. 
2.3. Characterization of materials 
The phase compositions of the oxides were identified from their XRD 
spectra (PANalytical B.V., Netherlands) generated for a 2θ range of 
10–80◦using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15186 nm), and the average crys­
tallite sizes and lattice parameters were calculated using the Scherrer's 
and Bragg's equations, respectively. The micro morphology and the 
elemental distribution on the surfaces of the oxides were investigated 
using a scanning electron microscope equipped with an X-ray energy 
dispersive spectrometer (SEM, Zeiss, sigma 30). The structures of the 
oxides were further investigated using their Raman spectra (LabRAM 
HR800, Horiba Jobin Yvon) generated using a backscattering geometry 
and a stabilized 532 nm diode-pumped solid-state laser. The in situ XRD 
spectra of the oxides were generated for a 2θ range of 5–80◦(scan rate 
0.14◦/s) using a diffractometer (PANalytical Empyrean) operating at 40 
kV and 40 mA and equipped with a Cu tube (λ = 1.5406 Å), a pixel 
detector (PIXcel3D), and a high-temperature furnace (Anton Paar HTK 
16). The heating and cooling rates of the furnace were set to 10 ◦C/min. 
2.4. Redox reactivity 
To investigate the redox reactivities of the oxides, their TGA and DSC 
curves were generated (Netzsch STA 449 F5). An 85 μL alumina crucible 
was used for the analyses, and the sample mass was approximately 20 
mg. The heating and cooling rates were set to 10 ◦C/min and the air flow 
to 100 mL/min (79 mL/min N2 and 21 mL/min O2). To calcine the 
samples prior to the redox cycles, they were heated to 1000 ◦C, cooled to 
950 ◦C, held at this temperature for 20 min, and then further cooled to 
750 ◦C. Each redox cycle consisted of two steps: heating from 750 to 
1000 ◦C and then cooling from 1000 to 750 ◦C. For every oxide, the 
reduction onset temperature was obtained from their respective TGA 
curve, and the efficiency of conversion (χ) was calculated using Eq. (1).

2.6. Computation details 
The thermodynamic phase diagram of a Co–Mg–O system was 
generated using the FactPS and FToxide databases present in the soft­
ware Factsage. In addition, the formation of all possible products in the 
forms of gas, solution, liquid, and solid compounds were considered 
using the information in Factsage. 
DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab-initio Simu­
lation Package (VASP). The single unit cell for Co3O4 was used for 
calculation, which included 24 cobalt atoms and 32 oxygen atoms, and 
the lattice parameters were 8.065 Å. Projector augmented-wave (PAW) 
pseudopotentials were used to describe the core electrons, and the 
plane-wave cut off energy was set to 400 eV, the K-point in the Brillouin 
area to 4 × 4 × 4, and the convergence criteria for EDIFFG to −0.05 eV. 
After the model was developed, the calculations of defect formation 
energy (Ef) were carried out using Eq. (6).

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Physicochemical properties of Co–Mg oxides 
As shown in Table 1, the results of the ICP-OES analyses confirmed 
that the actual amounts of Mg in most of the synthesized oxides (5 M, 20 
M, 40 M, 60 M, and 80 M) were approximately equal to their theoreti­
cally calculated amounts. In the synthesis process, the mixed solution 
was dried in an oven at 180 ◦C, in which it took place a rapid dehy­
dration reaction and was accompanied by dramatic volume expansion. 
This process was uncontrollable and there may be some loss of Mg- 
precursor. Thus, the Mg content referred to in the following sections is 
the measured value. 
XRD patterns for all samples were studied in depth using the Rietveld 
refinement methods. Experimental, computed data and their difference 
was show in Figs. S1–S7. Fig. 1(a) shows the refined XRD spectra of the 
Co3-xMgxO4 oxides. The diffraction peaks of the pure cobalt oxide (0 M) 
corresponded to the standard spectrum of a hexagonal crystal (PDF # 
78-1970) [44]. The spectra of samples 5 M–40 M indicated that these 
oxides were mainly comprised of the Co3O4 phase, and no characteristic 
peaks of MgO were observed. However, as shown by the 2θ values 
(36.0–37.5◦) in Fig. 1(b), every peak in the spectra of the samples 5 
M–80 M exhibited a slight shift from their characteristic positions, which 
indicated that Mg was successfully doped into the Co3O4 lattice of these 
samples. For 60 M and 80 M, as the Mg content was increased, charac­
teristic peaks corresponding to MgO (PDF # 77-2179) were observed in 
addition to the peaks of the Co3O4 phase (Fig. 1(a)). This indicated that 
at a low Mg content, Co3O4 is the predominant phase, whereas at a high

Table 1 and Fig. 1(c) show the values of the crystal size (d) and lattice 
parameter (a), which were estimated based on the XRD spectra, of the 
synthesized oxides. The lattice parameter (a) of the pure cobalt oxide 
was 8.0867 Å, and the values of the lattice parameter of the oxides 
increased with the Mg content (Fig. 1(c)). This increase could be 
attributed to the formation of crystal defects and an expansion of the 
crystal lattice of the Co–Mg binary oxide as the Co atoms are replaced 
by larger Mg atoms. A schematic of the change in the crystal structure is 
shown in Fig. 1(c). Although the correlation between the lattice 
parameter and Mg content could be attributed to Vegard's law [46], the 
samples 40 M, 60 M, and 80 M did not exhibit this correlation owing to 
the formation of a mixed phase consisting of MgO and Co3O4. At a lower 
Mg content (0 M, 5 M, 10 M and 20 M), the Mg atoms could well sub­
stitute the Co atoms in Co3O4 crystal lattice. However, with the 
increasing of Mg content (40 M, 60 M and 80 M), most of Mg atoms were 
formed MgO, and for 40 M, the MgO may attach to the surface of Co3O4 
crystal lattice uniformly, thus the Co3-xMgxO4 crystal lattice was com­
pressed corresponding a lower lattice parameter and MgO diffraction 
peaks could not be observed [46]. Furthermore, the gradual increase in 
peak width in Fig. 1b illustrated that the crystallinity for all samples 
decreased with the increasing of Mg content. And to quantitatively 
analyze the crystallinity of all synthesized materials, the crystal size (d) 
was also calculated and shown in Table 1. As shown in Table 1, the 
crystal size (d) decreased linearly with an increase in the Mg content. 
After doping Mg, the Co3O4 crystal nucleation and growth was pre­
vented. Moreover, owing to the larger atomic radius of Mg, Co3O4 
crystal was expanded in Fig. 1(c), the crystal symmetry was weakened, 
which may also be detrimental to crystal growth [47,48]. 
Fig. 1(d) shows the Raman spectra of the Co–Mg binary oxides with 
the various Mg content. The various sites of a spinel structure exhibit 
characteristic bands in a Raman spectrum (the octahedral sites with A1g 
symmetry and tetrahedral sites with F2g symmetry correspond to Raman 
shifts of 691 and 194 cm−1, respectively). The spectrum of Co3O4 
exhibited well-defined bands at approximately 488 (Eg), 522 (T2g), and 
691 (A1g) cm−1, which correspond to the vibration modes of the well- 
known spinel structure of Co3O4 [49]. As shown in Fig. 1(d), the addi­
tion of Mg resulted in a blue-shift and increase in intensity of the 
characteristic peaks, which could be attributed to the enhanced Co–O 
bond energy [50,51]. Owing to the larger radius of the Mg atom, the 
Co–O bond length may become shorter with the addition of Mg. 
The EDS mapping images of 20 M (Fig. 2) were chosen as the 
representative images to investigate the micro morphology and 
elemental distribution of the synthesized oxides. Moreover, the images 
of the other samples are shown in Figs. S8–S13. From Fig. 2(a) we 
observed that Co, Mg, and O were homogeneously distributed over the 
surface of the oxides, which indicated that Mg can be uniformly doped 
into Co3O4. The corresponding EDS spectra of 20 M indicated that the 
atomic ratio of Co, Mg, and O were 41.36 %, 40.5 %, and 54.59 %, 
respectively, while the value of Mg/(Co + Mg) was 8.9 % and that of X in 
Co3-xMgxO4 was 0.27, which was consistent with the ICP-OES result. 
Based on the ICP-OES, XRD, and EDS results we deduced that the syn­
thesis method used in this work is feasible, and the TCES performance of 
Co–Mg binary oxides can be reliably investigated using these samples.

3.2. The redox performance for 2 charging-discharging cycles

aimed at the calcination of the samples to remove impurities, such as 
adsorbed water and carbon dioxide from air, as they cause an irrevers­
ible weight loss of the oxide and consequently complicate the estimation 
of the redox mass change [52]. As shown in Fig. 3(a), although the TGA 
curve of every sample exhibits reversible weight loss and weigh gain 
regions, the TCES behavior indicated by every curve was different. In 
Fig. 3(b), the reduction onset temperature for the first reduction reaction 
is decreasing as the Mg content increased. While the reduction onset 
temperature for the second reduction reaction decreased for 0 M, 5 M, 
10 M, and 20 M but increased for 40 M, 60 M, and 80 M. Furthermore, 
the oxidation rates of the Co–Mg binary oxides were slower than that of 
the pure cobalt oxide. To further investigate the TCES performance of 
the samples, their reduction onset temperatures and efficiency of

As shown in Fig. 4(a), for the first redox cycles of the oxides, as the 
Mg content increased, the reduction onset temperature of the oxides 
decreased (858.2 and 672.9 ◦C for 5 M and 80 M, respectively), and the 
pure cobalt oxide (0 M) exhibited the highest reduction onset temper­
ature (909.3 ◦C). The decrease in the reduction onset temperature can be 
explained from a thermodynamics point of view. Fig. 4(b) shows the 
predicted metal oxide phases corresponding to the various temperature 
and atmospheric conditions of the calcination process and the subse­
quent redox cycling conditions (25–1200 ◦C in air at standard atmo­
spheric pressure). The curve between the monoxide and spinel +
monoxide phased exhibited a downward tendency as the Mg content 
increased, which indicated that the temperature at which phase change 
occurred during the heating step decreased as the Mg content increased. 
Furthermore, at room temperature (25–30 ◦C), the samples with a Mg/ 
(Mg + Co) ratio below 0.24 exhibited a spinel phase, and the ones with a 
Mg/(Mg + Co) ratio above 0.24 exhibited a mixed spinel + monoxide 
phase, which is in agreement with the XRD patterns shown in Fig. 1. For 
a temperature range of 750–950 ◦C, as 0 M, 5 M, and 10 M are composed 
of the spinel phase initially, the oxides absorb heat to undergo phase 
changes from spinel to spinel + monoxide to monoxide, resulting in a 
higher energy density of these samples. 
Notably, in the second redox cycle, the oxides exhibited different 
thermal behaviors compared with that in the first cycle. Based on our 
previous studies [35], we speculated that the first cycle typically does 
not reflect the actual TCES performance of an oxide owing to the pres­
ence of impurities, which could be attributed to the adsorbed water, 
carbon dioxides and the incompletely decomposed nitrates and organic 
components. After the high temperature calcination, the impurities 
would not interfere the TCES performance anymore. And thus, the data 
from the second cycle was considered to investigate the TCES perfor­
mance of the oxides. On comparison of the second redox cycles of the 
oxides, 20 M exhibited the lowest reduction onset temperature. It should 
be noted that the difference in the reduction onset temperatures in the 
first and second cycles could be attributed to an incomplete redox re­
action in the first cycle, in which with the addition of Mg, the re- 
oxidation step is weakened, especially for higher Mg content (40 M, 
60 M and 80 M), the re-oxidation rate is lower than the cooling rate. 
Therefore, owing to the present test conditions, the samples with higher 
Mg content showed incomplete oxidation reaction before the 
commencement of the subsequent reduction reaction, which delayed the 
second reduction reaction and consequently increased the reduction 
onset temperature of the second redox cycle. The reversibility of the 
redox reactions was examined via their efficiency of conversion, which 
were estimated based on the TGA curves shown in Fig. 3 and Eq. (1). The 
addition of Mg to the oxides led to a decrease in the efficiency of con­
version of the redox reactions, and as the Mg content was increased, the 
weight loss and weight gain of the samples decreased. Based on the 
weight gain and weight loss values shown in Fig. 4(c), the pure cobalt 
oxide exhibited the efficiency of conversion of approximately 0.97, 
which indicated an excellent redox reversibility. The efficiency of con­
version slightly decreased for 5 M and 10 M (0.93 and 0.92, respectively) 
and considerably decreased for 40 M, 60 M, and 80 M (0.86, 0.66, and 
0.21, respectively). This result is well supported by that of Fig. 3. On the 
contrary, 20 M exhibited a similar efficiency of conversion (0.95) to that 
of the pure cobalt oxide (0.97), which indicated that 20 M was the op­
timum synthesized Co–Mg oxide and could be effectively used in a 
TCES system owing to its excellent redox performance.

3.3. The long-term cyclability 
In a TCES system, the TCES material typically undergoes charging 
and discharging cycles at high temperatures (600–1200 ◦C) for long 
periods. Thus, although 20 M exhibited a favorable TCES performance, 
the investigation of its cyclic stability is significant. For this purpose, 0 M 
and 20 M were subjected to 50 redox cycles in air with a flow rate of 100 
mL/min (79 mL/min N2 and 21 mL/min O2) at a temperature range of 
650–950 ◦C and heating rate of 10 ◦C/min. To avoid the influence of 
impurities and an incomplete reaction on the investigation, the second 
and forty ninth cycles were considered as the first and last redox cycles. 
As shown in Fig. 5, although the efficiency of conversion of the pure 
cobalt oxide exhibited a higher stability than that of 20 M, the efficiency 
of conversion of both oxides were above 0.95 after 50 cycles, which 
indicated the favorable long-term cyclability of 20 M. Furthermore, on 
comparison with the oxides reported in previous studies after various 
number of cycles (Table S1), 20 M exhibited the excellent performance 
on the efficiency of conversion, lower onset reduction temperature and 
energy density. 
As shown in Fig. 5(b), the reduction onset temperatures of 0 M and 
20 M remained virtually constant after the second redox cycle, and at the 
end of the last cycle these temperatures were approximately 910–914 ◦C 
for 0 M and 816–823 ◦C for 20 M. Thus, based on the similar efficiency of 
conversion of 20 M to that of 0 M and a lower reduction onset temper­
ature of 20 M than that of 0 M (difference of 91–94 ◦C) after 50 redox 
cycles, we deduced that 20 M exhibited a superior long-term cyclability. 
3.4. Redox kinetics analysis 
The kinetics of the redox reactions of 0 M and 20 M were investigated 
using TGA. For the reduction kinetics analysis, the oxides were heated to 
1000 ◦C at three different heating rates (5, 10, and 20 ◦C/min) in N2 
atmosphere (100 mL/min) and subsequently cooled to 100 ◦C at a 
heating rate of 10 ◦C/min. Whereas for the oxidation kinetics analysis, 
the oxides were heated from 100 ◦C to 1000 ◦C at the same three heating 
rates in air (79 mL/min N2 and 21 mL/min O2). For the various effi­
ciency of conversion (0.1–0.9) estimated from the TGA curves, the 
corresponding activation energy values were calculated using Fried­
man's method (Fig. 6). As shown in Table S2, the R2 value for every 
efficiency of conversion value is above 0.98, which verifies the reli­
ability of the fit. 
As shown in Fig. 6(a), the activation energy increased as the effi­
ciency of conversion increased from 0.1 to 0.2 but decreased as the ef­
ficiency of conversion further increased from 0.2 to 0.9. For every 
efficiency of conversion value, the activation energy of 20 M was lower 
than of 0 M, which indicated that the reduction reaction of 20 M was 
straightforward compared with that of 0 M, which resulted in a lower 
reduction onset temperature of 20 M. 
As shown in Fig. 6(b), for the efficiency of conversion of 0.1, the 
activation energy of 20 M with respect to oxidation was significantly 
higher than that of 0 M, which indicated that the oxidation reaction of 
20 M was complicated compared with that of 0 M. Furthermore, for the 
higher efficiency of conversion, the activation energy values of 20 M 
were only slightly higher than those of 0 M. This indicated that the 
oxidation reaction for binary oxides is mainly influenced by the initial 
stage of the reaction, which could be ascribed to the intercalation and 
exfoliation of Mg ions into the Co3O4 lattice. Thus, compared to a single

Fig. 7(b) and (c) shows the in situ XRD spectra of 20 M as it under­

temperature in the in situ XRD instrument is lower than that in the TGA 
heating furnace e, thus in this work, the sample temperature is tem­
perature that is corrected by TGA. 
As shown in Fig. 7(b), prior to the reduction, the oxide was composed 
of solely Co3O4 and no diffraction peaks corresponding to MgO were 
observed, which indicated that Mg atoms had successfully doped into 
the Co3O4 crystal structure. During the heating period, the intensities of 
the characteristic peaks of Co3O4 decreased, and from 700 to 750 ◦C, 
characteristic peaks of MgO (PDF # 77-2179) were observed, which 
indicated that the oxide was composed of MgO and Co3O4. Furthermore, 
the appearance of these peaks indicated that MgO could be readily 
precipitated in the lattice, resulting in an unstable crystal structure of 
the oxide. From 850 to 950 ◦C, the characteristic peaks of CoO (PDF # 
78-0431) were observed, and at 950 ◦C, the peaks of Co3O4 disappeared. 
Thus, at this temperature, the peaks in the spectrum corresponded to 
CoO and MgO. The oxidation mechanism of the oxide was examined 
during the cooling period, and the tendency of the phase composition 
was opposite to that during the reduction reaction. As the cooling period 
commenced shown in Fig. 7(c), the intensities of the peaks of CoO and 
MgO decreased, and the peaks of Co3O4 appeared at 750 ◦C. The peaks of 
CoO and MgO disappeared at 650 ◦C, which indicated that Mg atoms 
reentered the Co3O4 lattice as the oxide cooled to 650 ◦C. Based on the

3.6. Cost-benefit analysis 
As shown in Fig. 9(a) and (b), the costs of the mirror field and heat 
storage material contribute significantly to the costs of a CSP plant [36]. 
The low reduction onset temperature and high energy density of the 
Co–Mg binary oxide (20 M) indicate that it can effectively reduce these 
costs. It should be pointed that the improvement of system efficiency 
caused by lowering reduction onset temperature is not considered in this 
work. Based on Eqs. (7) and (8), a lower absorber temperature (reduc­
tion onset temperature) could reduce the concentration ratio and 
consequently reduce the required mirror field area. 
For the onset of the reduction reaction, the oxide 20 M requires a 
temperature of approximately 815 ◦C, which corresponds to a concen­
tration ratio of approximately 59, whereas the pure cobalt oxide requires 
a temperature of 915 ◦C, which corresponds to a concentration ratio of 
approximately 84. Consequently, owing to 20 M, the cost of the mirror 
field can be reduced by 29.8 % ((84 −59) / 84 × 100), as shown in Fig. 9 
(d). 
Furthermore, owing to the higher energy density of 20 M than that of 
0 M and the lower cost of MgO than that of Co3O4 (Table 3), the cost per 
energy density of 20 M (0.64 $/(kJ/kg)) is 12.3 % lower than of 0 M 
(0.73 $/(kJ/kg)) (Fig. 9(d)).

was considered as the optimum heat storage oxide owing to its lowest 
reduction onset temperature (775.6 ◦C, which was 103.7 ◦C lower than 
of the pure cobalt oxide) and highest energy density (8.02 % higher 
compared to the pure cobalt oxide). Furthermore, after 50 redox cycles,
