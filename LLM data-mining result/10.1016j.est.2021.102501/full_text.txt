A B S T R A C T   
Thermochemical heat storage system exhibits extraordinary properties compared with sensible or latent heat 
storage system. Owing to the versatility of perovskite, Sr-based perovskites were investigated for redox-type 
thermochemical energy storage at medium-high temperature. In this study, SrCoO3-δ, SrFeO3-δ and SrMnO3-δ 
were prepared via a modified Pechini method. The oxidation enthalpy of each sample was measured using a 
combination of tubular furnace and DSC. Composition analysis, redox and cyclability tests revealed that SrFeO3-δ 
possesses favorable behavior validating the design principles.   
1. Introduction 
Recently, due to the increasing global demand for energy and 
exhaustion of fossil fuel resources, there has been a greater focus on 
technology for solar and other renewable energy technologies. In the 
field of solar energy, concentrating solar power (CSP) plants represent 
the most effective form of solar energy conversion. Suffering from the 
problem of solar energy intermittency, excellent storage systems are 
needed to make continuous operation possible. As for now, the heat 
storage systems can be classified into sensible, latent, and thermo­
chemical heat storage (TCS) system [1], of which TCS exhibits the most 
favorable properties, such as improved energy storage density, a wider 
range of operational temperature [2] and theoretically unlimited dis­
tance of transportation [3]. 
Redox type TCS system is based on reduction-oxidation reaction of 
metal oxide, as seen in Eq. (1). Driven by thermal energy, reduction 
(charge) process was conducted. When energy is later required, the 
metal oxides in their reduced state could re-oxidize in oxygen and 
release heat. Theoretically, it does not require a tank for the storage of 
gaseous products as oxygen can be extracted or released from the 
atmosphere. 
MOoxidized state + ΔHr⇌MOreduced state + O2
(1)

Co3O4/CoO [6–8], Fe2O3/Fe3O4 [9] and Mn2O3/MnO [10], perovskite 
oxides (ABO3) do not encounter a discrete structural phase change 
during the redox process [Eq. (2)].

Crystal phase transformation implies severe lattice reorganization 
will occur, potentially damaging the long-term recyclability of the ma­
terial. Therefore, perovskites are more appropriate for TCS system than 
traditional redox pairs. Furthermore, the reduction and oxidation of 
perovskites occur faster than traditional redox pairs mentioned above, 
which can be attributed to the faster O2−diffusion in nonstoichiometric 
oxides. Typically, perovskite oxides can re-oxidize in the air within a few 
seconds [11]. However, the heat storage density of perovskite material 
might be restricted by the absence of obvious crystal phase change. 
Pioneering this work, Babiniec et al. investigated LaxSr1-xCoyM1-yO3- 
δ (M=Mn, Fe) perovskite materials as thermochemical energy storage 
media for CSP system in 2015 [12]. Actually, LaxSr1-xCoyM1-yO3-δ can be 
regarded as SrCoO3-δ doped by La, Mn and Fe. Increasing La concen­
tration resulted in a lattice distortion away from the ideal cubic struc­
ture, which represented the highest redox capacity. Later, they reported 
the highest storage capacity (ΔH=390 kJ/kg) of perovskites until now, 
by using doped calcium manganites (CaBxMn1-xO3-δ, B=Al, Ti) [13]. 
Recently, Mastronardo et al. investigated CaFexMn1-xO3-δ (x = 0.1, 
0.3) for thermochemical heat storage [14]. The introduction of 0.1 Fe

Zhang et al. reported Ba and Sr series perovskites for thermochemical 
energy storage [15]. They claimed that oxygen monoatomic ion of pe­

energy threshold and results in an inconspicuous DSC peak during 
reduction and oxidation. Although Sr-based perovskites are already re­
ported by Zhang et al. [15], detailed studies on energy storage density 
are lacking due to the limitation of DSC apparatus. 
Currently, TCS technology is primarily applied for solar energy uti­
lization and recycle. However, other thermal technology fields, such as 
steel industry, electric vehicle, and seasonal indoor heating, also require 
advanced TCS technology. In practice, such applications cannot be 
operated under excessively high-temperature condition and volume 
restrictions for heat storage equipment may exist. Although most re­
searches in this filed focus on the high temperature, the development of 
medium-high temperature heat storage technology remains stagnant, 
resulting in wasted energy resources as well as gaps in TCS research. The 
utilization of thermal energy is always a challenge in the field of 
renewable energy; in particular, the exergy of thermal energy is limited 
as the temperature decreasing, making that energy is more difficult to 
capture. 
A few principles for medium-high temperature redox-type thermo­
chemical energy storage deign are summarized below:  
I Fast reaction kinetics; either reduction or oxidation.  
II Can be operable in the medium-high temperature range.  
III Appropriated operational oxygen partial pressure (A high or low 
pO2 requires more energy input).  
IV Sufficient thermochemical heat storage capacity.  
V Cyclability and durability.  
VI Affordable raw materials.  
VII Low toxicity. 
Herein, the composition analysis, redox ability and cyclability of Sr- 
based perovskite were investigated. Oxidation enthalpy was investi­
gated in particular by a combination of tubular furnace and DSC 
analysis. 
2. Experimental approach 
2.1. Synthesis 
SrBO3-δ (B=Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu) perovskite was synthesized by a 
modified Pechini method [16]. Stoichiometric amounts of nitrate salts 
Sr(NO3)2 (98%, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), Mn 
(NO3)2⋅6H2O (98%, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), Fe 
(NO3)3⋅9H2O (99%, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), Co 
(NO3)2⋅6H2O (98%, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), Ni 
(NO3)2⋅6H2O (98%, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and 
Cu(NO3)2⋅3H2O (98%, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) 
were dissolved in deionized water, then citric acid was added to the 
solution at a ratio of 1:2 (cation: citric acid). Ethylene glycol was 
introduced as a reactant with citric acid, then adjusted the pH value to 
around 9 with ammonia solution subsequently. The solution was dried 
and pre-calcined at 500◦C for 1h in air to remove all the organic com­
ponents. After grounding, these powders were pelletized with a 25 mm 
die at 30 MPa, then pellets were heated to 1200◦C for 5 hours in air. In 
order to preserve the metastable brownmillerite (reduction state) phase 
of perovskite until XRD analysis was performed, a few samples were 
quenched by liquid N2. The remaining samples were cooled at room 
temperature. For convenience, the quenched samples are denoted as 
Q-ABO3-δ and the samples without quenching are denoted as NQ-ABO3-δ. 
2.2. Structural characterization 
The pre-calcined sample, Q-ABO3-δ and NQ-ABO3-δ sample were 
investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (SmartLab, Rigaku) with Cu 
Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm, 40 kV – 25 mA). Crystalline phases were 
analyzed using PDXL2 (Rigaku Data Analysis Software). 
2.3. TG analysis and SEM morphology 
Three thermogravimetric analysis programs were performed for this 
study. For the first program, the redox capacity and mass change of the 
NQ-ABO3-δ and Q-ABO3-δ families were evaluated. The primary redox 
characterization of the NQ-ABO3-δ and Q-ABO3-δ families was tested 
using pellet fragments (~20mg) in a HITACHI STA7300. This experi­
ment could not determine the absolute δ values of each sample, but 
provided an appropriate standard for comparing different families. The 
first program consisted of two cycles. The “break-in” cycle aim to 
removing any impurity (i.e. adsorbed species on the surface of the 
sample) [12,17,18] and eliminating the quenching effect. The initial 
mass was recorded at 100◦C, and the sample was heated up to 1000◦C 
holding for 1 h then re-oxidized in the air while cooled to 100◦C for 90 
min. This investigation was vital for screening the best candidates for 
further study. The second program acted as a “pre-experiment” aims to 
obtaining an oxidation rate of the reduced perovskites at room tem­
perature. This ensured the validity of combining a tubular furnace and 
DSC analysis. The third program was set up for cyclability and durability 
tests. The SEM morphology was also studied using as-prepared, 1 cycle 
and 10 cycles samples. Details on each TG program will be presented in 
later section. 
2.4. Oxidation enthalpy measurement 
To measure oxidation enthalpy, NQ-ABO3-δ samples were heated to 
1000◦C in an argon flow using tubular furnace to obtain the most 
reduced ABO2.5 brownmillerite phase, then cooled down to the room 
temperature also in a protective argon flow. Isothermal oxidation 
method was performed for SrCoO3-δ and SrFeO3-δ. After heating to the 
appropriate temperature under N2 protection, the gas was changed to air 
when target temperature was stable. The exothermic signal was detected 
using DSC apparatus (DSC-60, Shimadzu Corp.). The oxidation enthalpy 
of SrMnO3-δ was measured using a non-isothermal oxidation method. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Synthesis mechanism and characterization of composition 
The preparation schematic of Sr-based perovskites via Pechini 
method was shown in Fig. 1. 
Metallic cations were initially chelated with citric acid. With the aid 
of polyalcohol, the corresponding metallic citrates were cross-linked to 
form a gel through esterification. The organic compositions were 
removed after pre-calcine process, and the cations were subsequently 
oxidized at high temperature to form a perovskite structure. 
Although SrNiO3-δ and SrCuO3-δ perovskite were also prepared using 
Pechini method, pure SrNiO3-δ and SrCuO3-δ perovskite were not ob­
tained in this study because of their instability in normal condition [19, 
20]. Moreover, the complex synthesis conditions do not satisfy the 
design principles and unsuitable for practical application. Therefore, 
only SrCoO3-δ, SrFeO3-δ and SrMnO3-δ were considered for further study. 
To confirm the validity of Pechini methods, XRD analysis of pre- 
calcined, NQ-ABO3-δ and Q-ABO3-δ samples were performed. The re­
sults are summarized in Table 1 and typical XRD pattern of NQ-ABO3-δ 
and Q-ABO3-δ samples are presented in Fig. 2. 
The XRD results of pre-calcined samples coincide with the mecha­
nism introduced in Fig. 1, but interestingly, Sr tends to create two kinds 
of simple substance rather than oxides. Conversely, transition metal

The phase composition of the unquenched samples (NQ-SrCoO3-δ, 
NQ-SrFeO3-δ, and NQ-SrMnO3-δ) were analyzed using XRD. After 
compared with standard PDF card, it was observed that instead of the 
predicted SrCoO3 phase, NQ-SrCoO3-δ exhibited a Sr6Co5O15 phase that 
the molar ratio of Sr and Co is not equal to 1:1. Previous evidence 
suggests that during room temperature cooling of SrCoO3-δ after the 
Pechini method, it will decompose into Sr6Co5O15 and a small amount of 
Co3O4. Based on the synthesis mechanism of pre-calcined samples, there 
are two possible explanations for this result: A. when cooling at room 
temperature, a small amount of Co was decomposed from SrCoO3-δ then 
slowly oxidized in air, while remaining SrCoO3-δ was evolved into more 
stable Sr6Co5O15 phase; B. during the cooling process, SrCoO3 was 
generated first, and a small amount of Sr was decomposed from SrCoO3-δ 
then reacted with the undecomposed SrCoO3-δ to form Sr6Co5O15. Irre­
spective of the mechanism, the XRD results suggested Sr6Co5O15 is more 
stable than SrCoO3 at room temperature, making it difficult to synthe­
size and use in practical applications. The Sr-Co-O phase diagram [21] 
shows that when temperature was lower than 1150 K, it was impossible 
to form SrCoO3 perovskite even if the mole ratio of raw materials was 
1:1. 
Conversely, SrFeO3-δ presented continued phase from high temper­
ature to room temperature. Nonetheless, even unquenched sample 
shows unsaturated phase mainly because the dense pelletization of 
sample may obstruct oxygen diffusion, the inner part cannot acquire 
enough oxygen. 
The metastable brownmillerite phase can be obtained using the 
quenching method. However, Q-SrFeO3-δ also consists of SrFeO2.875, 
maintaining a relatively larger δ value. This suggested that even sample 
was “frozen” by the quenching method, but it will still slowly oxidize 
over time. It is interesting to notice that the quenching effect of SrMnO3- 
δ has not been detected, which can be attributed to stronger Mn-O bond 
that needs higher temperature to release lattice oxygen. Moreover, 
SrMnO3 shows hexagonal structure instead of standard ABO3 perovskite 
cubic structure, leading its redox behavior, viz. oxygen exchange ca­
pacity, which deviates from another two [22].

3.2. Redox behavior 
The redox behavior of NQ-ABO3-δ and Q-ABO3-δ families were 
determined using TGA. Fig. 3 shows the profile of TGA program and 
mass change of each sample. SrCoO3-δ and SrFeO3-δ exhibited very 
similar redox behavior when heated to 1000◦C in the atmospheric 
ambient, while SrMnO3-δ showed inactivity under these conditions. 
Because some adsorbed species may remain on the surface of the sample, 
the first cycle is used to diminish the effect of impurities. The initial mass 
of the second cycle was used as the initial mass of each sample at a time 
of 210 min. 
The changes in mass of unquenched SrCoO3-δ, SrFeO3-δ and SrMnO3-δ 
samples are 2.11%, 2.34% and 0.34%, respectively. The changes in mass 
of quenched SrCoO3-δ, SrFeO3-δ and SrMnO3-δ samples were slightly 
lower (1.72%, 2.11% and 0.33%, respectively). The change in δ, namely 
Δδ, can be calculated easily using Eq. (3): 
Δδ = Δm⋅MWABO3
minitial⋅MWO
(3)  
where Δm is the change in mass, minitial is the initial mass, MWABO3 is the 
molecular weight of perovskite (δ = 0), MWO is the molecular weight of 
monoatomic oxygen. The results for Δδ are listed in Table 2. 
It is interesting to notice that after quenching process the redox 
behavior of ABO3-δ was slightly worse than those unquenched samples. 
This is mainly due to irreversible damage caused to ABO3-δ during the 
quenching process; being unable to return to most oxidized state leads to 
a narrow range of mass change. In particular, the Q-ABO3-δ families 
show rapid mass change behavior in the first cycle, around 700◦C and 
400◦C for SrCoO3-δ and SrFeO3-δ, respectively. By quenching, the 
perovskite sample can be “frozen” at reduced state until it returns to 
room temperature. When “frozen” perovskite was heated again, the 
oxidation rate was faster. As presented in Fig. 3 (b), when a certain 
temperature was reached, around 800◦C for SrCoO3-δ and 500◦C for 
SrFeO3-δ, Q-ABO3-δ almost return to the initial mass. However, as tem­
perature keeps increasing the reduction behavior was observed. 
Although the reduction reaction occurs during the entire heating pro­
cess, the reduction rate is lower than the oxidation rate until a certain 
temperature is reached. Nonetheless, unlike the other perovskites, 
SrMnO3-δ did not exhibit an obvious quenching effect except for a slight 
variation in the mass change. 
Actually, in the case of SrCoO3-δ and SrFeO3-δ, their redox behaviors 
are quite different in a sense due to their own property. The mass change 
of SrFeO3-δ seems like a linear function in the temperature range from 
400◦C to 1000◦C either reduction or oxidation. This property can exist 
over two cycles, which can be demonstrated by cyclability test. SrCoO3- 
δ, on the other hand, represents more complicated reduction behavior. 
This behavior can be classified into broken line function, with a change 
in slope at approximately 900◦C. It can be attributed to the variety of Sr- 
Co-O ternary component phase. Ondˇrej Jankovský et al. [21] previously 
investigated the detailed phase composition of Sr-Co-O. According to 
Sr-Co-O phase diagram, at a temperature below 877◦C, Sr and Co can 
only create quasi-one-dimensional Sr6Co5O15 [23] rather than the 
SrCoO3-δ perovskite structure. These results also show perfect consis­
tency with ours. From another point of view, varied phase change during 
the redox process seems unfavorable to reactor design because of the 
instability of kinetics. Even when the SrMnO3-δ was heated up to 
1000◦C, it remained in a relatively high oxidation state. In the supple­
mentary experiment performed by Zhang et al. [15], despite heating 
SrMnO3-δ perovskite to 1150◦C in an Ar atmosphere, namely reduced in 
an extremely low oxygen partial pressure, it only exhibited a mass 
change of less than 2%. This indicates that SrMnO3-δ may possess a large 
reduction enthalpy; the estimated value was reported by other authors 
[24,25], but in terms of heat storage at medium-high temperature, the 
rigorous operating conditions pose some problems. 
3.3. Oxidation enthalpy estimate 
To confirm that the oxidation rate in the ambient condition of the 
reduced sample was slow enough for DSC measurement, a pre- 
experiment was performed with the result shown in Fig. S1. The NQ- 
ABO3-δ family was heated to 1000◦C in a N2 atmosphere to obtain 
reduced samples, then gas was changed into synthesized air while 
temperature was fixed at 100◦C to determine the oxidation rate under 
normal condition. The values of Δδ in N2 atmosphere and air are listed in 
Table S1. To study the thermochemical heat storage capacity per unit 
volume, the density of each sample was measured using helium pyc­
nometer method, with results of 5.70 g/cm3 for SrCoO3-δ, 5.79 g/cm3 for 
SrFeO3-δ and 6.82 g/cm3 for SrMnO3-δ. 
3.3.1. Oxidation enthalpy of Sr(Co,Fe)O3-δ 
According to the calculation results from Fig. S1, SrCoO3-δ and 
SrFeO3-δ can be almost reduced into ABO2.5 brownmillerite phase, and 
the changes in δ for SrCoO3-δ and SrFeO3-δ were so small that they can be 
ignored while gas was changed into air. Oxidation state samples were 
subjected to heat treatment using tubular furnace to obtain reduced 
samples. Then reduced samples were heated to appropriate temperature 
in N2 protection, the gas was changed to air, and the DSC exothermic 
peak was monitored. This is known as the isothermal oxidation method. 
Typical DSC results were shown in Fig. 4. 
An appropriate oxidation temperature is crucial for each reduced 
sample due to the temperature and oxygen partial pressure sensitivity of 
perovskite materials. Excessively high oxidation temperature may result 
in incomplete oxidation, as high temperature also promotes reduction

3.3.2. Oxidation enthalpy of SrMnO3-δ 
It was found that SrMnO3-δ appears to have a significant oxygen af­
finity. Even when the reduced SrMnO3-δ was heated to 500◦C in N2 at­
mosphere (extreme low oxygen partial pressure), the reduced SrMnO3-δ 
still re-oxidized in 30 min. As seen in Fig. S2, the reduced SrMnO3-δ 
exhibits similar oxidation behavior in N2 or Air. For this reason, a non- 
isothermal oxidation method was adopted. The reduced SrMnO3-δ was 
heated at a fixed heating rate (10◦C/min) in air. After integrated the 
exothermic peak, the oxidation enthalpy was found to be 25.6 ± 5.8 kJ/ 
kg-ABO3 (230.3 ± 39.4 kJ/L). A typical pattern is shown in Fig. 5. 
Since it was found that SrMnO3-δ can only reach a small Δδ (from 
Fig. S2, Δδ only reached 0.1), even under high temperature and low pO2 
condition, its oxidation enthalpy was not satisfactory. On the contrary, it 
shows an unusual stability, suggesting that SrMnO3-δ may possess the 
highest oxidation enthalpy among the all Sr-based perovskites while its 
Δδ is sufficiently large. Unfortunately, because the relationship of Δδ 
and enthalpy is not always linear, it is difficult to predict the complete 
oxidation value by DSC method from only a single point. Vieten et al. 
[24] estimated the reaction enthalpy of SrMnO3-δ (373 ± 55 kJ/mol-O2) 
using Van’t Hoff approach, but this method poses severe measurement 
uncertainties since only small mass changes were detected. It is worth 
noting that the unit they used (kJ/mol-O2) means the amount of heat 
released when perovskite reacts with 1 mol of oxygen. In 2001, Rørmark 
et al. [26] measured the oxidation enthalpy of SrMnO3-δ to be 293 ± 10 
kJ/mol-O2 and 73.3 ± 0.5 kJ/mol-Mn (~384.65 kJ/kg). Furthermore, 
comparing with the DFT-based data [27–29], the reaction enthalpy of 
SrMnO3-δ is 340 kJ/mol-O2. From the literature, SrMnO3-δ does possess a 
larger reaction enthalpy, as expected from the design principles, but the 
high stability of SrMnO3-δ in the range of 500-1000◦C results in practical 
difficulties. In conclusion, only SrFeO3-δ was found to be able to operate 
under medium-high temperature conditions. 
3.4. Cyclability test 
SrFeO3-δ was chosen for further cyclability test in 10 redox cycles, as 
shown in Fig. 6. Aside from the first cycle not showing a satisfactory 
depth of reduction, the other cycles show an excellent cyclability in air. 
The large mass loss seen in the first cycle can be attributed to the 
desorption of water vapor or other adsorbed species. From this 
perspective, SrFeO3-δ could return to the relatively high oxidation state, 
at a mass very close to the authentic initial mass, when temperature was 
cooled down to 100◦C. 
As shown in Fig. 7 (a-b), some SrFeO3-δ agglomerated and generated 
relatively large particles whose size is over 20 μm. The agglomerated 
particles appear to have a terraced and exfoliated structure. In the 
magnified view, the terraced structure is distinct, with some small holes 
(about 1 μm) visible on the surface of particles. The point defects in

4. Conclusions 
In this research, Sr-based transition metal perovskites were prepared 
for thermochemical energy storage at medium-high temperature. Owing 
to the instability of SrCuO3-δ and SrNiO3-δ, only SrCoO3-δ, SrFeO3-δ and 
SrMnO3-δ were successfully formed via the modified Pechini method. 
The “frozen” samples were obtained using quenching method to inves­
tigate their crystal structure. The structure of SrMnO3-δ is deviate from 
the ideal perovskite cubic structure and shows diminished redox ca­
pacity at 1000◦C, even in the N2 atmosphere. However, several reports 
have shown that SrMnO3-δ may possess the highest reaction enthalpy 
(300~400 kJ/mol-O2) among three candidates due to the stronger Mn-O 
bonds and non-cubic structure. 
Although SrCoO3-δ has very similar redox capacity to that of SrFeO3- 
δ, according to the phase diagram of Sr-Co-O, its variant phase transition 
is unfavorable to the TCS system. SrCoO3-δ, SrFeO3-δ and SrMnO3-δ were 
heated to 1000◦C to obtain reduced phase. After DSC measurement, the 
oxidation enthalpies of each sample are 47.6 ± 5.7 kJ/kg-ABO3 (270.5 ±
32.3 kJ/L), 81.7 ± 3.4 kJ/kg-ABO3 (473.5 ± 20.0 kJ/L) and 25.6 ± 5.8 
kJ/kg-ABO3 (230.3 ± 39.4 kJ/L), respectively. Theoretically, if all 
samples reach the same Δδ, the oxidation enthalpy will increase in the 
increase of element metallicity, which may be related to the difference 
of M-O bonds strength (SrNiO3-δ and SrCuO3-δ could also be considered 
as their M-O bonds are too weak to form). 
Since SrFeO3-δ exhibited excellent redox capacity, further cyclability 
tests were performed and their morphology was investigated by SEM. 
The result showed a good cyclability for 10 cycles even after sintering. In 
conclusion, SrFeO3-δ meets the requirements for operation in moderate 
oxygen partial pressure and medium-high temperature range and pos­
sesses exceptional cyclability. However, the limited thermochemical 
energy storage capacity and reaction kinetics require further 
investigation.
