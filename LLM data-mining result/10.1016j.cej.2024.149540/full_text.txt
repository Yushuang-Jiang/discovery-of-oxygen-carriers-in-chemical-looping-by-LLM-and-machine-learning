A B S T R A C T   
In this study, we developed a CuMn2O4/CuMnO2-based porous foam thermochemical energy storage (TCES) 
module, which is free from any supporting materials. The raw material of CuMn2O4/CuMnO2 was synthesized 
using co-precipitation method which is different with the Pechini method we have used in the previous study, 
aiming to a large-scale synthesis. The porous foam modules were prepared using polyurethane (PU) foam replica 
method. We investigated the sintering temperature and holding time as critical parameters affecting the mod­
ule’s mechanical strength, chemical reactivity, and durability. The optimal condition for CuMn2O4/CuMnO2- 
based porous foam TCES module were identified as 1100 ℃ and 6 h. Our findings suggests that these modules are 
promising for the fixed-bed reactors in redox-type TCES systems.   
1. Introduction 
Thermal energy storage (TES) technology can be broadly classified 
into three types: sensible, latent, and thermochemical energy storage 
(TCES). Among them, TCES has emerged as a hotspot in TES research 
and application, attributed to its high energy storage density, wide 
operating temperature range, and theoretically unlimited transportation 
distance [1]. TCES technology can be sub-divided into open system and 
closed systems based on the natures of their reaction mechanisms. 
Compared with the closed TCES systems of metal carbonate, metal 
hydroxide, metal sulfate, and metal hydride, the open system based on 
redox reaction of metal oxide attracts much attention because the gas 
reactant (oxygen) can be obtained directly from the surrounding at­
mosphere without any additional gas storage tanks (Eq. (1)). In this case, 
the oxygen or air not only serves as the reactant in these redox reactions 
but also functions as the heat transfer fluid (HTF). Consequently, the 
thermal energy storage and release process can be efficiently regulated 
by manipulating the HTF’s flow rate, temperature, and oxygen partial 
pressure. However, owing to the complexity of chemical reactions and 
heat/mass transfer phenomena involved in TCES (including redox-type 
TCES) systems, much of the current research is still focused on devel­
oping new materials and conducing kinetic analysis. Practical 
applications, such as the design and demonstration of laboratory-scale 
reactor, are comparatively fewer.

To address the intermittent of renewable energy due to weather condi­
tions, the redox-type TCES technology is primarily utilized for managing 
the fluctuations in renewable electricity generation, especially in the 
field of concentrated solar power (CSP) plant. The energy conversion 
efficiency from thermal to power (T2P) necessitates that most redox- 
type TCES systems designed for CSP applications operate at a high 
temperature of 1000 ℃ approximately. Recently, some researchers 
successfully modulate the redox temperature of traditional redox couple 
(Co3O4/CoO, CuO/Cu2O, Mn2O3/Mn3O4, Mn-Fe oxides, etc.) through 
elemental doping. This advancement broadens the applications in me­
dium- to high-temperature thermal energy storage, such as waste heat 
recovery in the steel and non-ferrous metal industries, contributing to 
the carbon neutrality goals of these traditional sectors. For instance, 
doping Co3O4/CoO with elements like Cu [2], Ni [3–5], and Mg [6,7] 
has led to the formation of a (CuxCo1–x)O spinel phase or some special 
solid solution. Currently, the most promising material appears to be 
Co2.8Mg0.2O4, with an onset temperature of approximately 775.6 ℃ [7].

In our previous research of new materials development for medium- 
to high-temperature (500–1000 ℃) TCES, we explored Sr-based 
perovskite/brownmillerite redox couples [8] (e.g., SrCoO3-δ, SrFeO3-δ) 
which undergo non-stoichiometric reactions, and Cu-based spinel/ 
delafossite redox couples [9] (e.g., CuMn2O4/CuMnO2, CuFe2O4/ 
CuFeO2) that undergo primarily stoichiometric reactions. Notably, the 
Sr-based perovskite/brownmillerite couples did not exhibit a fixed re­
action equilibrium temperature in our investigation, which allowed for 
continuous thermal storage (reduction) over a wide temperature range 
(400–1000 ℃). Furthermore, the reduced material can re-oxidize and 
release heat from the chemical reaction again at approximately 400℃. 
However, the absence of significant phase transitions in these perov­
skite/brownmillerite redox couples limits their heat storage density for 
practical applications, as demonstrated by a maximum heat storage 
density of only about 81.7 ± 3.4 kJ/kg for SrFeO3-δ. 
Subsequently, our research shifted the focus to Cu-based spinel/ 
delafossite redox couples, where we found that the CuMn2O4/CuMnO2 
redox couple not only satisfies the requirement for short-term thermal 
energy storage, but also meets the requirements for seasonal or long- 
term thermal energy storage. This is because of the reduced state 
CuMnO2 can re-oxidize at 466 ℃, which implies that only a minimal 
amount of energy is needed to “trigger” the re-oxidation reaction to 
release the stored thermal energy. However, such a feature cannot be 
observed in other traditional stoichiometry-type redox couple. We 
further enhanced this system by doping Cr on the B-sites of CuMn2O4/ 
CuMnO2 redox couple and found that their re-oxidation can occur at a 
lower temperature of 421 ℃, and this temperature even closer to the re- 
oxidation temperature of Sr-based perovskite/brownmillerite redox 
couples [10,11]. Impressively, CuCr0.3Mn0.7O2 exhibited a heat storage 
density of 305.61 ± 16.45 kJ/kg, surpassing that of pure CuMnO2 by 
69.6 kJ/kg. Currently, we are working on modularizing our developed 
redox couple to align with practical engineering application re­
quirements. However, given the challenges in synthesizing Cu-Cr-Mn 
ternary oxides in large quantities with high purity at the laboratory 
level, we selected to use the CuMn2O4/CuMnO2 redox couple as the 
material for the next step of TCES module design. 
Over the last decade, various module designs for the redox-type TCES 
systems have been proposed by researchers (Fig. 1), including granules 
[12–16], honeycomb [17–21], and porous foam structures [22,23]. The 
granules are advantageous due to its cost-effectiveness and the ability to 
be produced in large quantities quickly using standard granulation 
equipment. However, the traditional packed bed reactor using granules 
face a significant pressure drop because of agglomeration after cycling, 
which can lead to a reduced flow rate of the HTF at the outlet of reactor, 
especially in a long-size reactor. Additionally, the granules are typically 
small spheres or cylinders with dense surfaces and low porosity. While 
this structure may benefit mechanical properties, it can hinder the 
diffusion rate of O2, a crucial reactant in redox-type TCES systems, 
thereby impacting on the overall performance of systems. 
The honeycomb-type TCES modules offer an effective solution to the 
pressure drop issue encountered in the packed bed reactors. It can adapt 
a very high flow rate of HTF, meeting the rapid requirements for heat 
storage and release. There are two sub-types of honeycomb TCES 
modules: material-coated and material-based honeycomb. The former 
involves coating redox-type TCES materials onto the surface of common 
cordierite or SiC honeycomb ceramic matrices [24]. However, this 
approach is limited by a lower heat storage density, as the thickness of 
the coating layer is always not sufficient. The latter approach involves 
directly synthesizing honeycomb structures from redox-type TCES ma­
terials, addressing the issue of low heat storage density. Nevertheless, 
this method requires specialized extrusion molding devices and molds, 
posing challenges for prototype preparation at the laboratory level.

In this study, we synthesized a large quantity of the CuMn2O4/ 
CuMnO2 redox couple using the co-precipitation method. With an eye on 
the current research trends in TCES modules, our goal is to develop a 
CuMn2O4/CuMnO2-based porous TCES module with low pressure drop 
and high reactivity. First, we characterized the samples prepared by the 
co-precipitation method using conventional materials science analysis 
techniques. PU foam replica method was used to prepare the CuMn2O4/ 
CuMnO2-based porous TCES module. We then focused on the effect of 
preparation conditions (e.g., sintering temperature and holding time) on 
the mechanical properties (compressive strength), reactivity (redox 
behavior) and durability of the porous modules and selected the optimal 
preparation conditions based on the CuMn2O4/CuMnO2 properties. 
2. Experimental approach 
2.1. Synthesis and characterization of powder material 
For efficient larger-scale synthesis, CuMn2O4 (oxidized state of 
CuMn2O4/CuMnO2 redox couple) was synthesized using a co- 
precipitation method (Fig. 2) instead of the modified Pechini method 
we usually used in the previous research. The raw materials were cor­
responding metal nitrates (Cu(NO3)2⋅3H2O (FUJIFILM Wako Pure 
Chemical Corp.), Mn(NO3)2⋅6H2O (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical 
Corp.) and precipitant (Na2CO3 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.)). 
The metal nitrates in the molar ratio of 1: 1 were dissolved in several 
beakers using the ion-exchange water (IEW, hereinafter referred to as 
“water”) with the concentration of 0.5 mol/L, and the metal nitrates 
solution was thoroughly mixed with a powerful stirrer at 700 rpm for a 
few minutes, resulting in a solution with a pH value of 2.0 approximately 
after stirring. 
For the co-precipitation reactions, a high concentration of Na2CO3 
solution (2 mol/L) was introduced as a pH regulator and precipitant. The 
Na2CO3 solution was carefully added dropwise into the metal nitrates 
solution while maintaining a constant stirring rate of 1000 rpm. By the 
addition of Na2CO3 precipitant, a bright green precipitate was formed, 
accompanied by the generation of numerous bubbles. The addition of 
Na2CO3 should be stopped when the pH of the solution changed from 
acidic to weakly basic, approximately pH of 7.5. The precipitated so­
lution was set aside for aging at room temperature for 12 h. After the 
aging treatment, the solution clearly separated into two distinct layers: 
an upper clarified liquid layer and a lower precipitate layer. The pre­
cipitate was filtered out using vacuum filtration, and the resulting filter 
cake was washed three times using water. The filter cake was moved into 
a vacuum drying oven and dried at 100 ℃ for 12 h. The dried filter cake 
was crushed and placed in a crucible and heated to 1000 ℃ using a 
muffle furnace under air atmosphere for 6 h to obtain the crude sample. 
The crude sample was crushed with a ball mill set (desktop pot mill 
rotating stand (PM-001, AS ONE Corp.); zirconia balls (φ 20 mm, NIK­
KATO Corp.); mill pot (BP-2, AS ONE Corp.)). Both the crude sample and 
the zirconia balls filled one-third of the pot’s internal space. During the 
ball milling process, the rotation speed of the mill pot was maintained at 
220 rpm for a duration of 12 h. The resulting powder was sieved through 
a metal mesh screen (with a pore size of ≤75 µm). This process yielded a 
final powder sample weighing approximately 7.5 kg. 
The powder sample was sampled randomly from different batches 
for physiochemical characterization. The particle size analysis was 
conducted using a scattering particle size distribution analyzer (LA-920, 
HORIBA Ltd.) with an ultrasonic dispersion with 1 min. The phase 
composition of the oxidized state and reduced state powder sample 
synthesized by co-precipitation method was investigated using X-ray 
diffraction analysis (XRD) (SmartLab, Rigaku Corp.) with a scanning 
speed of 5◦/min in 2θ range of 15–90◦. The phase composition of each 
sample was analyzed using PDXL2 (Rigaku Data Analysis Software). For 
morphology observations, the oxidized state sample was selected as the 
representative, and the elemental ratios of Cu and Mn in the system was 
investigated using scanning electron microscope equipped with energy 
dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) (JSM-7500F, JEOL Ltd.). The redox 
behavior of the powder sample was investigated using TGA (STA7300, 
HITACHI High-Tech Science Corp.).The heat storage density was 
measured using thermogravimetry and differential scanning calorimetry 
(TG-DSC) (STA 449F3, NETZSCH; TGA/DSC 3+, METTLER TOLEDO).

2.2. Preparation of porous TCES modules 
A series of porous TCES modules made entirely of CuMn2O4 material 
was prepared using the PU foam replica method or so-called sacrificial 
sponge foam template method. Commercially available open-porous 20 
× 20 × 20 mm3 cubic polyurethane (PU) foam (Yuanshengxing elec­
tronic material Co.) with a cell size of 13 ± 2 PPI was used as the tem­
plate. A PU foam with a larger 13 PPI pore size was selected as the 
template for better understand the wall formation process in the PU 
foam replica method and observe the macro-morphology changes after 
durability tests. 
The CuMn2O4 slurry was prepared by referring to the literature on 
porous Al2O3 [25,26,27] and perovskite-type CaMnO3 heat storage 
structures [22]. The slurry was prepared with 80 wt% CuMn2O4 powder, 
17 wt% water, and 3 wt% Dolapix CE 64® (Zschimmer & Schwarz 
Chemie GmbH, Lahnstein, Germany). The Dolapix CE 64® was 
employed as a dispersant and thickener. The solid component of the 
slurry constituted approximately 67.5 vol%, with the liquid component 
accounting for 32.5 vol%. The slurry exhibited a high viscosity (similar 
to that of mayonnaise), surpassing the maximum test range of 198 Pa⋅s 
of our laboratory’s equipment during the viscosity measurement. 
The PU foam was immersed in the slurry and squeezed three times to 
ensure complete absorption of the slurry. Subsequently, the coated 
foams were placed on Kimwipes® (Nippon Paper Crecia Co., Ltd.) and 
any excess slurry was promptly squeezed out. This step primarily aims to 
prevent the pores on the PU foam’s surface from getting clogged with 
excess slurry, which could potentially reduce the performance of porous 
TCES module. 
After coating, the PU foams were dried at room temperature under 
air atmosphere for 24 h and then transferred to a muffle furnace (ROP- 
003H, AS ONE Corp.) for sintering process. 
The sintering temperatures should be lie between the service tem­
perature of the TCES module and the melting point of TCES materials. In 
our previous work [9], we referenced the findings of Driessens et al. [28] 
that the melting point of Cu-Mn-O system is approximately 1150 ℃ 
when x(Cu) = 0.5. Considering that the material sample was prepared 
using the co-precipitation method, potential impurities might decrease 
its melting point. Therefore, we selected 1100 ℃, slightly lower than the 
melting point, as the maximum sintering temperature, and set 1000 ℃ 
as the minimum. The sintering holing time also affects the module’s 
mechanical and heat storage performance. A short holding time could 
lead to inadequate sintering, resulting in weak mechanical properties, 
while an extended holding time might cause the module’s surface to 
become overly dense, influencing the oxygen diffusion and the heat 
storage performance. Typically, the sintering holding time of porous 
materials that prepared by the PU foam replica method is often reported 
to be between 1 and 3 h [22,25,26,27]. 
Hence, three different maximum sintering temperatures (1000 ℃, 
1050 ℃, and 1100 ℃) and three holding times (1.5 h, 3 h, and 6 h) were 
selected, and the sintering process was carried out at a heating rate of 1 
℃/min and a cooling rate of 2 ℃/min. Eventually, we obtained nine 
kinds of module samples sintered under different conditions. The indi­
vidual samples were named using a combination of sintering tempera­
ture and holding time. For example, the module sample sintered at 1000 
℃ for 1.5 h was labeled as “1000–1.5 h”. 
2.3. Measurement of physical parameters and compressive strength of 
module samples 
An electronic balance (GR-202, A&D Co., Ltd.) was used to measure 
the weight of each module sample. Apparent volume (AV, cm3) and 
apparent density (AD, g/cm3) were calculated after measuring their 
lengths, widths, and heights. However, there is a measurement error 
since the sponge templates used in this study were manually cut. For 
convenience, the longest side was defined as length (L), followed by the 
width (W), and lastly the height (H). 
For true density (TD, g/cm3) measurement, we used a high-precision 
gas and vapor adsorption analyzer (BELSORP MAX II, MicrotracBEL 
Corp.) with gas (helium) displacement method. The volume of the 
chamber (Vchamber) inside the instrument and the volume of the sample 
cell (Vsample cell) are known parameters before the measurement. After 
placing the sample in the sample cell, the sample cell and the chamber 
were vacuumed. The valve between the sample cell and the chamber 
was then closed. Subsequently, the chamber was filled with a specific 
amount of helium gas, and the pressure was recorded as P1. Upon 
opening the valve, the pressure was recorded as P2 after the helium gas 
was allowed to enter the sample cell. The true volume of the sample can 
be calculated using Eq. (2). Once we know the TD, the ratio of the AD to 
this true density can be used to estimate the voidage (%) for each 
module sample.

Apparent volumetric heat storage density (AVHSD, kJ/L) was 
calculated by multiplying the heat storage density (kJ/kg) with the AD 
(g/cm3). In this study, we used a non-isothermal method to measure the 
heat storage density, which differs from the isothermal method used in 
previous studies [9,10]. This difference in measurement approach could 
influence the values of the AVHSD. Therefore, the range of AVHSD was 
established using the heat storage densities obtained through two 
methods. 
A tensile and compression testing machine (SDW-2000, IMADA Co., 
Ltd.) was used to test the mechanical compression strength of the nine 
as-prepared module samples prepared under various sintering temper­
atures and holding time conditions. 
It is widely acknowledged that these TCES modules must exhibit a 
certain level of structural strength to meet the requirements of trans­
portation and high flow rate of heat transfer fluid without experiencing 
significant deformation that may affect their heat storage performance. 
However, the discussion regarding the mechanical strengths of TCES 
modules (such as honeycombs or granules) have yet to be quantified, 
possibly because they seem to exhibit a satisfactory strength visually. So 
far, only Bielsa et al. have made a specific discussion about this aspect, 
reporting a crushing strength ranging from 0.126 to 0.166 N for 
Mn0.98Si0.02O3 granules [16]. However, they did not provide a specific 
compressive strength may because of the absence of a precise definition 
for the granule’s pressure force area and the specialized testing equip­
ment. For the porous foam module, Pein et al. tested compressive 
strength of CaMnO3 porous ceramic foam (φ20 × 10 mm), reporting the 
maximum values of 132.4 kPa [22]. For comparison, the compressive 
strength of the Al2O3 porous ceramic foam developed for non-TCES 
purposes ranged between 1.15 and 1.30 MPa [25,26,27]. 
In our experiment, a maximum range of 500 N was chosen, and the 
cross-sectional area of each module sample was measured. The module 
sample was placed horizontally on the testing bench, and the pressure 
sensor was manually adjusted to ensure a snug fit against the sample 
before testing. The testing process involved the pressure sensor gradu­
ally approaching and pressing the module sample at a rate of 0.08 mm/s. 
The maximum pressure peak observed within 150 s was recorded as the 
compressive strength of each module sample. 
2.4. Evaluation of heat storage performance of module samples 
2.4.1. Redox behavior 
Due to the volume constraints within the sample cell of the TGA 
experimental equipment, it was not feasible to directly observe and 
measure the comprehensive redox behavior of the TCES module via 
TGA. As a result, we indirectly investigated the overall redox behavior 
by using fragments of the module sample, which were obtained by 
gently crushing the module sample while retaining its porous structure.

2.4.2. Evaluation of durability of module samples 
A small muffle furnace (ROP-003H, AS ONE Corp.) was used to 
conduct a durability test directly on TCES modules prepared under nine 
different conditions, subjecting them to 50 cycles. The modules samples 
were positioned horizontally on an alumina sintering plate (AS ONE 
Corp.). The sintering plate was then placed at the center of the muffle 
furnace and heated from room temperature to 1000 ℃ at a rate of 10 
℃/min and held for 60 min. Subsequently, the module samples were 
gradually cooled down to 600 ℃ at a rate of 10 ℃/min, completing a 
heating–cooling cycling test (the rate of 10 ℃/min was selected due to 
the limitations of the muffle furnace’s performance). After every five 
cycles, the module samples were removed from the muffle furnace, and 
their shape and apparent morphology changes were recorded by pho­
tographs. Following 50 cycles, the redox behavior of the module samples 
was re-evaluated using TGA, utilizing the same method as described in 
Section 2.4.1 to obtain fragments. Additionally, SEM was employed to 
observe the morphological alterations on the surface of each module 
sample’s fragments before and after 50 cycles. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characterization of powder material synthesized using co- 
precipitation method

(hereinafter referred to as the Co-pre sample) was tested for the particle 
size distribution using the scattering particle size distribution analyzer 
with 1-minute ultrasonic treatment. Fig. S1 presents a representative 
particle size distribution and the powder sample exhibited particle size 
sizes smaller than 30 µm the crushing and sieving process. The average 
d50 (median diameter) is 3.06 ± 0.13 µm, showing a smaller size than 
the pore size of the screen mesh (75 µm).

powder sample are shown in Fig. 3a and b. For a convenient compari­

characteristic peaks matching those of CuMn2O4 (#01-074-1919, 2θ =

SEM was used to investigate the microscopic morphology of the Co- 
pre powder sample. The representative SEM image of the Co-pre powder 
sample (Fig. 3c) reveals most of the particles lie between 2 and 3 µm, 
consistent with the results mentioned in the particle size distribution 
experiment described earlier. Furthermore, we conducted a metal 
elemental ratio analysis using EDS. As discussed in the previous XRD 
analysis, it was evident that the Co-pre powder sample contains a small 
amount of Na-Mn oxide impurities. Therefore, the elemental ratio of Na 
is also considered. As shown in Fig. 3d, the ratio of Cu in both the Co-pre 
and Pechini powder samples consistently shows slightly lower than that 
of Mn. This discrepancy may be attributed to the sensitivity of EDS to 
specific elements’ wavelengths. Additionally, during the synthesis of the 
Co-pre powder sample, a highly concentrated Na2CO3 precipitant with 
strong alkalinity was used. This may have led to the formation of some 
soluble complexes from the former precipitated Cu(OH)2 under local­
ized strong alkaline conditions because Cu(OH)2 is also dissoluble in the 
strong alkali [29] such as NaOH or NH3⋅H2O, resulting in a worse pre­
cipitation of Cu2+. This could also account for why the elemental ratio of 
Cu in the Co-pre powder sample remains lower than that in the Pechini 
sample even after neglecting the Na content in the Co-pre powder 
sample (Fig. 3d). 
Furthermore, the two types of powder samples were tested using 
TGA and TG-DSC. Fig. 4a presents the redox behavior of the Co-pre and 
Pechini powder samples. Both the Co-pre and Pechini powder samples 
exhibits two reduction steps. The first reduction is a non-stoichiometric 
reaction, as discussed in our previous study [10]. The second reduction 
involves a phase transition reaction, significantly contributing to the 
heat storage density, specifically the phase transition of Cu-Mn oxides 
from spinel to delafossite structure. It is noteworthy that the Co-pre 
powder sample initiated its first reduction at a much lower tempera­
ture compared to the Pechini powder sample (100 ℃ vs. 500 ℃), and the 
former exhibits a larger weight loss during the first reduction (2.53 % vs. 
1.20 %). Additionally, the Co-pre powder sample only returned to 98.14 
% of its initial weight during oxidation, while the Pechini sample could 
reach 99.52 %. This phenomenon suggests that the first reduction of the 
Co-pre powder sample included a partial decomposition reaction of Na- 
Mn oxides, possibly explaining the presence of Na-Mn oxides in different 
forms in the XRD pattern of Co-pre powder sample’s reduced state. 
Interestingly, based on the TGA results, it shows challenging for Na-Mn 
to re-oxidize within a short period once they decomposed completely. 
However, the decomposition reaction equation of Na-Mn oxides remains 
unclear at present. When we exclude the effect by the decomposition of 
Na-Mn oxides, the Co-pre and Pechini powder samples demonstrate 
nearly identical redox behavior, as evidenced the maximum weight 
changes by using the weight gain of oxidation during cooling process 
(Co-pre: 4.01 ± 0.06 %; Pechini: 4.26 ± 0.05 %). 
A typical DSC curve is shown in Fig. 4b. The Co-pre powder sample 
exhibits a reduction enthalpy of 144.78 ± 36.81 kJ/kg and oxidation 
enthalpy of 128.81 ± 10.01 kJ/kg, which are close to the reduction 
enthalpy of 144.78 ± 12.50 kJ/kg and oxidation enthalpy of 141.04 ±
33.96 kJ/kg demonstrated by the Pechini powder sample. Considering 
the heat loss during the measurement of oxidation enthalpy, the 
enthalpy of reduction was used as the representative value for the heat 
storage density of the power sample ultimately. These findings are very 
close to the values of 144 kJ/kg reported by Hlongwa et al. [30] in their 
investigation of CuMn2O4 heat storage materials and the 145 kJ/kg 
reported by Andr´e et al. [31]. However, it remains lower than the heat 
storage density of 236.11 ± 5.40 kJ/kg measured through the 
isothermal DSC method in our prior study [9–11]. The reason for this 
divergence lies in the fact that we utilized a completely reduced state 
(synthesized at 1000 ℃ under an Ar atmosphere) during the isothermal 
DSC measurement, which potentially results in a larger reduction depth 
(maximum weight change of approximately 6 %) compared to samples 
reduced at 1000 ◦C under atmospheric air conditions. 
In summary, aside from the presence of some Na-Mn oxide impu­
rities, there are no significant differences in phase composition, redox 
behavior, and heat storage density between the Co-pre and Pechini 
powder samples. Consequently, we chose to proceed with the Co-pre 
powder sample for the further preparation of the TCES module.

3.3. Redox behavior of module samples 
TGA was used to investigate the redox behavior of each module 
sample under an air atmosphere within the temperature range of 100 to 
1000 ℃. In Fig. 7a, the module samples sintered at 1000 ℃ with 
different holding times exhibits small variations in their redox behavior. 
Notably, the TGA tests conducted on these module samples did not 
reveal an obvious decomposition of the Na-Mn oxides. The impact of 
holding time on the redox behavior of the module samples is evident in 
the samples sintered at 1050 ℃, as shown in Fig. 7b. For example, the 
1050–6 h sample fails to achieve a satisfactory reduction depth during 
TGA tests when held at 1000 ℃ for 30 min. This phenomenon is even 
more observable in the 1100 ℃ sample. Additionally, it is noteworthy 
that the 1100 ℃ sample exhibits a special weight gain during the heating 
process in the TGA test, as shown in Fig. 7c. This weight gain may be 
attributed to the fact that the cooling process of the 1100 ℃-sintered 
module sample is unable to ensure complete transformation from the 
high-temperature-reduced state to the room-temperature-oxidized state, 
even with a slow cooling rate of 2 ◦C/min. Consequently, the 1100 ℃ 
sample retains a portion of the reduced state, and when the module 
sample is reheated, oxidation reactions occurred preferentially before 
reaching the phase equilibrium temperature, resulting in the observed 
weight gain. 
To eliminate the influence of impurities on the redox behavior 
analysis of each sample, the weight gain during the cooling process was 
used as maximum weight change for relative comparison. As shown in 
Fig. 7d, the 1000 ◦C sample exhibits a maximum weight change very 
close to that of the Co-pre and Pechini powder samples. However, as the 
sintering temperature is increased and the sintering holding time is 
extended, the maximum weight change decreases, indicating a degra­
dation in the redox and thermal storage properties of the module 
samples.

process, oxygen releases from the samples and expands small pre-

decreasing of grain boundaries resulted in a shrinkage in the volume 
fraction of solids, preserving the pore structures. Subsequently, we 
performed a quantitative analysis of the changes in pore size before and 
after cycling for each module samples. This was achieved by using SEM 
images (Figs. S4, S5, and S6) and analyzing them with ImageJ software.

the module samples sintered at 1000 ℃ increase from approximately 3 
µm to 11 µm. Interestingly, the samples sintered at 1050 ℃ and 1100 ℃

13 µm after 50 cycles. However, it appears that there is no distinct 
correlation between the size of the pore structure formed and the sin­
tering conditions. It is also worth noting that the presence of pores can 
also damage the macroscopic mechanical strength of the module sam­
ples while the formation of pores enhances reactivity.

As highlighted in the introduction, the porous foam modules possess 
a crucial feature: the adaptability of large HTF flow rates. This feature is 
essential for rapid heat storage and release, a characteristic that also 
shares with the honeycomb module. However, the porous foam modules 
do not involve in complicated and high-cost preparation. Unlike the 
material-coated modules based on Al2O3 or ZrO2 porous foam supports, 
this study utilizes the PU form replica method to prepare porous foam 
modules directly from CuMn2O4/CuMnO2, which was named as 
material-based modules. Theoretically, this method is expected to 
enhance the heat storage density compared to the former one when the 
same heat storage material is used. 
Table 2 shows that the similarity between the CuMn2O4/CuMnO2- 
based modules developed in this study and the literature-reported 
CaMnO3-based porous modules on reactivity, heat storage densities, 
and durability. However, the mechanical properties of the former are 
slightly lower, mainly due to differences in voidage and -porosity since 
different PU foam template and various sintering temperatures were 
used. Although the PU foams of 30 PPI were used as templates in most of 
the prior studies, we chose a cubic PU foam with a larger 13 PPI pore size 
to better understand the wall formation process in the PU foam replica 
method and observe the macro-morphology changes during durability 
tests. Additionally, the perovskite-type CaMnO3 material requires 
different sintering temperatures as it does not undergo significant phase 
transitions. This phenomenon causes its oxygen uptake and release be­
haviors are different from those of CuMn2O4, which typically experi­
ences larger weight change. 
In general, the performance of material-based porous foam modules 
prepared from the same material is related to two key factors: voidage 
and porosity. Higher voidage results in lower pressure drops and faster 
heat storage and release rates but reduces heat storage density. 
Conversely, smaller voidage increases heat storage density but may 
cause larger pressure drops. Porosity affects reactivity and mechanical 
strength; larger porosity enhances reactivity but diminishes mechanical 
strength, and vice versa. 
While the material-coated porous foam modules have superior me­
chanical strength (primarily dependent on the support’s strength), the 
material-based modules possess higher heat storage densities, which 
offer a promising modularization concept for fixed-bed thermochemical 
reactors without frequent equipment transportation needs. However, 
the PU foam replica method might face issues like deformation or 
collapse with larger size modules, suggesting the use of multiple smaller- 
sized modules for stacking will be a wise choice. 
4. Conclusions 
In this study, we developed CuMn2O4/CuMnO2-based porous foam 
TCES modules that are adaptable to various HTF flow rates and exhibit 
high reaction activity. Initially, we used the co-precipitation method to 
produce CuMn2O4/CuMnO2 in large quantities. We then performed 
XRD, TG, and DSC analysis to compare the materials synthesized by both 
the co-precipitation method and the Pechini method we used in previous 
study. The findings revealed that there are no significant differences 
between the material synthesized by the two different methods, expect 
for the presence of a small amount of Na-Mn oxide impurities in the co- 
precipitation sample. Subsequently, we employed the co-precipitation 
material to prepare CuMn2O4/CuMnO2-based porous foam modules 
using the PU foam replica method with a size of 20 × 20 × 20 mm3 and 
pore sizes of 13 PPI. The focus of this study is to investigate how various 
preparation conditions, specifically sintering temperature and holding 
time, influence the mechanical properties, reactivity, and durability of 
these module samples. The results indicate that the modules’ compres­
sive strength improves with higher sintering temperature and extended 
holding time. This improvement is likely due to the densification of 
particles within the module walls under these conditions. However, we 
also noticed a deterioration in redox behavior during TG heating and 
cooling tests as the sintering temperature and holding time increased. A 
durability test of 50 cycles was then conducted on monolithic module 
samples using a small muffle furnace. After 50 cycles, the modules 
maintained their integrity but exhibited variations in volume expansion 
and color changes. Further investigation on the redox behavior of the 
after-cycling samples through TG revealed a surprising recovery in 
reactivity, particularly in samples sintered at 1050 ◦C and 1100 ◦C. SEM 
observations of the module samples’ fragments, both before and after 
cycling, showed that a dense surface formed pores of about several-µm 
after 50 cycles, which likely contributed to the reactivity’s recovery. 
Additionally, the formation of nanowire CuO on the surface of some 
samples was observed and we believe this formation is unfavorable to 
the reaction. Although the exact mechanism remains unclear, interest­
ingly, this phenomenon diminished with an increase in the number of 
cycles. 
After evaluating the material characteristics, mechanical properties, 
reactivity, and durability, we determined that the optimal preparation 
conditions for CuMn2O4/CuMnO2-based porous foam TCES modules are 
a sintering temperature of 1100 ◦C and a holding time of 6 h. Addi­
tionally, our comparison with current research trends in various porous 
foam TCES modules leads us to conclude that the material-based porous 
foam modules are a promising option for designing fixed-bed reactors in 
redox-type TCES systems. However, it is important to acknowledge the 
current limitations, particularly the weak mechanical strength of these 
modules and the challenges in fabricating larger sizes, which should be 
further investigated in future studies.
