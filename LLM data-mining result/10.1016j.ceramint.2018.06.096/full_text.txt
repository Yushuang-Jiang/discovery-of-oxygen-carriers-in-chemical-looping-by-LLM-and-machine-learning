A B S T R A C T
The inﬂuence of lanthanide metal cations doped into the CeO2 crystal structure (to form Ce0.9Ln0.1O2; Ln = La,
Pr, Nd, Sm, Gd, Tb, Dy, or Er) on thermochemical reduction and the CO2 splitting ability of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 is
scrutinized using thermogravimetric analysis. Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials are eﬀectively synthesized by co-
precipitation of hydroxides. As-synthesized Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials are further characterized based on their
phase composition, crystallite size, surface area, and morphology using powder X-ray diﬀraction, Brunauer-
Emmett-Teller surface area analysis, and scanning electron microscopy. The thermal reduction and CO2 splitting
aptitude of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials are examined by performing 10 consecutive thermochemical cycles. The
results imply that insertion of Sm3+, Er3+, Tb3+, Dy3+, and La+3 in place of Ce4+ in the ﬂuorite crystal
structure of CeO2 (forming Ce0.9Ln0.1O2) enhances the O2 liberation by 22.5, 14.6, 12.6, 5.85, and 2.96 μmol O2/
g·cycle, respectively. Besides, CeLa is observed to be more active towards the CO2 splitting reaction than CeO2
and the other Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials investigated in this study.
1. Introduction
Carbon dioxide production is inescapable because of the un-
warranted use of fossil fuels in various industries. The incessant lib-
eration of CO2 from industries adds to global warming from the
greenhouse eﬀect [1]. The feasible solution to circumvent this cata-
strophe is to convert CO2 into value-added products. Metal oxide (MO)-
based solar-driven thermochemical splitting of CO2 (in combination
with H2O splitting) oﬀers a favourable route for the utilization of CO2
and storage of concentrated solar thermal energy as chemical fuels [2].
Solar thermochemical splitting of H2O/CO2 produces a gas mixture
containing H2 and CO (known as syngas), which can be used to produce
transportation fuels via the catalytic Fischer-Tropsch process [3].
The MO-based solar thermochemical cycle is more promising than
direct thermolysis of H2O/CO2 because of the prerequisite lower op-
erating temperatures [4]. The MO-based solar thermochemical cycle
encompasses an endothermic solar thermal reduction of the MO, which
causes the release of O2 (step 1) and an exothermic re-oxidation of
reduced MO by splitting H2O/CO2 (step 2) [5]. The solar energy is
stored in the MOs during the endothermic thermal reduction step, and
it is released as a fuel, such as H2/CO, via the exothermic re-oxidation
phase.
Numerous volatile and non-volatile MOs (predominantly zinc oxide
[6–9], tin oxide [10–12], iron oxide [13–15], ferrites [16–22], ceria
[23–28], perovskites [29,30], and others [31,32] have been expended
for thermochemical splitting of H2O/CO2. Among these MOs, ceria-
based oxides have received a lot of attention in recent years because of
their outstanding thermal stabilities and fast oxidation kinetics. CeO2
(in the form of a powder, reticulated porous ceramic, felt, and coating
on ceramic honeycomb structures) has been tested for H2O/CO2 split-
ting cycles using various reactor arrangements [14,23–25,33–37]. The
long-term reactivity and thermal stability of CeO2 was veriﬁed during
102, 500, and 2000 thermochemical cycles in a solar reactor driven by
concentrated solar power [23,38].
Attempts to improve the oxygen mobility have focused on in-
corporating suitable dopants into the ﬂuorite crystal structure of CeO2.
Doped ceria materials are appealing for thermochemical H2O/CO2
splitting because of their enhanced redox reactivity at low thermal re-
duction and re-oxidation temperatures. In addition to transition and
alkali earth metal cations [39–53], lanthanides can be employed as
dopants to upgrade the redox reactivity of CeO2 [26,39,54–57]. For
example, lanthanide-doped ceria exhibited an excellent automotive
catalytic activity because of the amended oxygen mobility [56,58].
Likewise, La and Gd doping into a ceria-zirconia solid solution boosted
the amount of released O2 (via thermal reduction) and generated H2
(via H2O splitting) [26,50]. In particular by addition of Pr as a second
dopant into ceria has enhanced H2 production more than 25% during
H2O splitting [54]. Likewise, La and Y dopant ceria are capable of

2.2. Synthesis of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2
The detailed steps involved in the synthesis of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 via
hydroxide co-precipitation using aqueous ammonium hydroxide (28%)
as the precipitating agent are as follows:
1. The metal precursors were dissolved in deionized water (with un-
interrupted stirring).
2. The aqueous ammonium hydroxide (28%) solution was added to the
aqueous solution of metal precursors (dropwise addition) to achieve
pH ≈10.
3. The aqueous dispersion was agitated for 48 h (while maintaining
pH ≈10) and then allowed to stand overnight (undisturbed).
4. The precipitate was washed several times using deionized water
until the pH was reduced to 7.
5. The remaining water was removed via vacuum ﬁltration until a
thick, dry precipitate was obtained on the ﬁlter paper.
6. The precipitate was dried at 100 °C and then annealed at 800 °C for
2 h using an air-circulated muﬄe furnace.
7. The calcined powder of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 was stored in a dry location
before a) powder X-ray diﬀraction (PXRD), Brunauer-Emmett-Teller
(BET) surface area analysis, and scanning electron microscopy
(SEM), and b) thermogravimetric CO2 splitting experiments.
2.3. Characterization of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2
A PANalytical XPert MPD/DY636 powder X-ray diﬀractometer with
Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm, voltage = 45 kV, current = 20 mA,
angular range = 20–90° 2θ, step = 0.05° 2θ, recording time = 5 s) was
used to determine the phase composition and crystallite size of the
Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials (before and after the thermochemical cy-
cles). The speciﬁc surface area (SSA) of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 was estimated
using an ASAP 2420 BET surface area analyzer (Micromeritics).
Additionally, SEM was performed (Nova Nano 450, FEI) with a max-
imum magniﬁcation of 200 kx to analyse the microstructure of the
Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 powder.

2.5. Thermochemical CO2 splitting using Ce0.9Ln0.1O2
To execute the thermal reduction and CO2 splitting experiments,
approximately 100 mg of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 powder was loaded in an alu-
mina crucible (capacity: 100 µL). The powder in the alumina crucible
was then sited very accurately on a hanging balance. Several thermo-
chemical cycles were carried out from 1000 to 1400°C. The thermal
reduction step was performed at 1400 °C for 60 min with inert Ar, and
the CO2 splitting step was executed at 1000 °C for 30 min by exposing

from Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 via the thermal reduction step. In contrast, at
1000 °C, the overall mass of the sample increased because of the re-
oxidation of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 via the CO2 splitting reaction. By utilizing the

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2
Fig. 2a and b exhibit the PXRD peaks of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2. The PXRD
pattern of each Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox material is shifted with respect to the
CeO2 PXRD peaks (Fig. 2b), indicating the formation of ceria-lantha-
nide solid solutions (with cubic ﬂuorite crystal structures). PXRD peaks
associated with the individual dopant oxides are absent, which further
conﬁrms the high degree of substitution of Ln in the CeO2 crystal
structure to produce a phase-pure CexLn1-xO2 redox material.
SEM was used to examine the microstructure of the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2
redox materials synthesized via hydroxide co-precipitation; the ac-
quired images are reported in Fig. 3. The SEM images for all
Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 materials display large agglomerates with primary particle
diameters from 10 to 100 nm. The particles are spherical with no evi-
dence of well-deﬁned lattice planes. The sizes and structures of the
particles are analogous; therefore, the SSAs for all the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2
materials are comparable (38–42 m2/g).
3.2. Thermochemical CO2 splitting
To determine the redox reactivity and thermal stability of the
Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials, multiple thermochemical CO2 splitting
cycles were carried out using the TGA set-up (Fig. 1). The experimental
conditions employed for the TGA experiments are listed in Table 2. A
buoyancy eﬀect is inevitable because of the continuous interchanging
of gases and operating temperatures during multiple consecutive
thermal reductions and CO2 splitting steps. The buoyancy eﬀect needs
to be nulliﬁed to avoid discrepancies in the TGA results. To achieve this
task, baseline blank experiments were conducted under identical ex-
perimental conditions using an empty alumina crucible before per-
forming the TGA experiments using the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials.
The buoyancy eﬀect was eliminated by subtracting the TGA proﬁles
obtained during the blank experiments from those obtained during the
thermochemical CO2 splitting experiments.
Figs. 4 and 5 illustrate typical TGA proﬁles obtained during the ﬁrst
thermochemical cycle performed using the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox mate-
rials. The overall mass loss during the thermal reduction step was cal-
culated from the change in the weight of the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox ma-
terial starting from 500 °C and the material left behind after the thermal
reduction step (i.e. Ce0.9Ln0.1O2-δ) for 60 min at 1400 °C. Likewise, the
overall mass gain was computed by exploiting the weight diﬀerence
between the originally reduced Ce0.9Ln0.1O2-δ and the re-oxidized
Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 material obtained after CO2 splitting for 30 min at 1000 °C.
The overall mass of the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox material decreases during
the thermal reduction step because of the release of O2, which forms O2
vacancies in the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 crystal structure. In contrast, the mass of
the reduced Ce0.9Ln0.1O2-δ increases during the CO2 splitting step be-
cause O2 is regained by the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 crystal structure from CO2 (i.e.
re-oxidation), producing CO. The overall mass loss and gain can be
directly converted into the amounts of O2 released and CO produced by
the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox material according to Eqs. (3) and (4). The

tion capacity. Overall, CeSm provides the best thermal reduction ability,
whereas the CeTb and CeLa display the best CO producing capacities
during the ﬁrst thermochemical CO2 splitting cycle.
We have performed three uninterrupted thermochemical cycles
using Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials by retaining the experimental con-
ditions. The TGA proﬁles attained and the amounts of O2 released and
CO produced in three successive thermochemical cycles are presented
in Fig. 6 and Table 4. The amount of O2 released by all the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2
redox materials (except for CeGd and CeEr) decreases during the second
thermal reduction step (as compared to the 1st thermal reduction step).
The Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials utilized in the 1st thermal reduction
step were freshly prepared and the mass loss was calculated from 500 to
1400°C. In contrast, the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials used during the
2nd thermal reduction step are already went through one thermal re-
duction step at 1400 °C (for 60 min) and the mass loss during the 2nd
thermal reduction step was determined from 1000 to 1400°C. Hence,
the loss in the amount of O2 released by the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox mate-
rials in the 2nd thermal reduction step is expected.
The CO2 splitting capacity of all the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials is
also suppressed during the second CO2 splitting step. For example, the
CO production decreases by 22.6, 13.9, 11.2, 8.4, 14.9, 12.1, 44.3, 20.3,
and 26.2 μmol CO/g from the ﬁrst to the second CO2 splitting step for
Ce, CeLa, CePr, CeNd, CeSm, CeGd, CeTb, CeDy, and CeEr, respectively.
The drop in the CO production capacity of the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox ma-
terials during the second CO2 splitting step is likely caused by the re-
duction of the O2 liberated during the second thermal reduction step.
The O2 releasing abilities of CeLa, CeNd, and CeEr during the third
thermal reduction step are approximately the same as the respective
values during the second thermal reduction step (Table 3). For CeSm and
CeGd, the amounts of O2 discharged during the third thermal reduction
step are 6.2% and 16.1% lower, respectively, than that for the second
thermal reduction step. In contrast, the O2 liberating capacities of CePr,
CeTb, and CeDy are 1.13, 1.29, and 1.097 times larger, respectively,
during the third thermal reduction step than during the second thermal
reduction step. For the third CO2 splitting step, all Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox

second CO2 splitting step. The Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials investigated

The prime objective of this investigation is to identify a suitable
Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox material for thermochemical splitting of CO2. The
criteria for the selection of best Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox material is its higher
O2 releasing and CO producing capacity as compared to other materials
explored in this study. Among all the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials,
CeSm is the best material in terms of its thermal reduction capacity; and
CeLa is the best choice to produce CO by CO2 splitting (based on the
data obtained in the 3 consecutive thermochemical cycles).

3.3. Post-reaction characterization of Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials
The Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials used for 10 thermochemical cycles

4. Summary and conclusions
Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials (Ln = La, Pr, Nd, Sm, Gd, Tb, Dy, or
Er) were successfully synthesized by hydroxide co-precipitation. The
PXRD analysis of the derived materials indicate a phase-pure compo-
sition with the absence of individual peaks from the dopant oxides. The
BET study shows analogous SSAs for all the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox mate-
rials (38–42 m2/g). The SEM analysis conﬁrms the formation of sphe-
rical Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 nanoparticles (10–100 nm). The SEM evaluation
further exhibits that the size and structure of the nanoparticles is alike
for all synthesized Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials (because of this, the
SSA for all these materials is equivalent).
Multiple thermochemical CO2 splitting cycles were conducted using
TGA and specify that the Ce0.9Ln0.1O2 redox materials can be arranged
